# Scientific Proposal: Monitoring Gaps Predict ICU Deterioration

## Unresolved Question

**Clinical Problem:** In critically ill patients, clinicians rely on serial laboratory monitoring to detect early deterioration. However, the relationship between monitoring intensity and patient outcomes is poorly understood. Do patients who experience an abrupt reduction in testing while remaining hemodynamically unstable have higher rates of subsequent clinical deterioration compared to those who continue receiving adequate monitoring?

**Key Uncertainty:** Does the *timing and pattern* of testing cessation (abrupt reduction vs. gradual decrease) predict deterioration, independent of baseline severity and hospital-level practices? Existing research focuses on monitoring frequency but not on the *abruptness* of testing reduction as a risk marker.

## Clinical Importance

1. **Clinical Decision-Making:** Identifying patients at risk due to inadequate monitoring could prompt earlier intervention or escalation of care.

2. **Quality Improvement:** Understanding whether monitoring gaps are associated with deterioration could inform quality metrics and hospital policies around monitoring adequacy.

3. **Resource Allocation:** If monitoring gaps are a modifiable risk factor, this could guide decisions about when to intensify or de-escalate monitoring.

4. **Patient Safety:** Abrupt testing reduction may signal that monitoring has become inadequate relative to patient needs, potentially representing a preventable safety event.

## Substantive Advance Over Existing Knowledge

Current research on ICU monitoring:
- Typically examines *frequency* of monitoring in isolation
- Assumes monitoring intensity correlates with patient condition
- Does not distinguish between *abrupt* and *gradual* reductions in testing
- Lacks focus on the *temporal pattern* of testing cessation

**This proposal advances by:**
1. Testing whether *abrupt* testing reduction (vs. gradual decrease) is specifically predictive of deterioration
2. Examining whether the association varies across hospitals (suggesting systemic vs. patient-level factors)
3. Distinguishing between testing gaps due to patient deterioration (testing stopped because patient got worse) vs. testing gaps due to inadequate monitoring (testing stopped despite ongoing instability)

## Hypothesis

**Primary Hypothesis:** In ICU patients with hemodynamically unstable vitals (systolic BP < 90 mmHg or requiring vasopressors), an abrupt reduction in laboratory testing (defined as ≥24 hours without any lab measurement while remaining unstable) is associated with significantly higher rates of subsequent deterioration (defined as new organ dysfunction, hypotension requiring escalation, or death within 48 hours) compared to patients who continue receiving regular monitoring.

**Secondary Hypothesis:** The association between testing gaps and deterioration is weaker or absent in individual hospitals, suggesting that hospital-level practices (e.g., standardized monitoring protocols) account for most of the observed variation.

**Formal Definition:**
- **Testing gap:** Interval between consecutive lab measurements ≥24 hours while patient remains hemodynamically unstable
- **Abrupt gap:** First testing gap ≥24 hours while unstable (as opposed to gradual decrease in testing frequency)
- **Deterioration:** Any of the following within 48 hours of testing gap onset:
  - New hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation
  - New organ dysfunction (renal, respiratory, coagulation)
  - Death
  - ICU transfer to higher-acuity unit

## Population and Temporal Boundaries

- **Population:** ICU patients in the eICU-CRD database with:
  - Hemodynamically unstable vitals at index (SBP < 90 mmHg or vasopressor use)
  - ≥3 lab measurements within 48 hours prior to index
  - No prior ICU admission within 30 days
- **Temporal Window:** Index date (unstable vitals) to 48 hours follow-up
- **Inclusion:** Patients with complete lab and vital records
- **Exclusion:** Patients with end-of-life care, transfers out of ICU, or missing key variables

## Methodology

### Baseline Approach
**Logistic Regression with Hospital Fixed Effects:**
- Outcome: Deterioration within 48 hours (binary)
- Predictors: Baseline severity (SOFA score, APACHE II), age, comorbidities
- Adjustments: Hospital fixed effects to control for hospital-level variation
- Rationale: Standard approach that controls for unmeasured hospital-level confounding

### Substantive Alternative
**Time-Dependent Cox Proportional Hazards Model:**
- Outcome: Time to deterioration (time-to-event)
- Predictors: Time-varying testing gap indicator (0 = adequate monitoring, 1 = testing gap)
- Time-dependent covariates: Vasopressor use, vital signs
- Rationale: Can capture the temporal dynamics of deterioration and testing gaps

### Why the Alternative Adds Scientific Information

The baseline approach has limitations:
1. **Binary outcome:** Loses information about *when* deterioration occurs
2. **Static testing gap:** Treats testing gaps as a single binary event, ignoring the *abruptness* of the gap
3. **No temporal dynamics:** Cannot distinguish between gradual decline in monitoring vs. abrupt cessation

The alternative approach:
1. **Time-to-event:** Captures the timing and rate of deterioration
2. **Time-dependent features:** Can model testing gaps as time-varying exposures
3. **Hazard ratios:** Provides interpretable effect sizes with confidence intervals

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis would be **falsified** if:
1. Testing gaps show no association with deterioration (OR 0.8-1.2, 95% CI includes 1)
2. The association is stronger in patients with *gradual* testing reduction rather than *abrupt* gaps
3. Hospital-level analysis shows consistent effect across all hospitals (suggesting patient-level factors dominate)
4. Testing gap association disappears after adjusting for hospital-level practices

The hypothesis would be **supported** if:
1. Testing gaps are strongly associated with deterioration (OR ≥ 2.0, HR ≥ 1.5)
2. The association is specific to *abrupt* testing gaps (not gradual decline)
3. Hospital-level analysis shows heterogeneity (suggesting some hospitals may have different monitoring practices)
4. Association persists after adjusting for baseline severity and hospital fixed effects

The hypothesis would be **inconclusive** if:
1. Testing gap association is weak (OR 1.3-1.8, p > 0.05)
2. Results vary substantially by hospital or patient subgroup
3. Confounding by indication (patients with testing gaps may have different care patterns)
