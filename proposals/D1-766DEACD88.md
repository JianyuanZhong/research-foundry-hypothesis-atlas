# Scientific Proposal: Dynamic Liver Function Depletion Trajectories Predict HCC Treatment Complications and Survival

## Unresolved Question

**Do temporal trajectories of liver function reserve depletion (quantified by the rate of change in albumin, bilirubin, and INR during HCC treatment) predict treatment-related complications and long-term survival beyond what is captured by static baseline measures or composite scoring systems like Child-Pugh or MELD?**

## Background and Rationale

### Clinical Context
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment selection and monitoring traditionally rely on:
1. **Pre-diagnosis baseline liver function** (Child-Pugh, MELD scores)
2. **Static pre-treatment assessments**

However, HCC treatment (ablation, surgery, systemic therapy) causes dynamic changes in liver function that may:
- Predict treatment-related complications (bleeding, infection, liver failure)
- Identify patients at risk of early treatment failure
- Inform timing of treatment escalation or de-escalation

### The Unresolved Question
While existing research focuses on pre-diagnosis baseline liver function markers [K1][K2][K3], a critical gap remains:
- **Temporal dynamics**: How do liver function markers change *during* treatment, and do these trajectories carry independent predictive value?
- **Rate of depletion**: Is the *rate* of liver function decline during treatment more informative than the absolute baseline?
- **Non-linear patterns**: Do specific trajectory patterns (e.g., rapid early decline followed by stabilization vs. gradual progressive decline) predict different outcomes?

### Scientific Significance
1. **Personalized treatment monitoring**: Identifying patients at risk of treatment complications could trigger earlier interventions before clinical deterioration occurs.
2. **Treatment optimization**: Dynamic trajectories could inform timing of treatment escalation or de-escalation.
3. **Mechanistic insight**: Understanding how treatment affects liver function reserve could reveal biological mechanisms of treatment response and toxicity.

## Hypothesis

Patients with HCC who exhibit **accelerated rates of liver function reserve depletion** (measured by the slope of albumin, bilirubin, and INR changes during treatment) have:
1. **Higher risk of treatment-related complications** (bleeding, infection, liver failure requiring hospitalization)
2. **Worse long-term survival** after treatment
3. **Lower response rates** to curative-intent therapies (ablation, surgery)

## Study Population

- **Dataset**: HCC clinical dataset (12 source files)
- **Population**: Patients with HCC who received treatment (ablation, surgery, or systemic therapy)
- **Inclusion**: Patients with ≥3 laboratory measurements (albumin, bilirubin, INR) spanning from pre-treatment through post-treatment follow-up
- **Exclusion**: Patients with missing critical lab values or incomplete follow-up

### Key Variables
- **Primary outcome**: Treatment-related complications (bleeding, infection, liver failure requiring hospitalization)
- **Secondary outcome**: Long-term survival (death from any cause)
- **Exposure**: Rate of liver function depletion (slope of albumin, bilirubin, INR over time)
- **Covariates**: Age, sex, baseline liver function, comorbidities, tumor characteristics

### Temporal Boundaries
- **Pre-treatment window**: Up to 30 days before treatment initiation
- **Treatment window**: From treatment initiation to 90 days post-treatment
- **Follow-up**: Up to 2 years post-treatment for survival analysis

## Baseline Methods

1. **Logistic regression**: Predicting treatment complications based on baseline liver function markers
2. **Cox proportional hazards models**: Predicting survival based on baseline markers
3. **Mixed-effects models**: Accounting for repeated measurements within patients

These methods capture:
- Static associations between baseline liver function and outcomes
- Linear effects of liver function markers
- Patient-level random effects

## Alternative Methods

1. **Trajectory-based models**:
   - Piecewise linear regression to identify trajectory phases
   - Hidden Markov models to classify trajectory patterns
   - Growth mixture modeling to identify subgroups

2. **Machine learning approaches**:
   - Random forests with trajectory features
   - Gradient boosting with temporal interaction terms
   - Temporal deep learning models (LSTM, GRU) for sequence modeling

3. **Causal inference framework**:
   - G-computation for estimating causal effects of depletion rates
   - Propensity score matching to balance covariates

### Information Revealed by Alternatives

The alternative methods could reveal:
- **Non-linear relationships**: Whether the risk of complications increases exponentially with depletion rate
- **Trajectory patterns**: Whether specific patterns (e.g., rapid early decline vs. gradual decline) carry different risks
- **Temporal interactions**: Whether the effect of depletion rate varies by treatment type or timing
- **Individual heterogeneity**: Whether different patients respond differently to the same depletion rate

## Analysis Plan

### Primary Analysis
1. Calculate individual depletion rates (slope of liver function markers over treatment period)
2. Fit logistic regression models predicting treatment complications
3. Fit Cox models predicting survival
4. Compare model performance (C-index, calibration) between baseline-only and trajectory-based models

### Secondary Analysis
1. Identify trajectory subgroups using growth mixture modeling
2. Compare outcomes across subgroups
3. Test for interactions between depletion rate and treatment type

### Sensitivity Analysis
1. Vary treatment window definitions
2. Test robustness to missing data assumptions
3. Validate in held-out cohort if available

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be **falsified** if:
1. Depletion rates show no significant association with treatment complications (p > 0.05, adjusted for covariates)
2. Trajectory-based models perform no better than baseline-only models (ΔC-index < 0.02)
3. No specific trajectory patterns are associated with worse outcomes

### Supportive vs. Adverse vs. Inconclusive Results

**Supportive**:
- Depletion rates significantly predict complications and survival (p < 0.01)
- Trajectory-based models outperform baseline models (ΔC-index ≥ 0.05)
- Specific trajectory patterns (e.g., rapid early decline) are strongly associated with worse outcomes

**Adverse**:
- Depletion rates show no association with outcomes
- Baseline models perform as well as or better than trajectory-based models
- Any observed association is confounded by baseline severity

**Inconclusive**:
- Weak or moderate associations (p between 0.01-0.1)
- Models perform similarly (ΔC-index between 0.02-0.05)
- Requires larger sample size or longer follow-up for clarification

## Clinical Adjudication Required

2. **Clinical utility**: External validation in independent cohorts needed to establish clinical utility
3. **Intervention development**: If depletion rates predict complications, interventions to mitigate liver function decline need to be developed and tested
