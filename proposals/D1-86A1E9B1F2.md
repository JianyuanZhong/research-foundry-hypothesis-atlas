# Early Post-TACE Hepatic Deterioration as a Prognostic Marker in HCC: A Refined Design

## Question

In patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who undergo transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), does a composite measure of early hepatic deterioration during the first 7 days after the procedure predict subsequent hepatic decompensation, treatment failure, or death within 90 days, after adjustment for baseline liver function, tumor burden, and post-procedure observation intensity?

## Clinical importance

TACE is a common locoregional treatment for HCC, but a subset of patients experience early hepatic deterioration that may signal a need for escalation to systemic therapy or closer surveillance. Identifying these patients could inform treatment-selection decisions and resource allocation. The unresolved question is whether the early laboratory trajectory itself carries prognostic information beyond baseline liver function, or whether it merely reflects more intensive post-procedure monitoring.

## Evidence and closest prior work

- [K1] Decraecker et al. (2026) review the limitations of Child-Pugh scoring and the potential value of dynamic liver function monitoring and ALBI grade in HCC patients with moderate liver dysfunction. They do not establish a validated early post-TACE deterioration marker.
- [K2] Yang et al. (2026) develop the AAPS (AFP-ALBI-PIVKA-II-serum albumin) scoring system for HCC patients receiving PD-1 inhibitor plus TACE. Their model is retrospective, small, and focused on tumor marker response at 6-8 weeks rather than early hepatic deterioration.
- [K3] Shmatko et al. (2025) demonstrate that transformer-based models can learn longitudinal disease progression from health records, but their demonstration uses population-scale diagnoses and does not specifically address post-TACE hepatic deterioration.

## Hypothesis

A composite of bilirubin, albumin, and INR changes in the first 7 days after TACE is associated with a higher risk of hepatic decompensation, treatment failure, or death within 90 days, after adjustment for baseline liver function, tumor burden, and observation intensity.

## Composite definition

For each patient, define the early hepatic deterioration composite as follows:

1. **Baseline values**: the most recent lab value for each of bilirubin, albumin, and INR within 30 days before the index TACE procedure. If no pre-procedure value exists, use the first value in the first 24 hours post-TACE as the baseline.
2. **Early values**: the most recent lab value for each variable within days 0-7 post-TACE.
3. **Change**: for each variable, compute the change as `early_value - baseline_value`.
4. **Composite score**: sum the standardized (z-score) changes for bilirubin and INR, and subtract the standardized change for albumin (since lower albumin is worse). The composite is a single continuous variable.

A patient is classified as having "early hepatic deterioration" if the composite score is above the 75th percentile of the cohort. This is a secondary categorical definition; the primary analysis uses the continuous composite.

## Outcome definitions

The 90-day outcome is a composite of:

1. **Hepatic decompensation**: any diagnosis in the diagnoses table within 90 days of the index procedure matching `腹水` (ascites), `肝性脑病` (hepatic encephalopathy), `黄疸` (jaundice), `肝功能衰竭` (liver failure), or `肝衰竭` (liver failure).
2. **Treatment failure**: any subsequent TACE procedure or any diagnosis matching `进展` (progression) or `复发` (recurrence) within 90 days.
3. **Death**: any diagnosis matching `死亡` (death) within 90 days, or a last encounter time before 90 days with no subsequent encounters.

The primary outcome is the time to first occurrence of any of these events. Patients without an event by 90 days are censored.

## Addressing differential observation intensity

The strongest alternative explanation is that patients with more intensive post-procedure monitoring have more laboratory draws and therefore a higher probability of having an early lab value available, and that the apparent association between the composite and outcomes may reflect this selection rather than true hepatic deterioration.

To address this:

1. **Observation intensity variable**: count the number of encounters and laboratory draws in the first 7 days post-TACE for each patient.
2. **Primary adjustment**: include the observation intensity variable in the regression model as a covariate.
3. **Sensitivity analysis**: restrict to patients with at least one early lab draw for each of bilirubin, albumin, and INR (the core ALBI components). This reduces selection bias from missing early labs.
4. **Negative control**: test whether the composite is associated with a non-hepatic outcome (e.g., a diagnosis unrelated to liver function) within 90 days. If the association persists for a non-hepatic outcome, this suggests the composite is picking up observation intensity rather than true hepatic deterioration.
5. **Distinguishing result**: if the association between the composite and the hepatic outcome persists after adjustment for observation intensity and in the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with complete early labs, this supports true hepatic deterioration. If the association disappears after adjustment or in the sensitivity analysis, this suggests the signal is driven by observation intensity.

## Methods

### Baseline model

Logistic regression on baseline variables (age, sex, baseline bilirubin, albumin, INR, tumor burden) to predict the 90-day outcome. This establishes the performance of a model that does not use early post-TACE information.

### Time-aware alternative

Cox proportional hazards model (or flexible parametric survival model) using the early hepatic deterioration composite and its interaction with time. The landmark analysis is performed at day 7 post-TACE: patients with at least one early lab draw are included, and the model predicts the 90-day outcome from day 7 onward. This allows the early composite to be used as a time-varying predictor.

### Estimand

The primary estimand is the hazard ratio for the 90-day outcome per standard deviation increase in the early hepatic deterioration composite, adjusted for baseline covariates and observation intensity.

### Split

The data are split 70/30 into training and test sets at the patient level. The training set is used to fit the models and determine the 75th percentile cutoff for the categorical composite. The test set is used for evaluation.

### Outcome and uncertainty evaluation

The primary outcome is the 90-day composite. Uncertainty is evaluated using 95% confidence intervals for the hazard ratio, obtained by bootstrap resampling of patients (1000 resamples). The baseline and time-aware models are compared using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent AUC at 30, 60, and 90 days.

### Resource budget

The analysis is CPU-only. The expected runtime is less than 1 hour for the full cohort, given the large but manageable size of the data. No GPU is required.

## Interpretation of results

- **Supportive result**: the early hepatic deterioration composite is significantly associated with the 90-day outcome (hazard ratio > 1, p < 0.05) after adjustment for baseline covariates and observation intensity, and the association persists in the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with complete early labs. This supports the hypothesis that early hepatic deterioration is a prognostic marker.
- **Adverse result**: the association is not significant after adjustment, or the association disappears in the sensitivity analysis. This suggests that the early composite does not add prognostic information beyond baseline variables, or that the signal is driven by observation intensity.
- **Inconclusive result**: the association is significant in the primary analysis but not in the sensitivity analysis, or the confidence intervals are wide. This suggests that the evidence is not strong enough to draw a definitive conclusion, and further study is needed.

## Stronger conclusions requiring additional data

- Causal inference: the observational design cannot establish causality. A causal claim would require a randomized trial or a stronger causal design (e.g., instrumental variable analysis).
- External validation: the single-center data may limit generalizability. External validation in a different cohort would be needed to establish the robustness of the finding.
- Clinical utility: the prognostic value of the composite would need to be evaluated in a clinical decision-making context, including cost-effectiveness analysis.
