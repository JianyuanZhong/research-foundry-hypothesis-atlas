# Provisional UK Biobank hypothesis: discordant glycemia and weight trajectory

## Status

This is a provisional, explicitly unverified restart after the pancreatic-cancer seed failed its prespecified zero-event feasibility gate. It is not a selected hypothesis and no full cohort analysis has been run in this branch. The exact catalog/table binding and event-count audit remain to be completed before any scientific claim is made.

## Proposed relationship

Among UK Biobank participants without diabetes or cancer at a second assessment-center visit, a similar rise in HbA1c between two visits is associated with higher subsequent incident diabetes risk when accompanied by weight loss than when accompanied by weight gain. The clinical decision relevance is whether a patient with worsening glycemia should be interpreted differently when the same biochemical change co-occurs with unintentional or unexplained weight loss, rather than being treated as a single diabetes-risk state.

This is distinct from the failed pancreatic-cancer seed: the proposed endpoint is a common, clinically actionable metabolic transition, while the body-composition trajectory is the explanatory contrast. It does not claim that weight loss causes diabetes or that the pattern identifies occult cancer.

## Why this is worth testing

The unresolved question is whether the same glycemic deterioration has different prognostic meaning depending on the concurrent body-composition trajectory. A simple comparison of HbA1c change would lose this information. The clinically consequential rival is that weight loss is a marker of pre-existing illness, treatment, frailty, or observation intensity rather than a distinct pathway. A further rival is that the association is driven by the interval between visits or by selection into repeat assessment.

The substantive advance would be a temporally explicit, patient-level contrast: whether the joint pattern, rather than either measure alone, identifies a subgroup needing earlier metabolic follow-up. This is a prognostic association, not a causal or mechanistic claim.

The exact source paths, table names, column names, archive members, and join keys must be read from the catalog before execution. The intended join is within the same UKB/ukb672073 participant namespace using the documented participant key; no cross-dataset join is assumed. The primary time origin is the second valid assessment date, with outcomes restricted to dates after that origin and excluding diagnoses already present before or at the origin.

## Population, temporal boundaries, and outcomes

Planned population:
1. Adults with two valid assessment-center dates and paired HbA1c, glucose, weight, and BMI measurements.
2. No incident diabetes, cancer, or other prespecified competing diagnosis before the second visit.
3. Follow-up begins at the second visit; censor at death, loss to follow-up, or end of available registry follow-up.
4. Prespecify a short window (for example, 2 years) and a longer window (for example, 5 years), with the primary window fixed after the catalog audit and before outcome modeling.

Primary outcome: incident diabetes using the available diagnosis fields and dates, with a prespecified sensitivity definition that distinguishes inpatient diagnosis from broader diagnosis ascertainment. Secondary outcomes: incident cancer and death, only if event support is adequate; these are not substitutes for the primary question.

Exposure contrast: compare weight loss versus weight gain/stability at comparable HbA1c rise, with continuous and prespecified categorical versions. Do not define the exposure using post-origin outcomes.

## Baseline and substantive alternative

Simple baseline: a prespecified discrete-time survival or Cox model with HbA1c change, weight-change direction/magnitude, assessment interval, age, sex, baseline BMI, smoking, and available treatment/medication indicators. Include an interaction between HbA1c change and weight-change direction. Use a participant-level train/validation/test split defined before fitting, with clustered bootstrap or repeated split uncertainty.

Substantive alternative: a joint repeated-measurement trajectory model (or a structured two-stage model) for HbA1c and weight, estimating slope/curvature and their joint latent state, then relating the trajectory state to incident diabetes. It uses the same participants, outcome, time origin, split, and uncertainty evaluation as the baseline. It could reveal whether persistence, timing, and curvature carry information that a single between-visit difference loses; it would be deferred if repeated measurements are too sparse, the event count is inadequate, or the model is not identifiable.

The comparison is scientific, not a contest for a small predictive gain: the baseline tests the interpretable conditional contrast; the trajectory model tests whether the signal is a durable pattern rather than a noisy endpoint difference. CPU is adequate for the baseline and likely adequate for a bounded trajectory analysis; no GPU is required unless a later implementation demonstrates a concrete need.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive result: the weight-loss contrast remains positive after interval and treatment handling, with uncertainty excluding a clinically negligible effect, and the trajectory model shows a consistent pattern beyond the baseline. This would support a prognostic stratification hypothesis only.

Adverse result: no excess risk for weight loss at comparable HbA1c rise, or the association disappears after excluding early events, handling treatment, or requiring adequate follow-up. This would weaken or refute the proposed contrast.

Inconclusive result: wide uncertainty, sparse repeat overlap, inadequate event support, or instability across windows/splits. This would justify revising or abandoning the direction, not selecting a favorable subgroup.

A positive association would not establish causality, a mechanism, or a cancer diagnosis. Clinical adjudication of diabetes onset, treatment intent, unintentional weight loss, and preclinical disease would require additional clinical review or another study.
