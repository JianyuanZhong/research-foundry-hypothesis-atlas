# UKB-05: Low bone density with muscle weakness — independent fracture risk beyond BMD alone

## Unresolved question
In older adults with low bone mineral density (BMD), does low hand grip strength independently predict subsequent inpatient fracture risk beyond what BMD alone captures, and is this excess risk partly mediated by falls? Current fracture risk assessment (e.g., FRAX) is centered on BMD and clinical risk factors, but the incremental value of muscle strength is unclear. If low grip strength adds independent predictive value, this would support integrating muscle assessment into fracture risk stratification for osteopenic/osteoporotic patients.

## Clinical importance
Fragility fractures are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults. Current guidelines focus on BMD-based osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment. If muscle weakness independently predicts fracture in low-BMD patients, this would:
1. Identify a subgroup of osteopenic/osteoporotic patients at higher fracture risk who may benefit from combined bone + muscle interventions.
2. Support the emerging "osteosarcopenia" concept as a clinically actionable phenotype.
3. Inform whether fall prevention programs should be prioritized in low-BMD patients with low grip strength.

## Substantive advance over existing knowledge
- [K1] Lu et al. (2026, JCSM) showed that sarcopenia-specific BMD cut-offs improve hip fracture prediction vs conventional osteosarcopenia definition, in a 4,000-person Hong Kong cohort. However, they used DXA-derived body composition (not grip strength) and focused on hip fracture only. Our study uses grip strength (a direct muscle function measure) and all inpatient fractures, in a much larger UKB cohort (~46,000 with DXA + grip).
- [K2] Yue et al. (2026, Front Med) linked dementia to probable osteosarcopenia and fracture in UKB (151,751 participants), showing that low grip strength-defined sarcopenia is associated with fracture. However, they focused on dementia as the exposure, not on the independent contribution of grip strength beyond BMD in a DXA-verified low-BMD cohort.
- [K3] Lu et al. (2026, Front Med) proposed a bone-muscle-function framework for fragility fracture prevention, arguing that muscle dysfunction and fall propensity are distinct from bone fragility. This is a conceptual framework without a large-cohort empirical test of the independent contribution of grip strength beyond BMD.

Our study directly tests whether grip strength adds independent predictive value beyond BMD for all-cause inpatient fracture in a large UKB DXA subcohort, with falls as a potential mediator.

## Hypothesis (unresolved claim)
Among UKB participants with low BMD (T-score < -1.0 at lumbar spine or femoral neck), those with low grip strength (below sex-specific median) have a higher hazard of subsequent inpatient fracture than those with normal grip strength, independent of age, sex, BMI, and smoking. This excess risk is partly mediated by subsequent falls.

## Study design

### Population
- UKB participants with DXA (field 23204 L1-L4 BMD or 23208 femoral neck BMD at assessment instance 2 or 3).
- Grip strength (field 46 left or 47 right) at any instance.
- No prior fracture (41270/41280) before DXA date.
- Excluded: participants with prevalent cancer, severe renal disease, or amputation (if identifiable from 41270/20003).

### Exposure
- Low BMD: T-score < -1.0 (osteopenia) or < -2.5 (osteoporosis) at lumbar spine (23204) or femoral neck (23208).
- Low grip strength: below sex-specific median of grip (46/47) in the DXA cohort.
- Interaction: 4 groups — (1) normal BMD + normal grip, (2) low BMD + normal grip, (3) normal BMD + low grip, (4) low BMD + low grip.

### Outcome
- Primary: subsequent inpatient fracture (41270 ICD10 codes for fracture, 41280 date) after DXA date.
- Secondary: subsequent falls (2296 at instance 3, if DXA at instance 2) as a mediator.
- Competing risk: death (40000) before fracture.

### Adjustments
- Age at DXA, sex, BMI (21001), smoking status (20116), physical activity (if available).

### Analysis
1. **Baseline (Cox PH):** Hazard ratios for fracture by BMD category, grip category, and BMD×grip interaction, adjusting for covariates. Test whether the interaction is significant (p<0.05).
2. **Learned alternative (XGBoost):** Fit a gradient boosting model with BMD, grip, falls, age, sex, BMI, smoking as features, predicting fracture within 5 years. Compare AUROC and calibration vs Cox model. If XGBoost AUROC > Cox AUROC by >0.02, there are non-linear interactions the linear model misses.
3. **Mediation:** If low grip → more falls → more fracture, estimate the proportion mediated by falls (causal mediation analysis, acknowledging that falls are measured at a single later timepoint, not continuously).

### Falsification criteria
- **Adverse (hypothesis not supported):** If the BMD×grip interaction is not significant (p>0.1) and the HR for low grip within the low-BMD group is within the 95% CI of 1.0, then grip strength does not independently predict fracture beyond BMD.
- **Inconclusive:** If the interaction is borderline (p=0.05-0.10) or the XGBoost model shows only marginal improvement (AUROC difference <0.01), the result is suggestive but not conclusive.
- **Supportive:** If the interaction is significant (p<0.05) and the HR for low grip within the low-BMD group is >1.2 with 95% CI excluding 1.0, and the XGBoost model confirms non-linear interactions, the hypothesis is supported.

### What each outcome would and would not establish
- **Supportive result:** Would establish that grip strength adds independent predictive value beyond BMD for fracture in a large UKB cohort. Would NOT establish causality (residual confounding by frailty, medication, healthcare access). Would NOT establish that muscle interventions prevent fracture (requires RCT).
- **Adverse result:** Would suggest that BMD is the dominant predictor and grip strength's contribution is negligible in this population. Would NOT rule out a role for muscle in fracture risk in other populations (e.g., very old, institutionalized).
- **Inconclusive result:** Would warrant further investigation with longer follow-up, repeated grip measurements, or a different muscle function measure (e.g., gait speed).

### Method comparison
- **Baseline:** Cox PH with BMD category + grip category + interaction + covariates. Simple, interpretable, standard for time-to-event.
- **Learned alternative:** XGBoost with same features + falls. Can capture non-linear interactions and threshold effects. Compare AUROC, calibration, and feature importance.
- **What the alternative reveals:** If XGBoost significantly outperforms Cox, there are non-linear or higher-order interactions (e.g., grip only matters below a certain BMD threshold) that the linear model misses. If performance is similar, the linear interaction is the key finding and the relationship is approximately multiplicative.
- **Resource budget:** Both models fit in <10 minutes on CPU with 46,743 participants. No GPU needed. Total compute: <1 hour including data preparation.

### Limitations
1. **Single DXA measurement:** BMD is measured once (inst2/3), not longitudinally. BMD change over time is not captured.
2. **Single grip measurement:** Grip is measured at one or more instances, but not continuously. Muscle decline over time is not captured.
3. **Falls as a single timepoint:** Falls (2296) are self-reported at assessment, not continuously monitored. Mediation analysis is limited.
4. **Inpatient fracture only:** 41270/41280 capture inpatient diagnoses. Outpatient fractures (e.g., wrist, finger) are missed.
5. **Residual confounding:** Frailty, medication (bisphosphonates, steroids), healthcare access, and lifestyle factors not fully captured.
6. **Selection:** UKB participants are healthier than the general population. Results may not generalize to very old or institutionalized adults.

### Essential clinical adjudication
- Fracture ICD10 codes need clinical adjudication to confirm they are true fragility fractures (not traumatic fractures from high-energy injury). This requires expert review of a subset of cases.
- The proportion of fractures that are truly "fragility" (low-energy) vs traumatic is not directly available from ICD10 codes alone.
