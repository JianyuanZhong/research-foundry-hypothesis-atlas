# Recovery discordance after ICU discharge: when a reassuring last value hides incomplete physiologic recovery

## Scientific question and hypothesis

The episode-1 parent established a feasible prognostic question: residual oxygen/support burden or physiologic volatility in the final 24 hours before ICU discharge may predict early ICU return or death. The remaining scientific ambiguity is whether that signal represents incomplete recovery and persistence, or simply the last recorded value and the intensity/location of documentation.

Primary falsifiable hypothesis: among adults discharged alive from the first ICU stay of a hospitalization, patients whose final observed hour is relatively reassuring but whose preceding 24-hour record shows persistent support or repeated physiologic excursions have higher risk of ICU return or exact-timestamp in-hospital death within 48 hours than patients with the same final-hour snapshot and a stable/recovering preceding trajectory.

A companion information hypothesis is that a prespecified recovery-state representation of the ordered 24-hour record improves held-out risk calibration and risk separation over a last-hour snapshot baseline, and that this improvement persists after masking/order and care-context falsification checks. The claim being tested is prognostic information about recovery trajectory, not that delaying discharge, changing oxygen therapy, or any other intervention improves outcomes.

The strongest claim supported before the experiment is data feasibility: the frozen MIMIC-IV 3.1 archive contains the relevant ICU stay/admission timestamps, repeated charted physiologic/support variables, and within-hospital ICU-return/death fields. The parent’s bounded scan found 70,138 first-hospital-ICU stays with linked admissions, valid ICU intervals and dischtime after outtime, 3,872 later ICU returns within 48 hours, 856 exact-timestamp deaths in the window, and 4,630 composite events. Those are feasibility counts, not evidence for the hypothesis; final feature coverage, missingness, association estimates and held-out calibration remain unknown.

## Clinical importance and advance

ICU discharge is a consequential transition: a patient can have a normal final value while still requiring support or showing repeated instability. If the trajectory identifies a reproducible high-risk subgroup, it could motivate prospective validation of targeted floor monitoring, step-down placement, or discharge-readiness criteria. The substantive advance is to test “recovery discordance”—the difference between a final snapshot and the preceding trajectory—rather than treating a single last measurement or a generic severity score as recovery.

A supportive retrospective association would justify prospective adjudication and a multi-site validation study. It would not establish a discharge policy, causality, planned versus unplanned return, or treatment benefit. An adverse result would be valuable: it would show that the trajectory adds no reliable information beyond the snapshot/case mix under these definitions, weakening the rationale for using this signal alone.

## Population and time origin

Use MIMIC-IV 3.1 adults represented by anchor_age, retaining and reporting the >89-year top-code limitation (anchor_age 91).

- From each hospitalization (subject_id, hadm_id), select the first ICU stay by intime from icu/icustays with non-null outtime and outtime > intime, a linked hosp/admissions row, and dischtime > outtime. Exclude impossible timestamps and duplicate/anomalous links, reporting every exclusion. This avoids correlated discharge landmarks while allowing a later ICU stay in the same hospitalization to be the outcome.
- The index is the selected stay’s outtime. Predictors use only charttime in [outtime - 24 hours, outtime). storetime is not a predictor time. No post-outtime text, measurement, order, or transfer is used.
- Follow-up is (outtime, min(outtime + 48 hours, dischtime)]. ICU return is any subsequent icu/icustays row for the same subject_id and hadm_id with intime > outtime and intime no later than outtime + 48 hours. The data do not reliably distinguish planned from unplanned return, so the endpoint must be called ICU return.
- Primary outcome is ICU return or hosp/admissions.deathtime in the follow-up interval. Secondary outcomes are ICU return by 7 days, each component separately, and a competing-risk analysis of ICU return versus exact-timestamp in-hospital death before dischtime. hospital_expire_flag is a sensitivity flag only when deathtime is absent; it must not be assigned an artificial event time.
- Keep patients who later die in the risk set. If dischtime <= outtime, exclude from this 48-hour risk set and report the count. MIMIC’s deidentified dates are subject-specific shifted times; no cross-subject calendar split or calendar trend is inferred.

## Exposure/trajectory construct

The primary descriptive phenotype is recovery discordance among patients with a valid final-hour snapshot: no core-vital excursion in the final available hour, but either (a) at least 25% of observed hourly bins in the preceding 24 hours have a core-vital excursion, or (b) support remains above its prespecified threshold in at least 6 observed hourly bins. The denominator is observed bins for that variable; a missing bin is not normal. Report the coverage required for this phenotype and analyze the full cohort with masks as the primary model-based test. If support coverage is inadequate, the vital-excursion phenotype remains primary and the support phenotype is explicitly downgraded to infeasible/inconclusive rather than silently substituted.

The alternative recovery-state model uses the same 24 bins and variables, with a three-state missingness-aware emission model fit only in training data. States are ordered after fitting by support/instability emissions and labeled descriptively as lower-support/stable, recovering/intermediate, and persistent-support/unstable; labels are not diagnoses. Derive last state, time in each state, state transitions, and whether the final 6 hours improve over the previous 6 hours. This asks whether persistence and order contain information that a final snapshot loses.

## Baseline, alternative and analysis

The primary baseline is a penalized logistic regression for the 48-hour composite using final-hour numeric values, final-hour masks, separately encoded support/context, age, gender, ICU LOS, first/last careunit, admission_type, and a small prespecified set of admission covariates. A snapshot-only model without all trajectory summaries is the key comparator. A fixed-window-summary sensitivity adds medians, maxima, minima, slopes and observed-bin fractions but no order/state features.

The substantive alternative is the recovery-state representation above followed by a penalized logistic model with the baseline covariates. It is selected because it tests a physiologic/recovery interpretation and exposes persistence and transition order. A small masked GRU on the identical 24-bin inputs is deferred, not rejected categorically: it would be reconsidered if the state model is unstable or if nonlinear sequence interactions become a specific scientific question. Text modeling is deferred because local text extraction is unvalidated for clinical labels; raw waveform modeling is unavailable because the MIMIC waveform dataset is not in the configured snapshot.

Split by subject_id before any fitting so no patient crosses train/validation/test. Fit the state model, imputation/normalization, and penalties on training data only; select/tune on validation; lock the final models before testing. Report held-out AUROC, AUPRC, integrated Brier score, calibration intercept/slope/curve, bootstrap confidence intervals, and absolute risk differences across the prespecified discordance strata. Report calibration by age band, sex, first careunit, admission type and support-context group only when counts are adequate. Use cause-specific/competing-risk summaries for ICU return versus death.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive evidence requires all of the following: the discordant phenotype has higher adjusted 48-hour composite risk with a confidence interval excluding the null and a clinically meaningful prespecified risk margin; the result is directionally consistent for component outcomes; and the recovery-state model improves calibrated held-out risk estimation or risk separation over the snapshot baseline with uncertainty that rules out a practically negligible improvement. The effect should persist in threshold sensitivity and after adjusting for careunit/admission type. This supports a prognostic recovery signal and a prospective monitoring/adjudication study only.

Adverse evidence is a null or reversed discordance association, no reproducible state-transition signal, no calibrated held-out improvement, or disappearance after accounting for masks, careunit, admission type, or within-stay order permutation. That would falsify the stated incremental recovery hypothesis and argue against using the construct alone in discharge decisions.

Inconclusive evidence includes inadequate final-hour or 24-hour coverage, unstable state labels, too few phenotype/events, wide intervals crossing clinically important margins, missing/ambiguous death timing, non-positivity, or performance differences driven only by documentation masks. It supports reporting feasibility and uncertainty, not a clinical rule.

The data cannot adjudicate bedside clinical stability, goals of care, treatment limitation, floor staffing, reasons for ICU discharge, planned versus unplanned return, post-hospital deterioration, or whether changing discharge timing improves outcomes. MIMIC’s notes are not used as a validated primary label; radiology text is available but images are not, and raw waveforms are unavailable. Clinical interpretation therefore requires expert adjudication, prospective or multi-site validation, and possibly a causal study. Automatic readiness or verification can establish correct paths, computations and linkage of conclusions to outputs, but cannot establish clinical truth.
