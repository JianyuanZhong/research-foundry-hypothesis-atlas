# Provisional UK Biobank hypothesis: discordant glycemia and weight trajectory

## Status

This is a provisional, explicitly unverified restart after the prior episode’s pancreatic-cancer direction failed its prespecified zero-event feasibility gate. No full cohort analysis has been run in this branch. It is a hypothesis for later testing, not an observed result.

## Proposed relationship

Among UK Biobank participants without diabetes or cancer at a second assessment-center visit, a similar rise in HbA1c between two visits is associated with higher subsequent incident diabetes risk when accompanied by weight loss than when accompanied by weight gain. The clinical question is whether worsening glycemia should be interpreted differently when the same biochemical change co-occurs with weight loss, rather than being treated as one diabetes-risk state.

This is a substantive alternative to a simple “HbA1c predicts diabetes” model: the interaction asks whether the body-composition trajectory carries information about the biological meaning of glycemic change. It could inform risk stratification and the need for closer follow-up, but it does not establish causality or a treatment effect.

## Evidence and uncertainty

The available dataset catalog identifies UK Biobank source groups with assessment-center, biological-sample, population-characteristic and health-outcome tables, and the prior episode recorded a feasibility failure for pancreatic cancer but not for this metabolic endpoint. The exact table/column bindings, participant overlap, measurement dates, missingness, prevalent-diabetes exclusions, and incident-event support have not yet been verified in this branch. No literature has been inspected yet for this draft; therefore no novelty or external-support claim is made here.

The strongest claim currently supported is only that a common metabolic transition may be testable in principle with the configured UKB source groups. The unresolved claim is the interaction between HbA1c change and weight trajectory for incident diabetes. Weight loss may reflect illness, medication, or reverse causation; HbA1c change may be affected by assay timing and measurement error. These are explicit rival explanations, not evidence for the hypothesis.

## Falsifiable test

Define a second-visit baseline after excluding prevalent diabetes and cancer, with two prior HbA1c measurements and two weight measurements. Compare subsequent incident diabetes (using the health-outcome diagnosis/date fields once their exact columns and temporal cutoff are verified) across discordant groups: HbA1c rise with weight loss versus HbA1c rise with weight gain, with prespecified effect modification by weight trajectory. Use a simple baseline model with HbA1c level/change and conventional covariates, and compare it with a trajectory model that includes the interaction and, if justified, a mechanistic/latent trajectory alternative. Evaluate discrimination and calibration on a held-out temporal split, with uncertainty intervals and negative-control outcomes where feasible.

Supportive evidence would be a reproducible interaction with adequate event support and calibration, not merely a small predictive gain. Adverse evidence would be no interaction after accounting for measurement timing and baseline severity, or a signal driven by prevalent disease/weight-loss illness. Inconclusive evidence would include too few events, unstable estimates, or missingness that prevents a defensible comparison. Clinical interpretation requires adjudication of diabetes ascertainment and unavailable treatment/medication context.

## Method comparison

Baseline: a prespecified Cox or discrete-time model for HbA1c level/change, weight, age, sex and available covariates, with temporal train/validation/test splits and bootstrap or cluster uncertainty.

Alternative: a trajectory/interaction model comparing HbA1c rise by weight trajectory, or a latent trajectory model if repeated measurements justify it. The alternative can reveal whether the same glycemic change has different prognostic meaning by body-composition trajectory, information lost by a single HbA1c predictor. It is scientifically useful only if the interaction and calibration are stable; it is not justified by predictive complexity alone.
