# Multi-Organ Recovery Trajectory Hypothesis

## Scientific Question

After initial stabilization in critical illness, patients whose recovery trajectories across multiple organ systems show **asynchronous patterns** have worse outcomes than those with **synchronous recovery**.

This hypothesis extends the fundamental insight from Foy et al. (2022) that WBC-platelet co-regulation defines universal inflammatory recovery trajectories, to a multi-organ framework. While Foy et al. demonstrated that exponential WBC decay followed by delayed linear PLT growth characterizes favorable inflammatory recovery, this proposal tests whether this temporal coordination principle generalizes across multiple organ systems, and whether deviations from synchronous recovery (asynchronous patterns) predict adverse outcomes.

## Clinical Importance

The resolution of this hypothesis has direct clinical implications:

1. **Early Risk Stratification**: Identifying patients with asynchronous organ recovery trajectories could enable earlier intervention before deterioration becomes irreversible.

2. **Precision Risk Models**: Moving beyond aggregate organ scores (SOFA, APACHE) to trajectory-based risk assessment that captures the temporal coordination of recovery across organ systems.

3. **Therapeutic Timing**: Understanding whether interventions (e.g., renal replacement therapy, ventilator weaning) should be timed to align with recovery of dependent organ systems.

4. **Prognostic Enhancement**: Adding trajectory-based metrics to existing risk scores could improve prediction of mortality and complications.

## Substantive Advance Over Existing Knowledge

**Existing Knowledge (Foy et al. 2022)**:
- WBC-PLT co-regulation (exponential WBC decay + delayed linear PLT growth) defines favorable inflammatory recovery
- This 2D trajectory predicts outcomes with 32x relative risk for cardiac surgery patients deviating from the trajectory
- The principle applies across multiple surgical conditions

**Limitation of Existing Knowledge**:
- Blood cell dynamics are only one aspect of organ recovery
- No evidence that this temporal coordination principle generalizes to multi-organ systems (kidney, lung, heart, liver, etc.)
- No investigation of whether cross-organ coordination matters for outcomes

**Proposed Advance**:
- Test whether multi-organ recovery trajectories (not just blood cell dynamics) follow similar temporal coordination patterns
- Determine if asynchronous recovery across organ systems (e.g., kidney recovers before lung, or vice versa) predicts worse outcomes
- Identify which organ pairs show the strongest coordination signals and which discordances are most predictive

## Hypothesis Statement

**Primary Hypothesis**: After circulatory stabilization in critically ill patients, those whose recovery trajectories across multiple organ systems exhibit **asynchronous patterns** (where recovery of one organ lags significantly behind others) have worse 28-day mortality and longer ICU stays compared to patients with **synchronous recovery** (where multiple organ systems recover in coordinated temporal sequences).

**Secondary Hypothesis**: The degree of temporal coordination (measured by inter-organ recovery lag times) is more strongly associated with outcomes than single-organ recovery metrics or aggregate scores like SOFA.

## Study Population and Data Bindings

**Population**:
- Adults with infection or shock requiring ICU admission
- Minimum 48-hour ICU stay
- At least 3 days of daily laboratory measurements
- Follow-up for 28 days post-ICU discharge

**Primary Data Tables**:

1. **icu/icustays**:
   - `subject_id`, `hadm_id`, `stay_id`, `intime`, `outtime`, `los`
   - Defines ICU stay boundaries and discharge timing

2. **icu/chartevents**:
   - `subject_id`, `hadm_id`, `stay_id`, `caregiver_id`, `charttime`, `itemid`, `value`, `valuenum`, `valueuom`
   - Contains timestamped measurements of vitals and labs

3. **icu/ingredientevents** or **icu/inputevents**:
   - For medication timing and escalation tracking

4. **hosp/d_icd_diagnoses** or **hosp/diagnoses_icd**:
   - `subject_id`, `hadm_id`, `seq_num`, `icd_code`, `icd_version`
   - For infection/sepsis identification (ICD-9/ICD-10 codes)

5. **hosp/patients**:
   - `subject_id`, `gender`, `anchor_age`, `anchor_year`
   - For demographic covariates

6. **hosp/pharmacy**:
   - For vasopressor/organ support escalation tracking

**Key Items (itemid) for Organ Function**:
- **Kidney**: Creatinine (e.g., itemid 31092783), BUN
- **Respiratory**: PaO2/FiO2 ratio, pCO2, SpO2
- **Cardiovascular**: MAP, HR, SBP, SBP/DBP
- **Liver**: Bilirubin, AST, ALT
- **Metabolic**: Glucose, lactate

**Key Items for Infection/Severe Illness**:
- WBC count (itemid 31092783 or similar)
- Platelet count (itemid 31092783 or similar)
- Fever markers, inflammatory markers

## Analysis Plan

### Phase 1: Define Recovery Criteria

1. **Circulatory Stabilization**: Define as MAP ≥ 70 mmHg for 2 consecutive measurements with 2-hour intervals, or vasopressor weaning to minimal doses.

2. **Organ Recovery Definition**: For each organ system, define "recovery" as return to reference interval or improvement by ≥2 standard deviations from nadir.

3. **Recovery Timepoint**: Time from circulatory stabilization to organ recovery.

### Phase 2: Compute Recovery Trajectories

1. **Time Series Construction**: For each patient, extract daily (or more frequent) measurements for each organ system after circulatory stabilization.

2. **Recovery Lag Calculation**: For each pair of organ systems (kidney-lung, kidney-liver, etc.), compute the time lag between recovery of each system.

3. **Synchronous vs Asynchronous Classification**:
   - **Synchronous**: All organs recover within a window of ≤24 hours of each other
   - **Asynchronous**: Recovery lags between organs exceed 24 hours
   - **Degree of Asynchrony**: Sum of absolute lags between all organ pairs

### Phase 3: Outcome Assessment

1. **Primary Outcome**: 28-day mortality (from hosp/admissions deathtime)
2. **Secondary Outcomes**:
   - ICU length of stay
   - Hospital length of stay
   - 90-day mortality
   - Need for additional organ support post-discharge

### Phase 4: Statistical Analysis

1. **Cox Proportional Hazards**:
   - Hazard ratio for 28-day mortality comparing synchronous vs asynchronous recovery
   - Adjust for age, comorbidities ( Charlson index from diagnoses), infection type, severity of initial insult

2. **Multivariate Modeling**:
   - Include degree of asynchrony as continuous variable
   - Test for non-linear relationships (e.g., exponential increase in risk with increasing asynchrony)

3. **Sensitivity Analyses**:
   - Different recovery definitions (24h vs 48h vs 72h windows)
   - Different organ combinations
   - Subgroups by etiology (infection vs shock vs trauma)

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be **falsified** if:

1. No significant difference in outcomes between synchronous and asynchronous recovery groups after adjustment for confounders (p > 0.05 for primary outcome)

2. The degree of asynchrony shows no dose-response relationship with outcomes (e.g., hazard ratio not increasing with greater asynchrony)

3. Single-organ recovery metrics or aggregate scores (SOFA) perform equally well or better than multi-organ trajectory metrics

## Baseline Comparison

**Baseline Approach**:
- Use aggregate organ scores (SOFA, APACHE II) as the comparator
- Test whether trajectory-based metrics add predictive value beyond aggregate scores

**Expected Baseline Performance**:
- SOFA score at admission: Moderate prediction (AUC ~0.70-0.75)
- SOFA at day 3: Better prediction (AUC ~0.75-0.80)
- Trajectory-based metrics: Hypothesized to improve prediction (AUC > 0.80)

## Limitations

1. **Retrospective Design**: Cannot establish causality
2. **Measurement Frequency**: Daily measurements may miss rapid changes; continuous monitoring data not available
3. **Missing Data**: Some patients may have incomplete laboratory measurements
4. **Selection Bias**: ICU population may not be representative of all critically ill patients
5. **Confounding**: Unmeasured confounders (e.g., specific pathogen, genetic factors) may influence both recovery pattern and outcomes
