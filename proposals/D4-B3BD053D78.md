# UKB 04: Does systemic reserve add prognostic information beyond lung function after infection-related hospitalization?

## Unresolved question and clinical importance
Among UK Biobank adults with a pre-illness lung function measurement and a later infection-related hospitalization, does preserved systemic reserve identify lower short-term mortality beyond what is explained by pre-illness lung function and measured illness severity?

The clinical value is that lung function alone may not capture the physiologic reserve that determines recovery from infection. If reserve adds information after adjustment for illness severity, post-discharge monitoring, rehabilitation and escalation decisions could be better targeted. If it does not, the result would argue against using reserve as an independent post-infection prognostic marker in this population.

## Evidence opening and closest inspected work
The closest inspected work supports that frailty and muscle strength are associated with adverse outcomes, while infection studies show that baseline physiologic state matters for recovery. These works do not directly test whether systemic reserve adds information beyond pre-illness lung function and measured illness severity in a UKB 04 infection cohort.

The unresolved claim is whether systemic reserve is an independent prognostic signal after accounting for pre-illness lung function and illness severity. The leading claim is that reserve captures a distinct recovery capacity. The strongest rival is that reserve is a proxy for frailty, chronic disease, or illness severity and adds no information once those factors are accounted for.

## Population and temporal design
Use UK Biobank UKB 04 participants with:
- a pre-illness lung function measurement (FEV1 or FVC) recorded before the index hospitalization;
- a later infection-related hospitalization identified from in-patient diagnosis codes;
- available systemic reserve measurements (hand grip strength, albumin, and creatinine) recorded before the index hospitalization.

Define the index date as the first in-patient infection-related diagnosis date. Exclude measurements taken during or after the index event. Exclude participants with a prior cancer diagnosis before the index date, because cancer may confound both reserve and mortality.

## Variables and outcomes
Primary outcome: all-cause mortality within 90 days after the index hospitalization.

Exposure: systemic reserve, measured as hand grip strength (left and right), albumin, and creatinine, all recorded before the index hospitalization.

Covariates: pre-illness lung function (FEV1/FVC), age, sex, smoking status, body mass index, and illness severity proxies (length of stay, number of in-patient diagnoses, and comorbidity indicators).

Secondary outcome: mortality within 1 year after the index hospitalization.

## Method comparison
Baseline: a severity-adjusted Cox proportional hazards model with pre-illness lung function, age, sex, smoking, BMI, and illness severity proxies.

Learned alternative: a gradient-boosted model with the same covariates plus systemic reserve variables. The learned model can capture nonlinear and interaction effects that the linear baseline loses.

The comparison tests whether reserve adds information beyond the linear baseline. If the learned model improves discrimination and calibration beyond the baseline, reserve may capture a distinct recovery capacity. If it does not, the result supports the rival explanation that reserve is a proxy for frailty or illness severity.

## Analysis and falsification
Fit both models on the full cohort with a time-based split (train on earlier index dates, test on later index dates) to avoid leakage. Evaluate discrimination (C-index), calibration (Brier score), and calibration-in-the-large. Use bootstrap confidence intervals for the C-index difference.

Falsification criteria:
- Supportive: the learned model improves C-index by at least 0.02 over the baseline, with a bootstrap 95% CI excluding zero, and the improvement is driven by reserve variables (verified by permutation testing).
- Adverse: the learned model does not improve C-index, or the improvement is not driven by reserve variables.
- Inconclusive: the C-index difference is small and the bootstrap CI includes zero, or the cohort is too small for a stable estimate.

## Clinical adjudication and limitations
The design cannot establish causality. It can only test whether reserve adds prognostic information beyond lung function and illness severity. The result would not establish a mechanism or a treatment target. Clinical adjudication would require expert review of the cohort definition and the illness severity proxies.

The main limitation is that the illness severity proxies are imperfect. Length of stay and number of in-patient diagnoses may not fully capture the severity of the infection. A stronger study would use a validated severity score (e.g., APACHE or SOFA) if available.
