# Scientific Proposal: Dynamic Liver Function Reserve Depletion Patterns Predict HCC Treatment Response and Progression

## Unresolved Question

**Does the temporal pattern of pre-diagnosis liver function reserve depletion (characterized by the rate and trajectory of albumin and bilirubin decline from initial diagnosis) predict both disease progression patterns and differential treatment response in hepatocellular carcinoma, beyond what is captured by static composite scores like Child-Pugh or MELD?**

## Background and Rationale

### Clinical Context
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) management requires precise risk stratification to guide treatment decisions between curative options (resection, ablation, transplant) and palliative therapies (TACE, systemic therapy). Current practice relies heavily on composite scores like Child-Pugh and MELD, which integrate multiple parameters but may not capture the dynamic nature of liver function reserve during disease progression.

### Scientific Gap
While static assessments at diagnosis provide a snapshot of liver function, the **temporal evolution** of liver function markers may reveal critical information about:
1. The rate of functional decline preceding clinical deterioration
2. The "reserve capacity" that can be mobilized during treatment
3. The trajectory-specific predictors of treatment response

Existing literature focuses on baseline values or composite scores, with limited investigation into how the **pattern** of liver function depletion over time influences outcomes.

### Key References and Their Limitations

**[K1]** Teng et al. (2023) demonstrated that baseline ALBI grade (albumin-bilirubin composite score) predicts outcomes in regorafenib-treated HCC patients. However, this study only examines **static baseline measurements**, not the temporal dynamics of liver function changes.

**[K2]** Moore et al. (2025) reviewed different prognostic scores but did not investigate **longitudinal monitoring** or trajectory-based prediction methods, leaving a gap in understanding dynamic liver function patterns.

**[K3]** Gerstung et al. (2025) developed Delphi-2M, a transformer model that learns temporal disease progression patterns. This work provides a methodological precedent for learning temporal patterns from longitudinal health data, which can be adapted to liver function markers.

## Hypothesis

Patients whose pre-diagnosis liver function markers (albumin, bilirubin) show a **rapid decline trajectory** (high rate of change from initial to subsequent measurements) will:
1. Experience faster disease progression (earlier development of complications, more aggressive tumor growth)
2. Have reduced response to treatment, particularly to locoregional therapies like TACE
3. Exhibit different survival trajectories compared to patients with stable or slow-declining liver function

## Temporal Boundaries

- **Baseline**: Initial HCC diagnosis date (from diagnoses table)
- **Follow-up window**: 0-24 months from baseline
- **Key timepoints**:
  - 0-3 months: Early trajectory assessment
  - 3-6 months: Intermediate trajectory
  - 6-12 months: Mid-term progression
  - 12-24 months: Long-term outcomes

## Variables

### Primary Predictors (Dynamic Liver Function Reserve)
- **Albumin trajectory**: Rate of change (Δalbumin/Δtime) from baseline to 6 months
- **Bilirubin trajectory**: Rate of change (Δbilirubin/Δtime) from baseline to 6 months
- **Composite trajectory**: Combined trajectory score
- **Trajectory category**: Stable, declining, accelerating decline

### Outcome Variables
1. **Disease progression**: Time to first major complication (ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy) or tumor progression
2. **Treatment response**: Response rate to TACE or resection (complete, partial, no response)
3. **Survival**: Overall survival from diagnosis

### Covariates
- Age, gender
- Baseline albumin and bilirubin values
- Child-Pugh score at baseline
- Presence of cirrhosis
- Treatment modality received

## Baseline Methods

1. **Logistic Regression**: Predict treatment response based on baseline liver function markers
2. **Cox Proportional Hazards**: Time-to-event analysis for disease progression
3. **Linear Regression**: Predict survival time based on trajectory rates

These baselines establish the "simple" approach of using aggregate measures or static values.

## Alternative Methods

1. **Mixed-Effects Models**: Account for longitudinal correlation within patients, estimate individual trajectories
2. **Gradient Boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost)**: Capture non-linear relationships between trajectory patterns and outcomes
3. **Survival Analysis with Time-Varying Covariates**: Model how trajectory evolves and impacts time-to-event

The **substantive advance** over baselines:
- Baselines treat liver function as static or use simple averages
- Alternative methods capture the **temporal pattern** of decline, which may be more predictive
- Can distinguish between "slow steady decline" vs. "rapid acceleration" - patterns that may have different clinical implications

## Analysis Plan

### Step 1: Data Preparation
- Join diagnoses, labs, encounters, and procedures tables
- Filter for HCC patients with sufficient longitudinal data
- Calculate trajectory metrics (rate of change, acceleration)

### Step 2: Trajectory Classification
- Cluster patients by liver function trajectory patterns
- Define categories: stable, slow decline, moderate decline, rapid decline

### Step 3: Outcome Analysis
- Compare outcomes across trajectory categories
- Test for differences in treatment response, progression, and survival
- Adjust for confounders (age, baseline severity, treatment type)

### Step 4: Model Comparison
- Fit baseline models (logistic, Cox)
- Fit alternative models (mixed-effects, gradient boosting)
- Compare performance using AUC, C-index, calibration
- Assess whether trajectory patterns provide additional predictive value

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be **falsified** if:
1. No significant difference in outcomes across trajectory categories after adjustment for baseline severity
2. Static baseline values predict outcomes as well as or better than trajectory patterns
3. The temporal pattern of liver function change does not add predictive value beyond what is already captured by composite scores

## Clinical Importance

This research could advance clinical understanding by:
1. Identifying patients at risk of rapid deterioration based on early trajectory patterns
2. Informing treatment timing - aggressive treatment for rapid decliners vs. conservative management for stable patients
3. Personalizing treatment modality selection based on liver function dynamics
4. Potentially improving survival outcomes through earlier intervention in high-risk patients

## Missing Evidence Requiring Clinical Adjudication

1. **Treatment response definitions**: Need consensus on what constitutes "response" to TACE or resection in this dataset
2. **Outcome event definitions**: Need clinical adjudication to define major complications and progression
3. **Trajectory cutoff points**: Optimal thresholds for trajectory categories require validation
4. **Causal inference**: Observational design cannot establish causality; confounding by indication may bias results

## Methodological Considerations

- **Longitudinal data structure**: Must account for repeated measures within patients
- **Missing data**: Imputation strategy needed for missing laboratory values
- **Temporal confounding**: Treatments may affect liver function measurements
- **Selection bias**: Patients with frequent lab monitoring may differ systematically
