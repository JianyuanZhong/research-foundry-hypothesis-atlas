# Candidate proposal: timing of CRRT for severe ICU AKI

## Hypothesis and clinical importance
Among adults who develop objectively defined severe acute kidney injury (AKI) during an ICU stay and have not yet received renal replacement therapy (RRT), initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) within 6 hours of the first qualifying severe-AKI time (early strategy) reduces hospital mortality compared with a deferred strategy that initiates CRRT during hours 6–24, without an excess of adverse kidney-relevant outcomes. The competing clinical decision is whether to start CRRT promptly versus observe/support for a limited window; this is a management-strategy comparison, not a treatment-effect prediction model.

Existing evidence can support that CRRT is used for critically ill patients with severe AKI and that timing remains confounded by illness severity and indication; this database cannot establish that an observed timing association is causal. The unresolved, falsifiable claim is the contrast in outcomes under an explicit early-versus-deferred initiation policy.

## Target-trial emulation

### Population and time zero
Use the first ICU stay per hospital admission, age >=18 using `hosp/patients.anchor_age`, with ICU admission after hospital admission and at least 24 hours of observable ICU time. Exclude transfers/readmissions that have CRRT before the qualifying time, patients with CRRT already in progress at ICU entry, and stays with no adequate creatinine/urine observation. Define a qualifying severe-AKI time as the earliest hourly grid time in the first 48 ICU hours meeting KDIGO stage 2/3 from available data: either serum creatinine >=2.0 times a documented pre-AKI reference creatinine (minimum stable inpatient value before the rise, with a sensitivity analysis using the first measured value) or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for >=12 hours, with weight from `chartevents`/`inputevents` when available. The primary analysis should use a more reproducible creatinine-only severe-AKI definition if weight/urine completeness is inadequate, while reporting the urine-supported definition separately. Time zero is that first qualifying grid time, not CRRT start.

Require no CRRT or intermittent hemodialysis item before time zero in `procedureevents`. Exclude a clear emergent indication already documented at time zero only in a prespecified sensitivity analysis; do not use post-time-zero information to define eligibility. A practical primary cohort can require severe AKI by ICU hour 24 and define time zero at hour 24, which increases synchronized baseline measurement and avoids selecting only patients who survive to a later landmark; the preferred dynamic analysis instead uses each first qualifying hourly time and clones.

### Strategies, grace period, and estimand
Compare two feasible policies over a 24-hour window after time zero.

1. Early CRRT: start item 225802, 225803, or 225809 within 0–6 hours.
2. Deferred CRRT: remain free of CRRT through the first 6 hours, then start one of those CRRT items during hours 6–24 (or remain untreated through 24 hours, depending on the prespecified per-protocol version).

Primary estimand is the intention-to-treat-like 24-hour policy contrast on hospital mortality, assigning strategy at time zero and preserving deviations. Secondary per-protocol effect uses clone-censor-weighting: clone each eligible patient into both policies, censor a clone at first protocol deviation, and fit inverse-probability-of-censoring weights from measured baseline and hourly covariates. Because deferred treatment is not simply “never treated,” report separate contrasts for early versus deferred initiation and early versus no CRRT through 24h; do not conflate them.

### Outcomes and follow-up
Primary outcome: `hosp/admissions.hospital_expire_flag` by hospital discharge, with `deathtime` as the event time when present. Secondary outcomes: ICU mortality (death time before `icustays.outtime`), time to live hospital discharge with death as a competing event, ICU length of stay (`outtime-intime`), and 28-day mortality only if the deidentified timestamps and date-of-death linkage can be validated. If estimating kidney recovery, use no dialysis procedure and discharge creatinine only as exploratory outcomes; absence of a recorded procedure is not proof of renal recovery. Do not call discharge destination a clinical recovery endpoint.

### Confounding and analysis
Construct covariates using only information at or before time zero: age/sex, admission type and source, first-care-unit, chronic kidney disease and major comorbidities from diagnoses, baseline creatinine and trajectory, urine output trajectory, blood pressure/MAP, lactate, pH/bicarbonate, potassium, oxygenation/ventilation status, vasopressor and fluid exposure, mechanical ventilation, sepsis/shock proxies, and hourly SOFA-like organ dysfunction. Use only item IDs whose labels are verified in `d_items`/`d_labitems`; retain missingness indicators and clinically sensible last-observation-carried-forward windows for measurements, with multiple imputation or weighted missingness sensitivity analysis. Estimate marginal risk differences and risk ratios at discharge using pooled logistic models or weighted survival models with robust standard errors, hospital admission/ICU stay clustering where applicable, and stabilized treatment/censoring weights truncated at prespecified percentiles. Check overlap; report the target population after excluding non-overlap rather than silently extrapolating.

The key time-varying confounders are rapidly changing hemodynamics, potassium/acidosis, urine output, and ventilatory/vasopressor support. A baseline-only regression is inadequate for the per-protocol estimand. The analysis must not adjust for post-CRRT mediators such as subsequent fluid removal, later creatinine, or post-treatment lactate in the primary model.

### Immortal-time and competing-risk safeguards
Time zero precedes treatment. Do not define the cohort by surviving to CRRT or by having a later procedure. For the 6-hour early strategy, all patients enter at the qualifying time; deferred-policy clones are censored only at a defined protocol deviation, with weights estimated conditional on being alive/under observation. Report hospital death as a risk by discharge and discharge alive as a competing event; do not treat ICU transfer or discharge as ordinary censoring without sensitivity analysis. Deduplicate overlapping CRRT procedure intervals by stay/order and define initiation as the earliest valid start time.

## Falsification and robustness
- Negative-control outcome: a pre-time-zero or early post-time-zero administrative event not plausibly altered by CRRT, such as already-documented historical diagnosis-code composition; this is mainly a data/selection check, not a causal endpoint.
- Negative-control exposure: timing of a procedure unrelated to renal replacement among the same severe-AKI cohort, selected before looking at results and matched on observation intensity; a strong “effect” suggests residual severity/measurement bias.
- Timing placebo: shift the assigned CRRT initiation window backward to a period before qualifying severe AKI; no association should appear if time alignment and confounding control are adequate.
- Compare CRRT item 225802-only with inclusion of CVVHD/CVVHDF; test alternative AKI definitions, 6/12-hour early windows, 12/24-hour deferral, first versus all ICU stays, and weight/urine completeness restrictions.
- Quantify E-values/sensitivity to unmeasured confounding, weight distributions, positivity violations, and missingness patterns. A falsification failure, extreme weights, or materially different estimates across plausible AKI definitions should prevent a strong causal interpretation.

## Interpretation limits
Supportive results would be a clinically meaningful, overlap-supported reduction in adjusted hospital mortality under early CRRT, stable across time-zero/AKI/item definitions, with no comparable placebo or negative-control association. They would motivate prospective pragmatic testing, not prove benefit: indication confounding, undocumented goals of care, outside-hospital dialysis, clinician intent, and treatment complications are incompletely captured.

Adverse results (higher mortality or harm with early CRRT) would challenge routine acceleration but could reflect residual confounding by emergent illness. Null or unstable results would mean the MIMIC observational data do not identify a reliable timing benefit, not equivalence. Clinical adjudication is required for urgent hyperkalemia, refractory acidosis, pulmonary edema, hemodynamic intolerance, dialysis dependence, and cause-specific complications; MIMIC lacks randomization, reliable clinician intent, complete pre-ICU renal history, exact CRRT dose delivered, and adjudicated indications. A prospective trial or external validation is needed for treatment policy claims.
