# Sustained Hyperoxia and ICU Mortality: Does the Risk Differ by Pulmonary Status?

## A Falsifiable, Data-Bound Hypothesis for eICU-CRD (evolved from expert seed eicu-04)

---

### 1. Hypothesis

**Leading claim (to be tested):** In ICU patients with adequate oxygenation (SpO2 ≥ 95% or PaO2 ≥ 80 mmHg), sustained hyperoxia (FiO2 ≥ 60% for ≥ 25% of ICU stay) is associated with increased ICU mortality, and this association is stronger in patients with pulmonary admission diagnoses (pneumonia, ARDS, COPD, asthma) than in patients with non-pulmonary diagnoses.

**Strongest rival:** The association between hyperoxia and mortality is entirely explained by illness severity (sicker patients receive higher FiO2 and have worse outcomes), with no independent effect of oxygen exposure. Alternatively, the effect is uniform across pulmonary and non-pulmonary patients (no heterogeneity of treatment effect).

---

### 2. Scientific Opening

**Established:**
- Hyperoxia (supra-atmospheric oxygen) causes oxidative stress, inflammation, and alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction in preclinical models [K1].
- Multiple RCTs comparing conservative vs. liberal oxygen targets in mechanically ventilated ICU patients show no significant difference in 90-day mortality (OXY-BREATHES meta-analysis: RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.94-1.09; 9 RCTs, 20,447 patients) [K3].
- Subgroup analyses hint at differential effects: conservative targets yielded more vasopressor-free days in septic patients (MD 2.0, p=0.008) and potential survival benefit in post-cardiac arrest (RR 0.89, p=0.05) [K3].

**Unresolved:**
- Whether the null RCT results reflect a true absence of harm or heterogeneity of treatment effect (HTE) that is masked in pooled analyses [K2].
- Whether the risk of hyperoxia is concentrated in specific patient subpopulations (e.g., pulmonary disease) as suggested by mechanistic evidence [K1].
- Whether DURATION of hyperoxia exposure (not just peak FiO2) matters for outcomes.

**The gap:** No large observational study has tested whether the association between sustained hyperoxia and mortality differs by pulmonary status in a multi-hospital ICU cohort, using time-weighted FiO2 exposure rather than a single measurement or binary randomization.

---

### 3. Clinical Importance

Oxygen therapy is the most commonly administered intervention in the ICU. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign and other guidelines recommend targeting SpO2 92-96%, but in practice, many patients receive FiO2 > 60% for prolonged periods. If hyperoxia harm is concentrated in pulmonary patients, this would:
1. Support a lower oxygen threshold for patients with primary lung disease
2. Explain why pooled RCTs show null results (dilution by non-pulmonary patients)
3. Inform individualized oxygen therapy strategies [K2]

### 5. Analysis Plan

#### 5.1 Population
- ICU stays with ≥ 1 FiO2 measurement in `respiratoryCharting` AND ≥ 1 SpO2 measurement in `vitalPeriodic` AND known `unitdischargestatus` (Alive or Expired).
- Exclude stays < 24 hours (`unitdischargeoffset` < 24).
- Expected analytic cohort: ~50,000-70,000 patients (intersection of FiO2 and SpO2 availability).

#### 5.2 Exposure
- **Primary**: Fraction of ICU stay (hours) with FiO2 ≥ 60%. Computed by:
  1. Extract all FiO2 rows (valuelabel IN ["FiO2", "FIO2 (%)", "Set Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FIO2)"])
  2. Clean: exclude values < 0 or > 100 (outliers)
  3. For each patient, compute `fio2_high_fraction` = (hours with FiO2 ≥ 60) / (total ICU stay hours)
  4. "Sustained hyperoxia" indicator: `fio2_high_fraction` ≥ 0.25
- **Secondary**: Time-weighted mean FiO2 (continuous), peak FiO2, FiO2 at 24h.
- **Oxygenation adequacy check**: Mean SpO2 ≥ 95% or mean PaO2 ≥ 80 mmHg (from lab).

#### 5.3 Outcome
- ICU mortality: `unitdischargestatus` = "Expired".

#### 5.4 Stratification
- **Pulmonary**: Any ICD9 code with prefix in {486, 482, 481, 491, 492, 493, 494, 496, 512, 513, 518, 519, 428} in `diagnosis.icd9code`.
- **Non-pulmonary**: All other patients.

#### 5.5 Models

**Model 1 (Baseline):** Multilevel logistic regression (hospital random intercept via `hospitalid`)
- Covariates: age, gender, APACHE II score, ICU length of stay (hours), vasopressor use (binary: any norepinephrine/epinephrine/vasopressin/dopamine/phenylephrine in `infusionDrug`)

**Model 2 (Alternative):** Model 1 + `fio2_high_fraction` (continuous) + `sustained_hyperoxia` (binary)

**Model 3 (Interaction):** Model 2 + `fio2_high_fraction × pulmonary_status` interaction term

**Model comparison:**
- Likelihood ratio test (Model 1 vs 2, Model 2 vs 3)
- AIC, BIC
- Marginal R² (proportion of variance explained by fixed effects)

#### 5.6 Sensitivity Analyses
1. Restrict to mechanically ventilated patients (FiO2 > 21% at admission)
2. Use ABG PaO2/FiO2 (P/F ratio) as alternative oxygenation measure
3. Restrict to patients with adequate oxygenation (mean SpO2 ≥ 95%)
4. Exclude first 24h of ICU stay (to reduce confounding by indication at admission)
5. Use FiO2 from `lab` table (ABG FiO2) as alternative exposure source

#### 5.7 Falsification Criteria

| Result | Interpretation |
|--------|---------------|
| **Supportive** | `fio2_high_fraction` OR > 1.05 per 0.1 increase (95% CI excluding 1.0), interaction p < 0.05, pulmonary patients show stronger association (OR_pulm > OR_nonpulp) |
| **Adverse** | `fio2_high_fraction` OR 0.95-1.05 (95% CI including 1.0), no significant interaction (p > 0.10) |
| **Inconclusive** | Weak association (OR 1.01-1.05) without clear interaction pattern; or significant main effect but no heterogeneity |

### 6. Method Comparison: Baseline vs Learned Alternative

**Baseline (Model 1-3 above):** Multilevel logistic regression with linear FiO2 terms.
- Strengths: Interpretable coefficients, clear interaction tests, handles hospital clustering
- Limitations: Assumes linear dose-response; cannot capture threshold effects or non-monotonic relationships

**Learned alternative (XGBoost):**
- Features: `fio2_high_fraction`, `sustained_hyperoxia`, time-weighted mean FiO2, peak FiO2, FiO2 at 24h/48h/72h, age, gender, APACHE II, ICU LOS, vasopressor use, pulmonary status
- Hospital-level cross-validation (split by hospital to avoid leakage)
- Evaluation: AUROC, calibration slope, Brier score; SHAP values for feature importance

**What the learned model reveals that the linear model loses:**
1. **Non-linear dose-response**: XGBoost can detect threshold effects (e.g., harm only above FiO2 ≥ 70% for > 50% of stay) that a linear term misses
2. **Higher-order interactions**: Automatic detection of FiO2 × APACHE × pulmonary status interactions
3. **Temporal patterns**: If FiO2 trajectory features (early vs late hyperoxia) are included, the model can distinguish timing effects

**Selection rationale:** The linear model is the primary analysis (interpretable, testable hypothesis). XGBoost is a sensitivity analysis to check whether the linear model's null result is due to functional form misspecification. If XGBoost shows strong FiO2 features in SHAP but the linear model is null, this suggests non-linear effects warranting further investigation.

**Resource estimate:** XGBoost on ~60,000 patients × 20 features: < 5 minutes on CPU. Multilevel logistic regression: < 2 minutes. Total analysis: < 30 minutes including data extraction.

### 8. Limitations

1. **Observational design**: Cannot establish causality. FiO2 is a treatment, not a randomization; confounding by indication (sicker patients get higher FiO2) is the primary threat. APACHE II and vasopressor adjustment mitigate but do not eliminate this.
2. **Recording density**: FiO2 is recorded at irregular intervals; time-weighted interpolation assumes constant FiO2 between measurements.
3. **SpO2 ≠ PaO2**: SpO2 near 100% cannot reliably quantify hyperoxemia (expert seed caution). We use FiO2 as primary exposure and ABG PaO2 for validation.
4. **Single time period**: eICU-CRD spans 2014-2015; oxygen practices may have changed since.
5. **No continuous arterial line**: FiO2 in respiratoryCharting reflects ventilator settings, not actual alveolar oxygen.
6. **ICD coding**: Pulmonary classification based on ICD9 codes may misclassify some patients.
7. **No external validation**: Single dataset; findings require replication.

### 9. Interpretation of Possible Results

**If supportive (hyperoxia harm concentrated in pulmonary patients):**
- Supports the HTE hypothesis [K2] and mechanistic predictions [K1]
- Would justify a prospective trial with pulmonary-specific oxygen targets
- Does NOT establish causality; requires RCT confirmation
- Would explain why pooled RCTs show null results (dilution by non-pulmonary patients)

**If adverse (no association, no interaction):**
- Suggests hyperoxia at these levels/durations is not independently harmful in this population
- Consistent with OXY-BREATHES null result [K3]
- Would argue against restrictive oxygen strategies in the general ICU
- Does not rule out harm at higher FiO2 (> 80%) or longer durations

**If inconclusive (weak association, no clear pattern):**
- May reflect insufficient power, recording density limitations, or true heterogeneity
- Would warrant a larger study or different exposure definition
- Does not support or refute the HTE hypothesis

**Stronger conclusions requiring additional data:**
- Causal claims require RCT
- Dose-response shape requires continuous arterial monitoring
- Generalizability requires external validation in MIMIC or other datasets
- Mechanistic confirmation requires biomarker data (oxidative stress markers)
