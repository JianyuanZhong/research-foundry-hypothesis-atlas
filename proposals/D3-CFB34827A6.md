# State-specific metabolic reprogramming in treatment-resistant RA macrophages: a 5-condition gradient residual analysis

**Child of [prior hypothesis]** (D3-M3, E-MTAB-8322).
**Status: PLANNED analysis. A bounded feasibility diagnostic (gene mapping) was executed ([research job], [research job], [research job]); the full inferential analysis has NOT been executed.**

## Scientific opening

The parent candidate (candidate-2edf24fb, top of board, mu 33.69) asks whether treatment-resistant RA synovial macrophages carry a glycolytic/HIF-1α metabolic program that distinguishes them from remission RA. Its analysis plan — pathway-level comparison plus an all-genes vs inflammatory-only ML classifier — tests **separability**: can the groups be told apart, and do metabolic genes add discriminative information?

Its child (candidate-54d03f92, selected in ep5) refined this to a **residual analysis** testing whether metabolic gene expression in resistant macrophages exceeds what is predicted by their inflammatory state. However, that analysis used only the **binary resistant-vs-remission contrast** (n=13 donors), which has limited statistical power and cannot distinguish between:
1. A **distinct metabolic program** that is specifically upregulated in resistant disease (state-specific), vs.
2. A **monotonic metabolic response** that scales with disease severity across the full spectrum (HC < naive < UPA < remission < resistant).

The full 5-condition gradient in E-MTAB-8322 (n=25 independent donors) provides a more powerful and mechanistically informative test. If metabolic reprogramming is a **distinct regulatory program** that is specifically engaged in resistant disease, then:
- Resistant macrophages should show **positive metabolic residuals** (metabolic expression above what is predicted by their inflammatory expression) that are **not shared** by other conditions.
- UPA (undifferentiated arthritis, a transitional state between naive RA and established RA) should show **intermediate** metabolic residuals, reflecting partial engagement of the metabolic program.
- The residual pattern should **not** follow a simple monotonic gradient across all 5 conditions.

If metabolic reprogramming is merely a **scaled version of inflammatory activation**, then:
- Metabolic expression should be **fully predicted** by inflammatory expression (zero residuals across all conditions).
- Any condition differences in metabolic expression should be **proportional** to differences in inflammatory expression.
- The residual pattern should show **no condition-specific elevation**.

## Hypothesis

**The glycolytic/HIF-1α metabolic program in treatment-resistant RA synovial macrophages is a STATE-SPECIFIC regulatory program that persists independent of inflammatory activation.**

Specifically:
1. **Primary test**: After regressing per-donor metabolic score on per-donor inflammatory score (pooled OLS, n=25 donors), resistant RA macrophages will show **significantly elevated positive residuals** compared to all other conditions (HC, naive RA, UPA, remission RA).
2. **Secondary test (disease progression gradient)**: The residual pattern will **not** follow a simple monotonic gradient across the 5 conditions. Instead, resistant RA will show a **discontinuous jump** in metabolic residuals relative to remission RA, with UPA showing intermediate values.
3. **Tertiary test (condition-specificity)**: A model including condition as a covariate (metabolic ~ inflammatory + condition) will show that **condition adds significant explanatory power** beyond inflammatory score alone, specifically for the resistant RA group.

**Falsification**: If metabolic residuals are not significantly elevated in resistant RA (p > 0.05 after BH correction), or if the residual pattern follows a monotonic gradient without a discontinuous jump, or if condition does not add explanatory power beyond inflammation, the hypothesis is falsified.

## Clinical importance

Approximately 30% of RA patients fail to achieve remission on current DMARDs and biologics [K1]. The "difficult-to-treat" phenotype is defined by the EULAR, and understanding its mechanism is critical for developing new therapeutic strategies [K1]. Macrophage metabolic reprogramming has been identified as a key driver of RA pathogenesis, with glycolytic shift and HIF-1α activation promoting IL-1β production and sustained inflammation [K2, K3].

If treatment-resistant RA macrophages have a **distinct metabolic program** (state-specific), this would:
1. **Identify a mechanistic target** for converting resistant to remission (e.g., metabolic inhibitors that specifically target the resistant-state metabolic program without affecting the inflammatory program).
2. **Provide a biomarker** for predicting treatment response (metabolic residuals could be measured from synovial biopsies to identify patients likely to fail anti-inflammatory therapy).
3. **Explain why anti-inflammatory therapies alone fail** in resistant disease (the metabolic program is self-sustaining and not simply proportional to inflammation).

If metabolic reprogramming is merely **scaled inflammation**, then:
1. Metabolic interventions would only be effective if they also reduce inflammation.
2. There would be no distinct biomarker for treatment resistance beyond inflammatory markers.
3. The therapeutic strategy would remain "suppress inflammation more aggressively."

The distinction between these two scenarios has **direct therapeutic implications** for the design of combination therapies in difficult-to-treat RA.

## Analysis plan

### Step 1: Data preparation
1. Load counts matrix (33,694 genes × 27 samples) from `E-MTAB-8322.counts.tsv.gz`.
2. Load design file `E-MTAB-8322.design.json` to get condition assignments.
3. Exclude SA149P and UPASA149 (unresolved relatedness), leaving 25 independent donors.
4. Verify all 53 ENSEMBL IDs from the gene sets are present in the 33,694 gene set.
5. Compute log2(count + 1) for all genes.

### Step 2: Per-donor scoring
1. **Metabolic score**: For each donor, compute the mean log2(count + 1) across all 28 genes in the metabolic gene set.
2. **Inflammatory score**: For each donor, compute the mean log2(count + 1) across all 25 genes in the inflammatory gene set.

### Step 3: Pooled OLS regression (primary test)
1. Fit a linear regression: `metabolic_score ~ inflammatory_score` (n=25 donors).
2. Compute per-donor residuals: `residual_i = metabolic_score_i - predicted_metabolic_score_i`.
3. **Key test**: Are residuals significantly elevated in resistant RA compared to other conditions?
   - **Kruskal-Wallis test**: Test whether residuals differ across the 5 conditions (HC, naive, UPA, remission, resistant).
   - **Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests**: Compare resistant vs each other condition (resistant vs HC, resistant vs naive, resistant vs UPA, resistant vs remission).
   - **Multiple correction**: Apply Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction to the 4 pairwise p-values.
   - **Effect size**: Report rank-biserial correlation for each pairwise comparison.

### Step 4: Disease progression gradient (secondary test)
1. Order the 5 conditions by expected disease severity: HC < naive < UPA < remission < resistant.
2. Test whether the mean residual follows this monotonic gradient:
   - **Spearman correlation**: Correlate condition rank (1-5) with residual (n=25 donors).
   - **Jonckheere-Terpstra test**: Test for a monotonic trend in residuals across the 5 conditions.
   - **Discontinuity test**: Compare the residual difference between resistant and remission to the residual difference between remission and UPA. If the resistant-remission jump is significantly larger than the remission-UPA difference, this supports a discontinuous (state-specific) pattern rather than a monotonic gradient.

### Step 5: Condition-specificity (tertiary test)
1. Fit an extended model: `metabolic_score ~ inflammatory_score + condition` (n=25 donors, condition as a categorical variable with 5 levels).
2. Test whether condition adds significant explanatory power beyond inflammatory score:
   - **F-test**: Compare the R² of the full model (inflammatory + condition) to the reduced model (inflammatory only).
   - **Coefficient test**: Test whether the resistant RA condition coefficient is significantly different from zero (after controlling for inflammatory score).
3. If condition adds significant explanatory power, this supports the hypothesis that the metabolic program is not fully predicted by inflammation alone.

### Step 6: Sensitivity analysis (alternative method)
1. **Per-gene regression with donor fixed effects**: For each gene in the metabolic set, fit a mixed-effects model: `expression ~ inflammatory_score + (1 | donor)`.
2. Compute per-gene residuals and test whether the pattern of gene-specific residuals is enriched in resistant RA.
3. **What this reveals**: The baseline (per-donor score regression) loses gene-specific information. The alternative reveals whether specific metabolic genes (e.g., HIF1A, VEGFA, PDK1) drive the resistant-state residual pattern, or whether the effect is distributed across the entire metabolic program. It also controls for donor-specific baseline metabolic set points.

### Statistical power
- Primary test (resistant vs all others): n=6 resistant vs n=19 others. With a medium effect size (rank-biserial r=0.5), power is ~0.60 at α=0.05.
- Pairwise tests (resistant vs each condition): n=6 vs n=4-7. Power is limited (~0.30-0.50 for medium effects).
- The 5-condition gradient (n=25) provides better power for the Kruskal-Wallis test than the binary contrast (n=13).

## Falsification criteria

### Supportive results
- Resistant RA shows significantly elevated positive metabolic residuals compared to all other conditions (p < 0.05 after BH correction for at least 3 of 4 pairwise comparisons).
- The residual pattern does NOT follow a monotonic gradient (Jonckheere-Terpstra p > 0.05, or the resistant-remission jump is significantly larger than the remission-UPA difference).
- Condition adds significant explanatory power beyond inflammatory score (F-test p < 0.05).
- **Interpretation**: The metabolic program is a state-specific regulatory program that is specifically engaged in resistant disease, independent of inflammatory activation. This supports the hypothesis and suggests that metabolic interventions could be effective in resistant RA even if inflammation is not fully suppressed.

### Adverse results
- No significant difference in metabolic residuals between resistant RA and other conditions (all pairwise p > 0.05 after BH correction).
- The residual pattern follows a monotonic gradient (Jonckheere-Terpstra p < 0.05, with no discontinuous jump).
- Condition does NOT add significant explanatory power beyond inflammatory score (F-test p > 0.05).
- **Interpretation**: The metabolic program is a scaled version of inflammatory activation, not a distinct regulatory program. This falsifies the hypothesis and suggests that metabolic interventions would only be effective if they also reduce inflammation.

### Inconclusive results
- Small sample size (n=6 resistant) leads to wide confidence intervals and low power.
- The metabolic and inflammatory scores are too highly correlated (r > 0.9) to separate residuals.
- The UPA group has only 2 independent donors, limiting the intermediate-state test.
- **Interpretation**: The analysis is underpowered to distinguish between the distinct-program and scaled-inflammation hypotheses. Additional donors or a different experimental design (e.g., longitudinal sampling, single-cell RNA-seq) would be needed.

## Competing explanations and how the design addresses them

1. **Inflammatory confounding**: The primary test (residual analysis) directly controls for inflammatory score by regressing metabolic on inflammatory. If the metabolic difference is fully explained by inflammation, residuals will be zero.

2. **Cell-type composition**: Pseudobulk data cannot separate cell-type composition effects from within-cell metabolic changes. This is a limitation, not a competing explanation that the design can address. The design assumes that the macrophage population is relatively homogeneous across conditions.

3. **Treatment effects**: Resistant patients may have received different drugs that alter macrophage metabolism independently of disease state. The design cannot address this limitation (no treatment history data). However, the UPA group (which has not received treatment) provides a natural control for treatment effects: if UPA shows intermediate metabolic residuals, this suggests that the metabolic program is driven by disease state rather than treatment.

4. **Age/disease duration confounding**: Resistant patients may be older or have longer disease duration. The design cannot address this limitation (no age/duration data in the design file). However, the 5-condition gradient provides a natural control: if the metabolic residual pattern is specific to resistant disease rather than increasing with age/duration, this supports the state-specific hypothesis.

5. **Small sample size**: The design uses the full 5-condition gradient (n=25 donors) to maximize statistical power. The primary test (resistant vs all others) has n=6 vs n=19, which is more powerful than the binary contrast (n=6 vs n=7).

6. **Pseudobulk averaging**: Pseudobulk data cannot resolve cell-to-cell heterogeneity in metabolic state. This is a limitation, not a competing explanation. The sensitivity analysis (per-gene regression with donor fixed effects) partially addresses this by controlling for donor-specific baseline.

## Limitations

1. **Cross-sectional design**: Cannot establish longitudinal transitions from resistant to remission. The 5-condition gradient is a cross-sectional snapshot, not a time course.
2. **Pseudobulk data**: Cannot separate cell-type composition effects from within-cell metabolic changes.
3. **Small sample size**: n=4-7 per condition limits statistical power, especially for the UPA intermediate-state test (n=2 independent donors).
4. **No treatment history data**: Cannot control for specific drugs received by resistant patients.
5. **No clinical outcome data**: Cannot determine if "resistant" patients eventually achieved remission.
6. **Gene set definition**: The metabolic and inflammatory gene sets are predefined based on literature, not derived from the data. This is a strength (hypothesis-driven) but also a limitation (may miss novel metabolic genes).
7. **UPA group**: Only 2 independent donors after exclusion, limiting the intermediate-state test.

## Method comparison

### Baseline: Per-donor score regression
- **Method**: Pooled OLS regression of metabolic score on inflammatory score (n=25 donors).
- **Strengths**: Simple, interpretable, directly tests the distinct-program vs scaled-inflammation question.
- **Weaknesses**: Loses gene-specific information, assumes all metabolic genes contribute equally to the metabolic score.

### Alternative: Per-gene regression with donor fixed effects
- **Method**: For each gene in the metabolic set, fit a mixed-effects model: `expression ~ inflammatory_score + (1 | donor)`.
- **Strengths**: Reveals gene-specific residual patterns, controls for donor-specific baseline metabolic set points.
- **Weaknesses**: More complex, requires multiple testing correction, may be underpowered for individual genes.

### What the alternative reveals that the baseline loses
1. **Gene-specific residual patterns**: The baseline aggregates all metabolic genes into a single score, losing information about which specific genes drive the resistant-state residual pattern. The alternative reveals whether specific genes (e.g., HIF1A, VEGFA, PDK1) are responsible for the metabolic residual elevation.
2. **Donor-specific metabolic set points**: The baseline assumes that all donors have the same baseline metabolic set point. The alternative controls for donor-specific baseline, revealing whether the resistant-state metabolic program is a shift from the donor's own baseline or an absolute elevation.

### Selection
The baseline is sufficient for the primary test (distinct-program vs scaled-inflammation). The alternative is a sensitivity analysis that provides additional mechanistic insight but is not required for the primary hypothesis test.
