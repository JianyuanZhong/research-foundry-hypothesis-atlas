# Provisional HCC hypothesis: post-hepatectomy laboratory trajectory and 30-day deterioration

Status: provisional, episode 4; this is a hypothesis and experiment design, not a completed analysis or clinical conclusion.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a recorded liver resection in the HCC snapshot, the early post-operative trajectory of liver-related laboratory abnormalities will be associated with a clinically important deterioration signal during the subsequent 30 days, beyond baseline disease severity and operative context. The unresolved claim is whether a reproducible trajectory, rather than a single post-operative value, identifies a distinct recovery/deterioration pattern that could inform surveillance or escalation research.

This is not yet established. The HCC catalog and prior source audit support the existence of longitudinal procedure, laboratory, encounter, diagnosis, medication, examination, pathology, and clinical-document data, but they do not establish the proposed association or a causal mechanism.

## Clinical importance and advance

A positive result would identify whether early post-operative laboratory dynamics contain information beyond a baseline or single post-operative measurement, potentially supporting a prospective study of surveillance or escalation after liver resection. A null or adverse result would argue against treating the trajectory as a distinct warning signal and would prevent an overinterpretation of routine post-operative laboratory variation. The substantive advance is a falsifiable comparison between a simple severity baseline and a trajectory-based signal, with explicit temporal boundaries and falsification criteria.

## What is observed versus what remains unproven

Observed from the dataset catalog/prior audit: the HCC snapshot contains 48,925 resection rows and 24,712 patients in the prior recorded audit; bilirubin, INR, ALT, and albumin are available; 3,135 procedure start times were missing in that audit; laboratory units were unavailable; and some clinical content (including validated diagnosis extraction, images, waveforms, and complete front-page/transfer content) is not available. These are data-availability observations, not evidence for the hypothesis.

Unproven: the exact procedure-label cohort, assay labels/units and timestamp semantics, the operational deterioration endpoint, the association, and any clinical utility. No result has been computed in this episode.

## Proposed computable experiment

Population: adults in the HCC snapshot with a qualifying liver-resection procedure record, using the documented HCC procedure source and patient/encounter identifiers. The exact procedure-label rule and exclusion criteria must be frozen after schema/row inspection; do not infer a procedure from an unvalidated label.

Temporal boundaries: index time is the procedure start time when available. Use pre-index baseline values and early post-index laboratory values only through a prespecified window (for example, post-index days 0–7), and evaluate a prespecified 30-day post-index deterioration window. Exclude or separately report records with missing/unreliable index time rather than silently assigning them.

Variables: baseline liver-related laboratory values and available severity/operative context; early trajectory features for bilirubin, INR, ALT, and albumin (for example, slope, change from baseline, minimum/maximum and timing), with assay-specific handling only after units and labels are verified. The outcome is a prespecified deterioration signal in the 30-day window, to be defined from available encounters/procedures/laboratory/clinical-document fields and frozen before outcome analysis.

Baselines and alternatives: fit a simple baseline using baseline severity and a single early post-operative measurement, with an appropriate regression or survival model and uncertainty estimates. Compare it with a trajectory model using the prespecified early laboratory trajectory features, fitted on the same cohort and split. The trajectory model is scientifically substantive only if it tests whether temporal information adds predictive/explanatory information beyond current severity; it is not justified merely for a small predictive gain. A learned sequence model may be considered only if the available timestamp density and units make it interpretable and feasible; otherwise defer it and record the dependency.

Split and evaluation: use a temporal split by index time, with no patient appearing in both training and test sets. Report calibration, discrimination and uncertainty where appropriate, plus prespecified trajectory-stratum estimates and calibration checks. The primary scientific comparison is baseline versus trajectory, not model complexity.

Falsification and interpretation: supportive evidence requires a reproducible trajectory association with the prespecified endpoint beyond the baseline, stable under the temporal split and sensitivity analyses, with uncertainty reported. Adverse evidence is a null or reversed association, instability across strata, or failure to improve the prespecified comparison; this would weaken the trajectory claim. Inconclusive evidence includes insufficient timestamp/assay validity, sparse outcome events, wide uncertainty, or inability to define the endpoint without clinical adjudication. None of these outcomes establishes causality or clinical utility.

The actual scientific deliverable is a fitted baseline-versus-trajectory comparison on the frozen HCC cohort, with temporal-split estimates, uncertainty, and a conclusion tied to computed outputs. If the assay units/timestamps cannot be verified, the correct deliverable is an honest feasibility/inconclusive report, not an invented model result.
