# Earlier natural menopause and cardiac structural remodelling beyond measured haemodynamic load

## Scientific question and clinical importance

Among UK Biobank women with self-reported natural menopause, does earlier age at menopause remain associated with left-ventricular myocardial mass and left-atrial structure/function after adjustment for age, body composition, and measured blood-pressure load?

The clinically important unresolved question is whether menopause timing marks a subgroup with earlier subclinical cardiac structural change, or whether the apparent association is explained by residual haemodynamic load and shared metabolic ageing. This matters because menopause timing itself is not modifiable, but if it identifies a subgroup with earlier cardiac remodelling, it could motivate earlier cardiovascular surveillance and risk-factor control.

The leading explanation is that earlier natural menopause is associated with higher cumulative blood pressure and/or adverse body composition, which in turn drives left-ventricular hypertrophy and left-atrial remodelling. The strongest rival is a sex-hormone-related biological transition: loss of oestrogen may independently alter cardiac structure. The available UKB data can test the first explanation using measured blood pressure and body composition, but cannot establish a hormonal mechanism without longitudinal hormone measurements or a randomized intervention.

## Evidence and closest prior work

[K1] Menopause and cardiovascular structure/function literature supports that menopause timing and postmenopausal status are associated with adverse cardiovascular phenotypes, but observational adjustment for blood pressure and body composition is needed to separate timing from haemodynamic load. [K2] Cardiac MRI studies establish that myocardial mass and left-atrial dimensions/volumes are clinically meaningful structural measures of subclinical cardiac remodelling. [K3] Prediction and adjustment methods literature supports comparing a transparent linear adjustment hierarchy with a held-out nonlinear alternative, while requiring stable feature effects before interpreting an association as explanatory.

The proposal adds a prespecified UKB estimand for a 5-year earlier menopause, an explicit Model 0–4 adjustment hierarchy, exact cardiac MRI and covariate bindings, and a held-out gradient-boosted comparison. It does not claim that menopause causes remodelling or that the association is mediated by oestrogen.

## Population, timing and variables

Use UK Biobank participants with female sex, self-reported natural menopause, a plausible menopause age, and cardiac MRI. Exclude participants with missing menopause age, missing cardiac MRI outcome, or implausible age at recruitment. Define baseline as the assessment-centre visit and derive all covariates from fields recorded at or before that visit. Do not use future diagnosis or treatment fields as covariates.

Required bindings are the UKB/ukb672073 split tables and the prepared Parquet views described in datasets/README.md: participant identifier `eid`; sex and age fields; menopause status and age-at-menopause fields; body-composition fields including BMI, waist circumference and body-fat measurements; measured systolic and diastolic blood pressure and antihypertensive medication fields; cardiac MRI left-ventricular mass, left-ventricular volume, left-atrial volume and left-atrial function fields where available. Join only on `eid` within the same UKB identifier namespace. Preserve the source-specific identity and do not join UKB identifiers to other datasets.

The primary exposure is age at natural menopause, standardised as a 5-year earlier-menopause contrast. The primary outcomes are left-ventricular myocardial mass indexed to body size and left-atrial volume indexed to body size; secondary outcomes include left-ventricular ejection fraction and other available left-atrial function measures.

## Analysis and competing explanation

Fit a transparent linear Model 0 with age only, then add body composition, measured blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment, and remaining prespecified demographic/lifestyle covariates in Models 1–4. Report the 5-year earlier-menopause coefficient, 95% confidence interval, and change from Model 0 to the fully adjusted model. Use missingness indicators and multiple imputation only for covariates with reasonable observed support; report complete-case sensitivity analysis.

The competing explanation is tested directly: if the association is materially attenuated after adding measured blood pressure and body composition, the data support residual haemodynamic/metabolic explanation rather than an independent menopause-timing signal. A persistent association does not establish hormonal mediation.

## Baseline and learned alternative

The baseline is the prespecified linear adjustment hierarchy. The substantive alternative is a gradient-boosted tree model fitted on the same covariates and outcome, with a held-out split and no leakage from future fields. Compare held-out R-squared improvement over the linear baseline and inspect partial dependence for menopause age. The alternative can reveal nonlinear or interaction effects that a linear coefficient compresses, but a gain without stable partial dependence is predictive only, not evidence for the explanatory claim.

## Interpretation and falsification

Supportive: the fully adjusted coefficient remains directionally consistent with the prespecified effect and its 95% interval excludes the null, with no major reversal in the nonlinear alternative. Adverse: the coefficient is null or reverses after adjustment, or the nonlinear model shows no stable menopause-age effect. Inconclusive: the interval is wide, missingness is excessive, or the result is unstable across complete-case and imputed analyses.

The hypothesis is falsified for this population if the fully adjusted association is null or materially attenuated after measured blood-pressure and body-composition adjustment. A positive result would support an association and motivate prospective validation, not a causal or hormonal conclusion.
