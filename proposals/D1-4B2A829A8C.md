# Episode 83 successor: separate cross-practice ranking transport from mean-risk calibration transport

## Unresolved question, clinical importance, and substantive advance

At a fixed 24-hour reassessment after an early recorded norepinephrine/Levophed entry, a numeric repeat lactate could help place more patients who will die in hospital within seven days into a fixed-capacity silent-review queue. Yet repeat testing is optional and hospital-dependent. Episode 82 asks the important transport question: can a rule trained in hospitals with low repeat-lactate availability improve the queue in high-availability hospitals, and vice versa? Its decision contract makes acceptable target calibration a prerequisite for any ranking conclusion.

That prerequisite conflates two clinically different claims. A within-hospital top-k queue depends only on rank: a source-to-target shift in the intercept can badly miscalibrate probabilities while leaving every queue assignment and its event yield unchanged. Conversely, acceptable mean risk does not establish useful ranking. A hospital deciding whether to use a fixed number of daily review slots needs to know whether ranking transports; a hospital displaying absolute risks or applying a probability threshold needs separate local calibration evidence.

The substantive advance is therefore a prespecified two-axis transport experiment. It preserves the exact Episode 82 population, clocks, features, opposite-stratum training, capacities, and outcomes, but removes mean-risk calibration from the primary rank-computability gate and tests it as a separate, jointly bootstrapped secondary claim.

**Primary falsifiable hypothesis H-rank:** in both directed transfers—low-availability hospitals used for training and high-availability hospitals used for testing, and the reverse—and at every capacity (c\in C=\{0.05,0.075,0.10,0.125,0.15,0.175,0.20\}), adding the numeric repeat-lactate value to an otherwise identical observation-process model increases the fraction of occupied review slots containing a D7 event by at least 0.03. Thus 0.03 means 0.03 deaths per slot, exactly 3 additional D7 deaths per 100 occupied slots. Let (	au_7=min_{a\to b,c}Delta_7(a\to b,c)). Support for H-rank requires the simultaneous copied-hospital 95% lower bound for (	au_7) to be at least 0.03, the same-queue eventual-death guard to be nonnegative, and deletion-stability guards to pass.

**Secondary falsifiable hypothesis H-mean-cal:** for each directed target and for both R_process and R_full, the signed calibration-in-the-large error (e_{m,b}=overline p_{m,b}-overline D_{7,b}) is within ([-0.05,+0.05]). Equivalence requires the simultaneous 95% interval for every (e_{m,b}) to lie wholly within that band. This tests mean-risk transport only; calibration slope and Brier score are descriptive, and no probability-threshold deployment claim is made.

The 0.03 slot-yield and 0.05 mean-risk margins are operational anchors, not elicited utilities. H-rank can be supported while H-mean-cal is adverse or inconclusive. That pattern would justify only a prospective silent ranking validation plus local probability calibration before displaying absolute risks. It would not justify ordering lactate, changing treatment, or claiming preventable deaths.

Miscalibration means source-fitted mean predicted D7 risk differs materially from the target mean; it does not invalidate a rank-only queue but forbids calibrated-probability or threshold claims. Adverse means this specified transport/materiality claim failed, not that lactate lacks prognosis. Inconclusive is not equivalence.
