# Provisional HCC hypothesis: post-hepatectomy laboratory trajectory and 30-day deterioration

Status: provisional, episode 3; not a completed analysis or clinical conclusion.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a recorded liver resection in the HCC snapshot, the early post-operative trajectory of liver-related laboratory abnormalities will be associated with a clinically important deterioration signal during the subsequent 30 days, beyond baseline disease severity and operative context. The unresolved claim is whether a reproducible trajectory, rather than a single post-operative value, identifies a distinct recovery/deterioration pattern that could inform surveillance or escalation research.

This is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated effect. The available evidence establishes that the snapshot contains longitudinal clinical data, including procedures, laboratory results, encounters, diagnoses, medications, examinations, pathology, and clinical documents. It does not establish the proposed association or a causal mechanism.

## Clinical importance and substantive advance

A result could distinguish an early warning pattern that merits prospective validation from the simpler explanation that late post-operative values merely reflect baseline illness, surgical severity, or observation intensity. The substantive advance would be a prespecified comparison between a baseline/single-value model and a trajectory model on the same temporal split, with explicit handling of missingness, measurement timing, and competing deterioration definitions. A positive result would justify expert adjudication and prospective validation; a null result would be useful evidence against treating laboratory trajectories as a standalone warning signal in this setting.

## Population, time, variables, and outcome

Planned population: adults with a qualifying liver-resection procedure label in `procedures`, linked to the same HCC patient identifier; restrict to records with a usable procedure start time and sufficient post-operative follow-up. The exact procedure-label rule, age rule, and inclusion of repeat procedures must be frozen after header/schema inspection and a bounded label audit.

Index time is procedure `开始时间`. The primary exposure window is post-index through day 7, with a prespecified baseline window before index. Candidate laboratory features are bilirubin, INR, ALT, and albumin only if their assay labels, numeric values, and timing can be verified; do not combine incomparable assay units. The primary outcome is a prespecified deterioration signal within 30 days after index, using only information available after index (for example, transfer/disposition or a validated clinical endpoint), with the exact operational definition frozen before fitting. Missing procedure start times are excluded from the primary analysis and reported.

## Baseline versus substantive alternative

The simple baseline is a prespecified logistic or survival model using baseline severity, operative context, and a single post-operative laboratory summary, with patient-level temporal splitting and uncertainty intervals. The substantive alternative is a trajectory model (for example, a regularized mixed-effects or functional model of repeated liver-related laboratory measurements) that estimates whether slope/shape and within-patient change add information beyond the baseline and single-value model. The alternative can reveal whether recovery speed or persistent elevation is the informative pattern; the baseline loses that temporal structure. Both models use the same rows, split, outcome, and uncertainty evaluation. A neural sequence model is deferred unless the trajectory alternative shows sufficient event support and the added complexity is scientifically justified; it is not excluded categorically.

## Analysis and falsification

Fit both models on a temporal split, with no post-index outcome information in features. Compare held-out calibration and discrimination, but treat predictive improvement as secondary to the prespecified trajectory coefficient and its uncertainty. Report confidence intervals, missingness patterns, sensitivity to assay selection, and negative controls (pre-index trajectory or a non-liver laboratory trajectory) where data permit.

Supportive evidence requires a reproducible trajectory association with the frozen outcome, adequate calibration, and robustness to missingness and timing rules, not merely a small performance gain. Adverse evidence is a null or reversed trajectory effect, instability across assay/timing definitions, or failure of the trajectory model to outperform the baseline with adequate precision. Inconclusive evidence includes sparse outcomes, unusable timestamps/units, or wide intervals; it should lead to deferral or acquisition of adjudicated endpoints, not a claim of no effect.
