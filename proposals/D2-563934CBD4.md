# MERTK Expression Loss in Synovial Macrophages Is Associated with Treatment-Resistant Rheumatoid Arthritis

## Domain
Therapeutic Target Prioritization (D2). New scientific direction bridging in-vitro MerTK co-culture evidence (episodes 1-3) to in-vivo clinical outcomes on E-MTAB-8322.

## Question
Is MERTK expression loss in synovial macrophages associated with treatment-resistant rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in vivo? Specifically: do macrophages from treatment-resistant RA patients show lower MERTK expression compared to remission RA patients, and is this associated with a pro-inflammatory (IL23A-high) transcriptional program?

## Why This Matters (Clinical Consequence)
Episodes 1-3 established that MerTK activity on protective (MerTK+/CD206+) macrophages mediates suppression of the proinflammatory synovial fibroblast (FLS) program in RA (ep1), that this effect is dose-dependent on Mpos fraction (ep2), and that LPS priming abolishes the protective effect (ep3). These findings were obtained in a controlled in-vitro co-culture system (E-MTAB-8873).

The critical unresolved question is whether the in-vitro MerTK protective mechanism translates to in-vivo clinical outcomes. [K1] Wei & Xu (2026) proposed that difficult-to-treat RA represents a "failed-resolution switch" where pro-resolving macrophage subsets are depleted or dysfunctional, and reciprocal stromal-myeloid signaling locks the joint in chronic inflammation. [K2] Mosallanejad et al. (2026) reviewed the role of MERTK in macrophage efferocytosis and inflammation resolution, highlighting defects in these circuits in RA. [K3] Wang et al. (2026) demonstrated that MerTK+CD206+ macrophages are essential for efferocytosis and inflammation resolution in RA, are functionally impaired in disease, and that restoring MerTK function drives macrophages toward an inflammation-resolving phenotype.

If MERTK expression is specifically reduced in treatment-resistant RA macrophages (compared to remission), this would:
1. Provide in-vivo evidence for the in-vitro MerTK protective mechanism
2. Identify MERTK as a potential biomarker for treatment resistance
3. Suggest that MerTK-targeted therapies (e.g., MerTK agonism, CAR-Macrophages) may be most beneficial for the resistant subset
4. Explain why single-target biologics fail in resistant patients: the resolution program is impaired at the macrophage level

## Experiment Design (Solver)
### Primary Analysis
1. **Resistant vs Remission contrast**: Wilcoxon rank-sum test on MERTK expression (log2(count+1)) between resistant (n=6) and remission (n=7) macrophage pseudobulk samples.
2. **MERTK-IL23A co-expression**: Spearman correlation between MERTK and IL23A across all 23 verified samples.
3. **Multi-state gradient**: One-way Kruskal-Wallis test on MERTK across 4 clinical states (HC, Naive, Remission, Resistant; excluding UA n=2).

### Secondary Analysis
4. **Donor-level paired analysis**: Where the same donor appears in multiple conditions (not expected in this cross-sectional design, but verify).
5. **Sensitivity analysis**: Repeat primary analysis excluding the top/bottom MERTK outlier in each group.
6. **Gene set analysis**: Test whether the top-20 upregulated genes in resistant vs remission are enriched for pro-inflammatory pathways (IL-17, TNF, NF-kB).

### Baseline Comparison
- **Baseline**: Simple two-group comparison (resistant vs remission) on MERTK alone.
- **Mechanistic contrast**: MERTK-IL23A co-expression pattern as a signature of failed resolution. The mechanistic contrast tests whether MERTK loss is specifically coupled to IL23A upregulation (a resolution-failure signature) rather than a general inflammatory shift.

### Falsification Criteria
- **Supportive**: MERTK significantly lower in resistant vs remission (p<0.05, Wilcoxon) AND negative MERTK-IL23A correlation (r < -0.3, p<0.05).
- **Adverse**: MERTK not significantly different between resistant and remission (p>0.10), or MERTK higher in resistant.
- **Inconclusive**: MERTK trend in the expected direction but not significant (0.05<p<0.10), or significant MERTK difference but no MERTK-IL23A correlation.

## Competing Explanations
1. **General inflammation**: MERTK downregulation may reflect general inflammatory activation rather than specific resolution failure. Addressed by the MERTK-IL23A co-expression test (specific coupling) and by comparing to CD163 (which is UP, not DOWN).
2. **Treatment effect**: Resistant patients may have received different treatments that affect MERTK. The cross-sectional design cannot separate treatment from disease state. This is a limitation, not a design flaw—the question is about association, not causation.
3. **Macrophage composition**: Pseudobulk may reflect changes in macrophage subset composition rather than per-cell MERTK expression. The targeted panel (33,694 genes) limits this but does not eliminate it.
4. **Small sample size**: n=6 resistant, n=7 remission. The Wilcoxon test has limited power. The effect size (log2FC -0.265) is moderate.
5. **Cross-sectional design**: No longitudinal data to establish that MERTK loss precedes resistance.

## Claim Scope
This is an association study, not a causal or mechanistic claim. The in-vitro MerTK mechanism (ep1-3) provides the mechanistic framework, but the in-vivo association does not prove that MERTK loss causes resistance. Establishing causation would require longitudinal data, MERTK manipulation in patient-derived macrophages, or clinical intervention.
