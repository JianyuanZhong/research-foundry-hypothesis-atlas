# Bone-muscle discordance and incident fracture risk: a two-pathway design without residualization

## Scientific question and clinical importance

Among UK Biobank participants aged >=50 with valid DXA BMD and grip strength, does low grip strength predict incident fracture risk independently of BMD, and does this excess risk differ by baseline fall history?

The clinically consequential unresolved question is whether low BMD and low grip strength represent a single latent frailty process (where muscle weakness is merely a marker of overall biological ageing) or two separable pathways: (1) bone fragility driving fracture independent of muscle, and (2) muscle weakness driving falls that then cause fracture. This distinction has direct implications for screening and prevention: if the two pathways are separable, then dual anti-resorptive + resistance-exercise interventions would be more effective than bone-targeted therapy alone for the discordant subgroup.

The leading explanation (single latent process) is that low BMD and low grip strength are both downstream of a shared ageing/frailty trajectory, so grip strength adds no independent fracture risk beyond what BMD alone predicts after adjustment. The strongest rival (two-pathway) is that muscle weakness drives falls, which cause fracture through a mechanism independent of bone fragility, so grip strength adds independent risk even after BMD adjustment.

## Evidence for the opening

Wang et al. (Medicine 2026) [K1] showed in a cross-sectional NHANES study that the joint presence of low BMD and low relative grip strength identifies a higher-risk fracture subgroup (OR 3.09, 95% CI 1.13-8.45) compared to low BMD alone (OR 2.43, 95% CI 0.93-6.33), supporting a two-pathway hypothesis. Harvey et al. (JBMR 2025) [K2] demonstrated that the FRAX model improves fracture prediction with additional clinical variables, supporting the value of multi-parameter risk stratification beyond BMD alone. Evenepoel et al. (WCO-IOF-ESCEO 2025) [K3] emphasized that bone fragility is a disorder of bone strength that increases fracture risk, and the treatment gap is pronounced in postmenopausal women, supporting the clinical importance of identifying high-risk subgroups.

The unresolved question is whether the BMD x grip-strength interaction persists after full adjustment and whether it is modified by baseline fall history, which would distinguish a two-pathway mechanism from a single latent frailty process.

## Estimand and design

Primary estimand: the BMD x grip-strength interaction coefficient in a Cox proportional hazards model for incident fracture, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol, and physical activity.

The interaction is tested directly (not via residualization, which can induce collider bias). The model is:

hazard(t) = h0(t) * exp(beta1 * BMD + beta2 * grip + beta3 * BMD * grip + beta4 * age + beta5 * sex + beta6 * BMI + beta7 * smoking + beta8 * alcohol + beta9 * physical_activity)

A significant beta3 (p < 0.05) with a hazard ratio for low grip strength that is larger at lower BMD supports the two-pathway hypothesis. A non-significant beta3 supports the single latent process.

Sensitivity analysis: stratify by baseline fall history (1003). If the interaction is stronger in the fall-history group, this supports the falls-mediated pathway. This is a sensitivity analysis, not a causal mediation analysis.

## Adjustment hierarchy

Model 0: unadjusted (BMD, grip, interaction)
Model 1: + age, sex
Model 2: + BMI
Model 3: + smoking, alcohol
Model 4: + physical activity

The primary analysis is Model 4. Models 0-3 are reported as sensitivity analyses.

## Learned alternative

A gradient-boosted survival model (XGBoost with survival objective) is fitted on the same covariates plus the BMD x grip interaction term. The model is evaluated on a held-out 20% test set using C-index. The interaction adds predictive value if the C-index improves by >=0.01 over the main-effects-only model.

The learned alternative can reveal non-linear or higher-order interactions that the Cox model cannot capture. If the XGBoost model shows a significant improvement but the Cox interaction is non-significant, this suggests a non-linear two-pathway mechanism.

## Falsification criteria

The hypothesis is falsified if:
1. The BMD x grip interaction is non-significant (p >= 0.05) in Model 4.
2. The hazard ratio for low grip strength is not larger at lower BMD (i.e., the interaction coefficient is negative or non-significant).
3. The XGBoost C-index improvement is <0.01 over the main-effects-only model.

## Supportive, adverse, and inconclusive results

Supportive: significant BMD x grip interaction (p < 0.05) with HR for low grip larger at lower BMD, and XGBoost C-index improvement >=0.01. This supports the two-pathway hypothesis.

Adverse: non-significant interaction or negative interaction coefficient. This supports the single latent process hypothesis.

Inconclusive: significant interaction but XGBoost C-index improvement <0.01, or vice versa. This suggests the interaction is present but not predictive, or the learned model captures a different pattern.

## What this would and would not establish

This analysis would establish whether BMD and grip strength have a significant interaction in predicting fracture, and whether this interaction is modified by fall history. It would not establish causation or a hormonal mechanism. The falls stratification is a sensitivity analysis, not a causal mediation analysis.

Stronger claims (e.g., that muscle weakness causes falls that cause fracture) would require longitudinal fall data, intervention studies, or causal mediation analysis with appropriate assumptions.
