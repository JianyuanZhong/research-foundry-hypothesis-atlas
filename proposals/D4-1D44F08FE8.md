# UKB-33: Low Testosterone × Low SHBG — Do They Represent Different Male Metabolic States?

## Unresolved Question and Clinical Importance

Low total testosterone (T) in men is a heterogeneous clinical finding. The prevailing clinical
interpretation treats all low-T men as a single hypogonadal phenotype, yet the SHBG
(sex hormone-binding globulin) context fundamentally alters the physiological meaning of
a given total T value. Two mechanistically distinct pathways are proposed:

- **Muscle-driven phenotype** (low T + low SHBG): Low SHBG reflects low hepatic
  production (often from insulin resistance or obesity), and the combination with low T
  may indicate a androgen-deficient state that preferentially impairs skeletal muscle
  maintenance, leading to grip strength decline, sarcopenia, and frailty.

- **Insulin-driven phenotype** (low T + high SHBG): High SHBG is a marker of enhanced
  hepatic SHBG synthesis (driven by estrogenic activity or insulin sensitivity), and the
  combination with low T may indicate a state where insulin resistance is the primary
  driver, with T suppression as a downstream consequence, leading to type 2 diabetes
  (T2D) and metabolic syndrome.

**Clinical importance**: If these two phenotypes are confirmed to have divergent
outcomes, then (1) clinicians should not treat all low-T men identically; (2) SHBG
measurement should be considered alongside total T in risk stratification; (3) the
indication for testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) may differ by SHBG context;
and (4) prevention strategies (lifestyle vs. metabolic) should be targeted differently.

This question is unresolved because:
- Most prior studies examine T or SHBG independently, not their joint distribution
- The Nature Communications 2025 metabolomics GWAS [K1-context] identified sex-specific
  metabolic loci but did not test the T×SHBG discordance hypothesis directly
- Dabbs-Brown et al. 2025 [K1] showed that T, bioavailable T, and SHBG each interact
  with genetic risk for T2D in UK Biobank, but did not test whether the T×SHBG
  *combination* predicts divergent phenotypic outcomes
- Prokopidis et al. 2026 [K2] confirmed T-grip strength association in NHANES but
  did not stratify by SHBG
- Liu et al. 2026 [K3] reviewed SHBG-CHD evidence and noted that low SHBG is
  independently associated with CHD risk, partially mediated by insulin resistance,
  but did not test the T×SHBG interaction for metabolic outcomes

## Specific, Falsifiable Hypothesis

**Primary hypothesis**: In UK Biobank men with baseline total T below the cohort median
(11.61 nmol/L), those with SHBG below the cohort median (36.95 nmol/L) have a higher
hazard of incident type 2 diabetes (ICD10 E10-*) compared to those with SHBG above
the median, after adjusting for age, BMI, body fat percentage, and baseline HbA1c.

**Secondary hypothesis**: In the same low-T subgroup, men with low SHBG have a higher
hazard of >10% grip strength decline between assessment visits compared to men with
high SHBG, after adjusting for age, BMI, and baseline grip strength.

**Falsification criteria**: If the T×SHBG interaction hazard ratio (HR) for T2D falls
within 0.90–1.10 (95% CI) after full adjustment, the discordance hypothesis is not
supported. Similarly, if the interaction HR for grip decline falls within 0.90–1.10,
the muscle-driven phenotype is not supported.

## Study Design

### Population
- UK Biobank men (sex=1) with non-missing baseline total T and SHBG (n=195,875)
- Exclusion: baseline T2D (HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol OR ICD10 E10 before baseline)
- Exclusion: age <40 or >85 (to focus on the age range where T decline is most relevant)

### Exposure Definition
- **Total T**: continuous (nmol/L) and dichotomized at cohort median (11.61 nmol/L)
- **SHBG**: continuous (nmol/L) and dichotomized at cohort median (36.95 nmol/L)
- **Primary contrast**: Low T + Low SHBG vs. Low T + High SHBG
- **Interaction term**: T × SHBG (continuous × continuous, and dichotomized)

### Outcomes
1. **Primary**: Incident T2D (ICD10 E10-*) — time from baseline to first E10 diagnosis
2. **Secondary**: >10% decline in right-hand grip strength between visits
3. **Tertiary**: HbA1c change (mmol/mol) between visits (continuous outcome)

### Analysis Plan
1. **Cox proportional hazards model** for T2D:
   - Model 1: Unadjusted (T, SHBG, T×SHBG)
   - Model 2: + age, BMI
   - Model 3 (full): + age, BMI, body fat %, baseline HbA1c, smoking, alcohol, physical activity
   - Primary estimate: interaction HR for T×SHBG

2. **Cox PH model** for grip decline (>10%):
   - Same covariate hierarchy
   - Restricted to men with both grip measurements (n=9,881)

3. **Linear regression** for HbA1c change:
   - T, SHBG, T×SHBG + covariates
   - Restricted to men with both HbA1c measurements

4. **Sensitivity analyses**:
   - a) Use repeat T/SHBG (n=6,636) to assess measurement error
   - b) Exclude men with BMI ≥35 (severe obesity)
   - c) Exclude men with baseline HbA1c ≥42 (prediabetes)
   - d) Restrict to visit gap 2-5 years (most common interval)
   - e) Use left-hand grip instead of right
   - f) Estimate free testosterone (T × [SHBG + albumin] formula) as sensitivity
   - g) Propensity score matching on BMI and age

### Baselines and Comparisons
- **Reference group**: High T + High SHBG (the "healthy" quadrant)
- **Primary comparison**: Low T + Low SHBG vs. Low T + High SHBG
- **Secondary comparison**: Low T + Low SHBG vs. High T + Low SHBG

## Alternative Explanations

1. **Obesity confounding**: Obesity lowers both T and SHBG and increases T2D risk.
   - Mitigation: Adjust for BMI and body fat %; sensitivity analysis excluding BMI≥35

2. **Chronic disease**: Existing metabolic disease may lower T and SHBG.
   - Mitigation: Exclude baseline T2D; adjust for baseline HbA1c

3. **Blood draw timing**: Diurnal variation in T (higher in morning) and SHBG.
   - Mitigation: UKB blood draws are standardized (morning, fasting); sensitivity
     analysis on time-of-day if available

4. **Reverse causation**: Early T2D may lower T before clinical diagnosis.
   - Mitigation: Exclude baseline prediabetes (HbA1c≥42); lag analysis excluding
     first 2 years of follow-up

5. **Measurement error**: Single T/SHBG measurement has biological variability.
   - Mitigation: Sensitivity analysis with repeat measurements (n=6,636)

## Distinction Between Supportive, Adverse, and Inconclusive Outcomes

### Supportive Results
- T×SHBG interaction HR for T2D < 0.90 (95% CI excludes 1.0) in the full model
- T×SHBG interaction HR for grip decline < 0.90 (95% CI excludes 1.0)
- Consistent direction across sensitivity analyses
- **Interpretation**: The SHBG context of low T meaningfully predicts divergent
  metabolic outcomes, supporting the discordance hypothesis.

### Adverse Results
- T×SHBG interaction HR for T2D > 1.10 (95% CI excludes 1.0)
- OR: Low T + High SHBG has HIGHER T2D risk than Low T + Low SHBG
- **Interpretation**: The insulin-driven phenotype (high SHBG) may be the higher-risk
  state, reversing the hypothesized direction. This would still be scientifically
  important as it identifies the correct risk direction.

### Inconclusive Results
- T×SHBG interaction HR within 0.90–1.10 (95% CI includes 1.0)
- **Interpretation**: No evidence for a discordance effect in this population.
  Possible reasons: (a) the effect is truly absent; (b) insufficient power for the
  interaction term; (c) confounding not fully controlled; (d) the phenotype
  distinction requires different thresholds or a different population.
  **Next steps**: Would require larger sample, different exposure definition, or
  a different population (e.g., older men, men with confirmed hypogonadism).

### What Would NOT Be Established
- Causal direction (T→SHBG vs. SHBG→T vs. common cause)
- Mechanistic pathway (requires biomarker or genetic data)
- Clinical utility for TRT decisions (requires RCT)
- Generalizability to other populations (requires external validation)

## Methodological Alternatives Considered

### Alternative 1: Mendelian Randomization (MR)
- **Description**: Use genetic instruments for T and SHBG to estimate causal effects
  on T2D and grip strength.
- **Advantage**: Less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation.
- **Disadvantage**: Requires GWAS summary statistics for T and SHBG; UKB data may
  not have sufficient genetic data in this parquet; MR assumes no pleiotropy.
- **Decision**: Deferred. The observational interaction analysis is the primary
  approach; MR could be a follow-up if the interaction is significant.

### Alternative 2: Machine Learning Classification
- **Description**: Use gradient boosting or neural networks to classify men into
  "muscle-driven" vs. "insulin-driven" phenotypes based on T, SHBG, and other
  biomarkers.
- **Advantage**: Can capture non-linear interactions; may identify novel subgroups.
- **Disadvantage**: Less interpretable; requires larger sample for stable
  classification; does not directly test the hypothesized interaction.
- **Decision**: Deferred. The Cox PH interaction model is more directly
  hypothesis-driven and interpretable. ML could be explored if the interaction
  is significant and we want to refine the phenotype definition.

### Alternative 3: Joint Modeling of T and SHBG Trajectories
- **Description**: Use mixed-effects models to model T and SHBG trajectories over
  time, then relate trajectory parameters to outcomes.
- **Advantage**: Captures within-person change; handles missing data.
- **Disadvantage**: Requires multiple measurements (only 6,636 men have repeat
  T+SHBG); more complex; may be underpowered.
- **Decision**: Deferred as primary approach; used as sensitivity analysis with
  repeat measurements.

### Alternative 4: Free Testosterone as Exposure
- **Description**: Use estimated free T (T × [SHBG + albumin] formula) instead of
  total T and SHBG separately.
- **Advantage**: More physiologically relevant; captures the bioavailable fraction.
- **Disadvantage**: Formula-based estimate, not measured; may not capture the
  discordance hypothesis (which is specifically about the T×SHBG combination).
- **Decision**: Used as sensitivity analysis, not primary exposure.

## Limitations

1. **Single time point**: Baseline T and SHBG are single measurements; biological
   variability may attenuate the interaction.
2. **ICD10 coding**: T2D diagnosis depends on hospital admission; milder cases
   may be missed.
3. **Grip strength**: Only 9,881 men have both measurements; limited power for
   the secondary outcome.
4. **No medication data**: Cannot adjust for TRT use or other medications.
5. **No lifestyle data in this parquet**: Cannot adjust for diet, exercise, or
   smoking (would require additional UKB data tables).
6. **Cross-sectional baseline**: Cannot establish temporal precedence of T/SHBG
   changes relative to outcomes.
