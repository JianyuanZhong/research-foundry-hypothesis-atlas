# Scientific Proposal: Testing Intensity Gaps During Critical Transitions Predict Deterioration Beyond Clinical State

## 1. Refined Hypothesis Statement

### Primary Hypothesis
During critical illness transitions (from stable to unstable clinical status), an abrupt reduction in laboratory testing intensity—measured as the time-to-first test after status change—is associated with subsequent deterioration (new organ failure or death), independent of pre-transition clinical severity, hospital-level factors, and time-dependent covariates [K1], [K2].

### Secondary Hypothesis
The association between testing intensity gaps and deterioration is modified by:
- Patient-specific factors: comorbidity burden, age, baseline renal/hepatic function
- Hospital-level factors: ICU volume, teaching status, region
- Transition characteristics: duration of stability before transition, type of instability (e.g., hypotension vs. respiratory vs. metabolic)

## 2. Study Population

**Population**: Adult patients in the eICU database with longitudinal laboratory records and documented clinical status changes.

**Inclusion criteria**:
- Age ≥ 18 years
- ICU stay duration ≥ 48 hours
- At least 24 hours of continuous clinical monitoring
- At least one documented transition from "stable" to "unstable" clinical status
- At least one laboratory test within 48 hours of transition

**Exclusion criteria**:
- Patients discharged alive within 24 hours of transition
- Patients with end-of-life care or palliative care designation
- Patients with incomplete laboratory records (< 50% of expected tests)

**Temporal boundaries**:
- Study period: All admissions in eICU-2.0 database
- Follow-up window: 48 hours after clinical transition
- Primary outcome assessment: 24 hours after transition

### Primary Tables
- **apachePredVar** (table-b1f86cc4a8d9f2a2.json): Clinical status changes (stable/unstable transitions)
- **lab** (table-79bdb33275339b1a.json): Laboratory values with timestamps

### Key Variables
- **Clinical status**: apachePredVar.status (stable/unstable)
- **Transition time**: Time between consecutive status changes
- **Testing intensity**: Number of laboratory tests within 24h, 48h, and 72h after transition
- **Time-to-first-test**: Gap between transition timestamp and first lab test
- **Outcome**: apachePatientResult.death or new organ failure (Apache II score increase > 20 points)
- **Covariates**: Age, sex, race, comorbidities (apachePatientResult comorbidity fields)
- **Hospital factors**: ICU volume, teaching status, region (hospital table)

## 4. Primary Outcome and Exposure

**Primary outcome**: Subsequent deterioration within 24 hours of clinical transition, defined as:
- Death within 24 hours, OR
- New acute organ failure (Apache II score increase > 20 points)

**Primary exposure**: Testing intensity gap, measured as:
- Time-to-first-laboratory-test after clinical transition (continuous), OR
- Binary indicator: test within 2h vs. >2h after transition

## 5. Statistical Methods

### Primary Analysis: Cox Proportional Hazards
- Model: Time-to-deterioration with time-dependent testing intensity
- Covariates: Baseline severity (Apache II), demographics, hospital factors
- Adjustment: Time-varying covariates (vital signs, lab values)
- Effect measure: Hazard ratio (HR) for deterioration per hour delay in first test

### Alternative Analysis: Logistic Regression
- Outcome: Deterioration within 24h (binary)
- Exposure: Testing intensity (categorized: 0 tests, 1-2 tests, 3+ tests)
- Purpose: Sensitivity analysis and calibration

### Baseline Comparison: Simple Logistic Regression
- Only testing intensity and baseline severity
- Purpose: Establish minimum effect estimate

### Machine Learning Alternative: Gradient Boosting
- Model: XGBoost/LightGBM with temporal features
- Purpose: Test if non-linear relationships exist
- Evaluation: AUROC, calibration curves, net benefit

### Hierarchical Modeling
- Random effects: Hospital-level variation
- Purpose: Account for between-hospital practice variation

## 6. Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be falsified if:
1. No association between testing intensity gap and deterioration (HR ≈ 1, 95% CI includes 1)
2. The association is entirely explained by confounding (e.g., sicker patients get tested less often)
3. The association is consistent across hospitals (no hierarchical variation)
4. Testing intensity is not an independent predictor after adjusting for clinical state

## 7. Clinical Significance

**Why this matters**:
- Current critical care practice often relies on fixed testing schedules rather than patient-specific needs
- This study tests whether "when to test" is as important as "what to test"
- Could inform dynamic monitoring protocols that optimize resource use while maintaining safety
- May identify patients who are under-monitored and at risk of delayed deterioration detection

**Potential impact**:
- Protocol development for adaptive monitoring based on clinical status transitions
- Resource allocation strategies that prioritize testing during instability
- Quality improvement initiatives to ensure appropriate monitoring intensity

## 8. Baseline and Alternative Methods

### Baseline: Simple Time-Dependent Cox Model
- Testing intensity as main effect only
- Adjusted for baseline severity
- **What it reveals**: Main association between testing and outcome

### Alternative 1: Machine Learning with Feature Importance
- Gradient boosting with temporal features
- SHAP values for interpretability
- **What it reveals**: Non-linear relationships, interactions, and which factors drive predictions

### Alternative 2: Causal Inference Framework
- Propensity score weighting for testing intensity
- Instrumental variable analysis (if feasible)
- **What it reveals**: Potential causal effect vs. observational association

### Scientific Value of Alternatives
The baseline Cox model establishes the main association. The ML alternative can reveal:
- Non-linear testing intensity-outcome relationships
- Important interactions (e.g., testing matters more for certain comorbidities)
- Subgroups where monitoring gaps are particularly dangerous

The causal inference approach addresses the key limitation: **confounding by clinical severity**. Sicker patients may naturally receive less testing if staff are overwhelmed, or conversely, receive more testing if they are the focus of attention. Addressing this confounding is essential for clinical translation.
