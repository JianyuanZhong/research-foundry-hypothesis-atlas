# Provisional HCC hypothesis: post-hepatectomy laboratory trajectory and 30-day deterioration

Status: provisional, episode 5; hypothesis and experiment design only, not a completed analysis or clinical conclusion.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a recorded liver resection in the HCC snapshot, the early post-operative trajectory of liver-related laboratory abnormalities will be associated with a clinically important deterioration signal during the subsequent 30 days, beyond baseline disease severity and operative context. The unresolved claim is whether a reproducible trajectory, rather than a single post-operative value, identifies a distinct recovery/deterioration pattern that could inform surveillance or escalation research.

This is not established. The HCC catalog and prior source audit support the existence of longitudinal procedure, laboratory, encounter, diagnosis, medication, examination, pathology, and clinical-document data. They do not establish the proposed association, a causal mechanism, or clinical utility.

## Clinical importance and substantive advance

A positive result would distinguish an early warning pattern that merits prospective validation from the simpler explanation that late deterioration is only a marker of baseline illness or operative severity. A null or adverse result would argue against treating a laboratory trajectory as an independent warning signal and would redirect attention to severity, timing, and measurement artifacts. The advance is a falsifiable comparison of trajectory information against a single-value baseline on the same cohort, rather than an unqualified prediction claim.

## Baseline versus substantive alternative

Baseline: a prespecified single post-operative laboratory value (or a simple linear slope if the schedule permits), adjusted for baseline severity and operative context, predicting the 30-day deterioration signal.

Alternative: a low-dimensional trajectory model fitted to the same laboratory observations, such as a regularized multivariate linear mixed model or a prespecified functional summary (intercept, slope, and recovery/relapse component), with the same covariates and outcome. The alternative can reveal whether timing and shape of recovery contain information lost by a single value; it is not justified by predictive complexity alone.

Use a temporal split by procedure date, with no patient appearing in both training and test sets. Report calibration, uncertainty intervals, and a prespecified discrimination/comparison metric; do not select the model by repeated favorable peeking. A simple baseline is scientifically adequate if the trajectory adds no reproducible information. A more complex learned model is deferred unless the low-dimensional trajectory model shows a substantive, held-out signal.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive: the trajectory model improves held-out prediction or explains a prespecified deterioration contrast beyond the single-value baseline, with uncertainty excluding the prespecified null and results stable to missingness and timing sensitivity.

Adverse: no reproducible incremental signal, or the apparent signal disappears after correcting for baseline severity, procedure timing, missingness, or transfer ascertainment.

Inconclusive: insufficient valid procedure timestamps, unresolvable assay units, sparse follow-up, or wide uncertainty. An imprecise null does not refute the hypothesis.

A supportive result would establish an association worth prospective validation, not causality or a treatment rule. Mechanistic claims, clinical adjudication of true deterioration, and external validation require expert review and additional data.
