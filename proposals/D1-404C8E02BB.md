# Residual Instability Before ICU Discharge: Pre-Discharge Vital Sign and Oxygen Requirement Abnormalities and Subsequent ICU Readmission or In-Hospital Death

## Parent
- Expert seed: `[prior hypothesis]` ([starting question])
- Dataset: MIMIC-IV 3.1

## Scientific Question

In ICU patients discharged alive, do residual abnormalities in a fixed 24-hour window before ICU discharge — specifically elevated oxygen requirements (FiO2 >0.4 or O2 flow >4 L/min) and vital sign instability (HR >100, SBP <90, RR >20, SpO2 <92%) — independently predict ICU readmission or in-hospital death, after adjusting for baseline severity and comorbidities?

## Unresolved Claim

Current ICU discharge decisions rely on clinician judgment and institutional criteria, but there is no systematic evidence that pre-discharge physiological instability (as captured by routine vital signs and oxygen requirements) is an independent predictor of ICU readmission. The hypothesis is that patients with residual abnormalities in the 24 hours before ICU discharge represent a distinct risk group with higher rates of ICU readmission and in-hospital death, independent of their baseline severity. This would support the development of objective, data-driven discharge readiness criteria.

## Clinical Importance

- ICU readmission rates are 4.6–10% in developed countries and are a key quality metric.
- Current discharge decisions are heterogeneous and based on clinician judgment.
- If pre-discharge instability is an independent predictor, it could:
  1. Inform objective discharge readiness criteria
  2. Identify patients who need step-down unit monitoring rather than direct ward transfer
  3. Reduce avoidable ICU readmissions and associated costs
  4. Improve patient safety by flagging "unstable" discharges for additional monitoring

## Evidence Base and Rival Explanations

**Supporting evidence:**
- [K1] Remoundou et al. (2026, Healthcare): Using MIMIC-IV, predicted 30-day ICU readmissions with XGBoost (AUROC 0.65–0.69) and LSTM (AUROC 0.73). Found that length of stay was a primary predictive driver after causal integration. This establishes that ICU readmission is predictable from EHR data, but does not specifically test pre-discharge physiological instability.
- [K2] Rens et al. (2026, Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf): In surgical ICU patients, vital sign abnormalities (tachycardia, hypotension, hypoxemia) on the ward after ICU transfer were associated with 11.44-fold increased odds of ICU readmission or death within 6 hours (95% CI: 7.49–17.49). This directly supports the hypothesis that post-ICU physiological instability predicts readmission, but was conducted after transfer, not before discharge.
- [K3] Zhou et al. (2026, Sci Prog): In MIMIC-IV, ICU-acquired delirium was independently associated with unplanned readmission (aHR=1.87, 95% CI: 1.20–2.92) and 90-day mortality (aHR=1.91, 95% CI: 1.04–3.50) in acute pancreatitis patients. This supports the concept that residual neurological/physiological dysfunction at ICU discharge predicts adverse outcomes.

**Rival explanations:**
1. **Severity confounding**: Patients with higher baseline severity may have both residual instability and higher readmission risk, with the instability being a proxy for severity rather than an independent predictor.
2. **Discharge timing bias**: Clinicians may discharge sicker patients earlier (to free beds), creating a spurious association between instability and readmission.
3. **Measurement artifact**: Vital signs may be less frequently measured in stable patients, creating missing data bias.
4. **Ward-level factors**: Post-ICU ward monitoring intensity may differ by patient, affecting both the detection of instability and the likelihood of readmission.

## Study Design

### Population
- Adult ICU patients (age ≥18) in MIMIC-IV 3.1
- Must have ≥1 ICU stay with discharge (not death in ICU)
- Must have vital sign data in the 24 hours before ICU discharge
- Must have O2 requirement data (FiO2 or O2 flow) in the 24 hours before ICU discharge
- Exclude: ICU stay <24h, patients who died in ICU

### Temporal Boundaries
- **Index time**: 24 hours before ICU discharge (outtime from icustays)
- **Exposure window**: [outtime - 24h, outtime]
- **Outcome window**: From ICU discharge to ICU readmission or hospital death

### Variables

**Exposure (Pre-Discharge Instability in 24h window):**
- *O2 requirement*: FiO2 >0.4 OR O2 flow >4 L/min at any point in the 24h window
- *HR instability*: HR >100 bpm at any point in the 24h window
- *Hypotension*: SBP <90 mmHg at any point in the 24h window
- *Tachypnea*: RR >20 breaths/min at any point in the 24h window
- *Hypoxemia*: SpO2 <92% at any point in the 24h window
- *Composite instability*: ≥2 of the above abnormalities present in the 24h window

**Covariates (measured at ICU admission or index time):**
- Age, sex (patients table)
- APACHE II score (chartevents)
- Sepsis status (ICD codes)
- Mechanical ventilation duration (chartevents)
- ICU length of stay
- Comorbidities (ICD codes)
- Vasopressor use (inputevents)

**Outcomes:**
- Primary: ICU readmission (≥1 additional ICU stay in same admission)
- Secondary: Hospital mortality (admissions.hospital_expire_flag)
- Tertiary: Time to ICU readmission (Cox model)

### Analysis Plan

1. **Cohort construction**: Identify all ICU discharges (outtime not null, patient alive). For each discharge, extract vital signs and O2 requirements in the 24h window before outtime.

2. **Exposure classification**: Classify each patient as:
   - *Stable*: No abnormalities in the 24h window
   - *Mild instability*: 1 abnormality
   - *Moderate-severe instability*: ≥2 abnormalities

3. **Primary analysis**: Cox proportional hazards model for ICU readmission, comparing instability groups vs. stable, adjusted for covariates. Report hazard ratio with 95% CI.

4. **Sensitivity analyses**:
   - Restrict to patients with ≥6 vital sign measurements in the 24h window
   - Use 12h and 48h windows instead of 24h
   - Propensity score matching on baseline severity
   - Restrict to medical vs. surgical ICUs

5. **Falsification criteria**:
   - **Supportive**: HR for moderate-severe instability vs. stable ≥1.3 with 95% CI excluding 1.0
   - **Adverse**: HR ≤1.0 (no increased risk or protective)
   - **Inconclusive**: 95% CI spans 1.0 with wide interval (imprecise estimate)

### Method Comparison

**Baseline**: Unadjusted comparison of readmission rates by instability group (descriptive).

**Learned alternative**: XGBoost classifier predicting ICU readmission using pre-discharge vital signs + covariates, compared to Cox model. The ML model could reveal:
- Non-linear interactions between different vital sign abnormalities
- Which specific vital sign combination is most predictive
- Whether the relationship is more complex than a simple binary instability variable

**Why both**: The Cox model provides interpretable hazard ratios for clinical decision-making. The ML model tests whether the relationship is more complex than a single binary instability variable. If the ML model substantially outperforms the Cox model, it suggests that the simple binary classification loses important information about the pattern of instability.

**Resource estimate**:
- Cohort construction: ~10 min on 8 CPU (reading chartevents for 24h windows)
- Cox model: <5 min
- XGBoost: <10 min (CPU, 16 cores)
- Total: <30 min on CPU

### Limitations
1. Single-center data (MIMIC-IV = Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center)
2. Vital sign measurement frequency varies by patient and clinical context
3. Cannot distinguish true physiological instability from measurement artifact
4. Discharge timing may be influenced by bed availability (confounding by indication)
5. No data on post-ICU ward monitoring intensity
6. ICU readmission is a composite outcome that may include planned readmissions

### What Would Change Understanding
- If pre-discharge instability is independently associated with ICU readmission: supports development of objective discharge readiness criteria, identifies patients who need step-down monitoring
- If no association: suggests that current clinician judgment is adequate, or that instability is a marker of severity rather than an independent predictor
- If interaction with severity: suggests instability matters most in moderate-severity patients where it could change management
