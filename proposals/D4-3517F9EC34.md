# Hypothesis: Asynchronous Organ Recovery After Circulatory Stabilization in Critically Ill Patients

## Scientific Question

**Unresolved Question**: In patients with severe infection or shock who have achieved initial circulatory stabilization, do parallel recovery trajectories across organ systems (kidney, respiratory, cardiovascular) predict subsequent clinical deterioration, independent of single-timepoint severity scores?

**Clinical Importance**: Current ICU prognostication relies heavily on single-timepoint severity scores (e.g., SOFA, APACHE II). However, these scores capture only a snapshot and miss the dynamic recovery process. Understanding whether asynchronous recovery (some organs recovering faster than others) predicts deterioration could:
1. Identify high-risk patients who may need intensified monitoring despite "stable" initial scores
2. Inform timing of clinical decision points (e.g., when to escalate or de-escalate therapy)
3. Reveal whether specific organ combinations are more predictive than individual organ scores

**Substantive Advance Over Existing Knowledge**:
- **Limitation of current approaches**: Single-timepoint severity scores assume recovery is either complete or incomplete at a fixed time, ignoring the temporal dynamics
- **What this study tests**: Whether the *pattern* of recovery (which organs recover, and when) carries prognostic information beyond the severity score itself
- **Novelty**: Most ICU prognostic models use static features; this approach explicitly models recovery trajectories as time-varying features

## Hypothesis to Test

After achieving circulatory stabilization (defined as MAP ≥ 65 mmHg and SOFA score ≤ 8 for ≥24 hours), patients who exhibit **asynchronous organ recovery** (defined as ≥2 organs showing delayed recovery beyond a threshold relative to baseline) are more likely to experience subsequent deterioration (requiring escalation of organ support or death) within 7 days, compared to patients with synchronous recovery or no recovery.

Specifically, we test:
1. **Primary hypothesis**: The proportion of organs showing delayed recovery predicts subsequent deterioration, independent of baseline severity
2. **Secondary hypothesis**: Specific organ combinations (e.g., delayed kidney + delayed respiratory) carry stronger predictive signal than individual delayed organs
3. **Mechanistic question**: Whether asynchronous recovery reflects underlying inflammatory heterogeneity or resource allocation challenges [K2]

## Study Population and Main Data

**Population**: MIMIC-IV patients with ICU stay ≥48 hours who:
- Had at least one documented infection (ICD-10 A00-B99) or shock (ICD-10 I95-I96)
- Achieved circulatory stabilization (MAP ≥ 65 mmHg for ≥24 hours)
- Had laboratory and vital sign measurements for at least 2 of 3 target organs (kidney, respiratory, cardiovascular)

**Main Data Tables**:

## Baseline Model

**Stratified Cox Proportional Hazards Model**:
- **Exposure**: Binary indicator of "delayed recovery" (≥1 organ showing delayed recovery vs. no delay)
- **Outcome**: Time to deterioration (escalation of organ support or death)
- **Covariates**:
  - Baseline SOFA score at stabilization
  - Age, sex, comorbidities (Charlson index)
  - Initial severity (peak SOFA during ICU stay)
- **Outcome measure**: Hazard ratio for deterioration with delayed recovery

**Rationale**: This baseline tests whether delayed recovery is simply a marker of severity, not an independent predictor.

## Alternative Model

**Time-Varying Organ Recovery Score Model**:
- **Exposure**: Continuous "recovery asynchrony score" calculated as:
  - For each organ: Z-score of recovery rate relative to population median
  - Asynchrony score = standardized deviation of organ-specific recovery rates from expected pattern
  - Categorized as: synchronous (all organs recover within 2 SD of expected), partially asynchronous (1-2 organs delayed), fully asynchronous (≥3 organs delayed)
- **Outcome**: Same as baseline (time to deterioration)
- **Covariates**: Same as baseline + organ-specific baseline values
- **Outcome measure**: Hazard ratio for each asynchrony category; test for linear trend

**What this could reveal that baseline loses**:
1. Whether the *pattern* of recovery (which organs are delayed) matters more than the *number* of delayed organs
2. Whether specific organ combinations have synergistic predictive value
3. Whether recovery trajectories interact with baseline severity (effect modification)

## Falsification Criteria

**Supportive evidence requires**:
1. HR for delayed recovery ≥ 1.15 (95% CI excludes 1.0) for primary hypothesis
2. Significant association between asynchrony score and deterioration (p < 0.01)
3. Consistency across organ combinations (e.g., delayed kidney+respiratory > delayed kidney alone)
4. Effect modification by baseline severity (stronger signal in severe patients)

**Adverse evidence requires**:
1. HR < 1.0 for delayed recovery (suggesting delayed recovery is protective or neutral)
2. No association between asynchrony score and deterioration
3. All organ combinations show similar effects (no synergistic pattern)
4. Effect is only present in low-severity patients (suggesting selection bias)

**Inconclusive evidence**:
1. HR between 1.05-1.15 (borderline significance)
2. Mixed results across organ combinations
3. Effect present but attenuated after adjustment for severity
4. Insufficient events for precise estimation

## Missing Evidence / Clinical Adjudication Required

1. **Definition of "delayed recovery"**: Requires expert consensus on what constitutes delayed vs. expected recovery for each organ
2. **Threshold for deterioration**: Escalation criteria need clinical validation (e.g., FiO2 increase of 2 levels may not capture all clinically relevant changes)
3. **Causal inference**: Observational design cannot establish causality; confounding by indication must be addressed
4. **External validation**: Requires independent cohort (e.g., eICU-CRD) to confirm findings
5. **Intervention implications**: Even if association is established, whether targeting recovery patterns improves outcomes requires RCT evidence
