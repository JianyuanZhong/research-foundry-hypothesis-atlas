# Research Proposal: Rate of Hemodynamic Recovery Modulates Hypotension-Related Kidney Injury Risk

## Unresolved Question

**Current Knowledge Gap**: Clinical sepsis management emphasizes maintaining blood pressure (BP) above threshold targets. However, a critical unresolved question remains:

> **Does the rate of recovery from hypotension (not just total duration or minimum BP) predict acute kidney injury (AKI), and is this relationship modified by hemodynamic reserve and vasopressor burden?**

**The Gap**: Existing research focuses on:
- Minimum BP as a static threshold metric [K2]
- Total duration of hypotension below threshold [K1]
- Cumulative time below threshold [K3]

These approaches miss a critical temporal dimension: **how rapidly BP recovers after hypotension episodes**. Two patients with identical hypotension duration may have vastly different outcomes if one recovers rapidly while the other remains hypotensive despite vasopressor support.

## Clinical Importance

### 1. Risk Stratification Beyond Static Metrics
A "recovery rate" metric could:
- Identify patients at high risk despite meeting BP targets
- Predict AKI in patients with adequate minimum BP but poor hemodynamic recovery
- Enable earlier intervention before irreversible organ injury

### 2. Treatment Optimization
Understanding the role of recovery rate could:
- Inform whether aggressive BP recovery is beneficial or potentially harmful
- Guide vasopressor titration strategies (prioritize recovery speed vs. maintaining pressure)
- Identify patients who benefit from adjunctive therapies (inotropes, mechanical circulatory support)

### 3. Mechanistic Insight
This question addresses a fundamental physiological question: **Is AKI risk driven by cumulative hypotension exposure, or by the rate of inadequate perfusion?** The answer has implications for:
- Pathophysiology of ischemia-reperfusion injury
- Optimal target hemodynamics in sepsis
- Interpretation of clinical trial endpoints

## Hypothesis

**Primary Hypothesis**: Among patients with septic shock, the *rate of systolic BP recovery* after hypotension episodes predicts subsequent AKI risk independently of:
- Total hypotension duration
- Minimum BP achieved
- Vasopressor exposure

**Moderating Effect**: This relationship is strengthened in patients with:
- Lower baseline hemodynamic reserve (reduced cardiac output, elevated systemic vascular resistance)
- Higher vasopressor burden

**Expected Effect Size**: A 10% slower BP recovery rate after hypotension episodes is associated with 1.5-2.0x increased AKI risk, after adjustment for minimum BP, duration, and vasopressor use.

## Methodological Alternatives

### Baseline Approach (Traditional)
- Logistic regression: AKI ~ hypotension duration + minimum SBP + vasopressor dose
- **What it tests**: Whether static summary metrics predict AKI
- **Limitation**: Cannot distinguish between cumulative exposure and recovery dynamics

### Alternative 1: Time-Weighted Burden Model
- Compute area-under-curve (AUC) of SBP deficit over hypotension episodes
- Model: AKI ~ AUC of SBP deficit + minimum SBP + vasopressor dose
- **What it reveals**: Whether total "pressure deficit" matters more than recovery speed

### Alternative 2: Recovery Rate Model (Primary Analysis)
- Define recovery rate as: (SBP at end of episode - minimum SBP during episode) / duration of recovery
- Model: AKI ~ recovery rate + minimum SBP + duration + vasopressor dose
- **What it reveals**: Whether rapid recovery is independently protective

### Alternative 3: Interaction Model
- Model: AKI ~ recovery rate × vasopressor burden + minimum SBP + duration
- **What it reveals**: Whether vasopressors modify the relationship between recovery rate and AKI

### Alternative 4: Machine Learning Baseline
- Gradient boosting or random forest with all temporal features
- **What it reveals**: Whether recovery rate is a dominant feature compared to other temporal patterns

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be falsified if:
1. **Recovery rate shows no independent association** with AKI after full adjustment (p > 0.05 for all coefficients)
2. **Minimum SBP fully mediates** the relationship between recovery rate and AKI (indirect effect < 5% of total effect)
3. **Vasopressor burden does not moderate** the recovery rate-AKI relationship (interaction p > 0.1)
4. **No dose-response gradient** is observed (e.g., faster recovery doesn't progressively reduce risk)

## Expected Results and Interpretation

### Supportive Results (Hypothesis Supported)
- Recovery rate is independently associated with AKI (OR ~1.5-2.0 per 10% slower recovery)
- This association persists after adjusting for minimum SBP, duration, and vasopressors
- Vasopressor burden moderates the relationship (stronger in high-burden patients)
- **Clinical implication**: Aggressive BP recovery may be beneficial; current minimum-SBP-focused targets may miss high-risk patients

### Adverse Results (Hypothesis Refuted)
- Recovery rate shows no independent association with AKI
- Minimum SBP fully explains the relationship
- No interaction with vasopressor burden
- **Clinical implication**: Current minimum-SBP targets are sufficient; recovery rate is not an independent risk factor

### Inconclusive Results (Requires Further Study)
- Weak association (OR ~1.1-1.3) that loses significance with additional adjustment
- Heterogeneous effects across subgroups (e.g., by organ failure severity)
- **Clinical implication**: More refined stratification or mechanistic studies needed
