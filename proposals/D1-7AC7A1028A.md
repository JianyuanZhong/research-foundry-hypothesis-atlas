# eICU landmark proposal: reduced testing during persistent instability

## Scientific question

Among adult ICU patients who remain physiologically unstable at a 48-hour landmark, does an abrupt reduction in laboratory testing during the next 24 hours predict short-term clinical deterioration, independent of current severity, preceding physiologic trajectory, treatment changes, hospital testing norms, discharge, and end-of-life practice?

The unresolved claim is **not** that reduced testing causes deterioration. It is whether monitoring intensity carries prognostic and practice-relevant information after conditioning on observable patient state. The leading rival is that reduced testing simply marks improving patients or hospitals with naturally sparse testing; a secondary rival is that reduced testing reflects impending discharge or end-of-life transitions rather than unrecognized instability.

## Population and time zero

- Source: eICU Collaborative Research Database v2.0 snapshot, joined only within eICU by `patientunitstayid`.
- Adults: `patient.age >= 18` after numeric coercion.
- ICU stay at least through the exposure window: `patient.unitdischargeoffset > 4320` minutes (72 hours).
- Landmark/time zero: ICU hour 48 (`offset = 2880` minutes).
- Baseline window: hours 24–48.
- Exposure window: hours 48–72.
- Outcome window: hours 72–96, with administrative censoring at ICU discharge/transfer if it precedes 96 hours.
- Require at least one core laboratory result in both baseline and exposure windows and at least one usable heart-rate or arterial/mean blood-pressure observation in each window.
- Persistent instability at time zero is defined from baseline-window data only, using any of:
  1. active vasopressor infusion before hour 48 (`infusionDrug.infusionoffset < 2880`);
  2. mean arterial pressure or noninvasive mean pressure below 65 mmHg, or systolic pressure below 90 mmHg;
  3. oxygen saturation below 92%, respiratory rate above 24/min, or heart rate above 120/min;
  4. worst baseline creatinine above 2.0 mg/dL, lactate above 2.0 mmol/L, potassium outside 3.0–5.5 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L, or hemoglobin below 7 g/dL.
- Exclude/censor stays with an end-of-life discussion recorded before hour 48 (`carePlanEOL.cpleoldiscussionoffset < 2880`); perform sensitivity analyses with censoring rather than exclusion.

## Exposure

Primary transparent exposure: **no core laboratory result in hours 48–72** among patients with at least one core result in hours 24–48, while still unstable at hour 48.

Secondary continuous exposure: `log((1 + core tests in 48–72) / (1 + core tests in 24–48))`, plus categories of relative reduction.

A hospital-and-patient expected-testing alternative will model the hour 48–72 core-test count with a negative-binomial model using pre-landmark severity, baseline test count, hospital, and time since admission. The residual or observed/expected ratio captures departures from expected monitoring intensity. This is more informative than the raw ratio because it separates a sparse-testing hospital from an abrupt patient-level reduction.

Core labs are defined by these exact eICU labels and units, validated from `lab.csv.gz`:
- `bedside glucose` or `glucose` — mg/dL
- `potassium` — mmol/L
- `creatinine` — mg/dL
- `lactate` — mmol/L
- `bicarbonate` or `HCO3` — mmol/L
- `WBC x 1000` — K/mcL
- `Hgb` — g/dL
- `platelets x 1000` — K/mcL
- `BUN` — mg/dL

## Outcomes

Primary outcome within hours 72–96 (less dependent on subsequent laboratory testing than lab-only deterioration):

- ICU death, **or**
- new or increased vasopressor support, **or**
- new or increased invasive ventilation/ventilatory support.

Vasopressors are defined by exact normalized `infusionDrug.drugname` strings, with active status from `drugrate` or `infusionrate` above a prespecified positive threshold. Validated example strings include:
- `Norepinephrine (mcg/min)`
- `Phenylephrine (mcg/min)`
- `Vasopressin (units/min)`
- `Dopamine (mcg/kg/min)`
- `Epinephrine (mcg/min)`
- `Dobutamine (mcg/kg/min)`
- `Levophed (mcg/min)`

Ventilation/ventilatory support is defined from `respiratoryCharting.csv.gz` using `respcharttypecat`, `respchartvaluelabel`, and `respchartoffset`. A new or increased invasive support event is a documented transition to invasive ventilation, or a clinically meaningful increase in ventilatory support, during hours 72–96 among patients with no comparable support in the pre-landmark window. `respiratoryCare.csv.gz` provides `airwaytype`, `ventstartoffset`, and `ventendoffset` to corroborate artificial airway and ventilation timing. No post-exposure information may be used to define baseline status.

Secondary outcomes:
- ICU mortality and hospital mortality.
- A separately reported lab-documented deterioration composite (new abnormal potassium/bicarbonate/creatinine/lactate or hemoglobin drop), clearly labeled as testing-sensitive and not primary.

## Baseline analysis

A transparent landmark logistic regression or discrete-time survival model for the 72–96-hour outcome:

- exposure: no core lab versus any core lab in 48–72 (and continuous ratio secondary);
- patient covariates from before hour 48: age, sex, unit type, APACHE score/version, admission diagnosis grouping, baseline core-test count, worst physiologic values and 0–24 to 24–48 trends, treatment intensity, vasopressor/ventilation status, and EOL status;
- hospital clustering: random intercept or hospital bootstrap;
- report adjusted odds/hazard ratio, 95% confidence interval, absolute risk difference with uncertainty, calibration, and event counts.

## Substantive alternative

A dynamic landmark model using a hospital-and-patient expected testing intensity residual:

- Fit a negative-binomial model for the 48–72h test count using only pre-landmark severity, treatment, baseline testing, and hospital.
- Use the observed/expected ratio or residual as the exposure in the deterioration model.
- Compare with the baseline raw reduction model using likelihood, calibration, decision-curve analysis, and cross-validated hospital holdout performance.
- The alternative can distinguish an abrupt patient-level monitoring departure from a hospital's routine sparse testing and tests whether nonlinear severity trajectories change the testing–deterioration relation. It does not itself establish causality.

If the dynamic model materially changes the estimate or reveals a dose–response absent from the raw contrast, that is scientifically informative. If it does not improve calibration or estimates, the simpler transparent baseline remains preferred.

## Falsification and interpretation

- **Supportive:** Among unstable 48-hour survivors, absent 48–72h core testing is associated with higher 72–96h clinical deterioration after adjustment, with a confidence interval excluding no association and evidence of dose–response using continuous reduction. This supports the claim that monitoring intensity tracks unrecognized patient need, not that testing itself prevents deterioration.
- **Adverse:** Absent or reduced testing is associated with lower risk, or the adjusted estimate is confidently near or below the null. This suggests reduced testing primarily marks recovery, treatment de-escalation, or discharge readiness rather than unrecognized risk.
- **Inconclusive:** Confidence intervals are too wide for a decision-relevant minimum effect, exposure or outcome prevalence is too low, or the result is highly sensitive to hospital effects, censoring, ventilation-label definitions, or instability definitions.
- **Rival-specific checks:** estimates should attenuate toward the null if current severity and improving trajectory explain the association; persist after hospital adjustment if the signal is patient-level monitoring mismatch; and disappear after EOL/discharge censoring if it reflects care-transition practice.
- No result from this observational design can establish that more testing would improve outcomes. A causal or interventional claim requires randomized monitoring strategies, expert adjudication, or another study.

## Evidence and references

[K1] A registered stepped-wedge trial is explicitly testing whether reducing arterial-blood-gas sampling, routine laboratory testing, and chest radiography changes 30-day mortality and related ICU outcomes, establishing clinical importance and unresolved safety. This proposal adds a patient-level eICU analysis of whether abrupt testing reduction during persistent instability predicts near-term deterioration, complementing hospital-level de-implementation trials.

[K2] A surgical-ICU quality initiative did not sustain reduced testing; phase-1 testing rose from 12.9 to 13.3 tests/patient-day with similar mortality, showing that practice change is difficult and aggregate safety results do not resolve patient-level risk. This proposal tests an observable high-risk subgroup—persistently unstable patients—rather than only hospital-wide testing intensity.

[K3] Dynamic ICU models can predict a clinically meaningful deterioration endpoint comprising vasopressor initiation and/or early death, showing that physiological trajectories contain short-term prognostic information. This proposal asks whether testing intensity adds practice-relevant information after conditioning on severity and trajectories, rather than optimizing another multimodal predictor.

Computationally checkable claims are the counts, exposure labels, outcome labels, model estimates, calibration, and sensitivity analyses above. Clinical adjudication is required for whether the structured ventilation and vasopressor labels represent the intended bedside interventions. A causal claim that increased testing would improve outcomes requires randomized monitoring strategies, expert adjudication, or another study.
