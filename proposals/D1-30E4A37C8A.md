# When monitoring falls behind observed instability: an observation-aware, hospital-transportable eICU study

## Scientific question and evidence boundary

The repaired parent asks whether early time-weighted hypotension predicts a later observed creatinine-rise signal beyond MAP nadir. This successor tests a different clinical and deployment uncertainty:

> Among adult ICU stays with observable follow-up, does an abrupt fall in recorded laboratory-result activity during a period of measured physiologic instability predict a subsequent *recorded ICU escalation* or unit death, and does adding this testing-process signal impair transportability to hospitals with different observation workflows?

This is an observational prognostic and transportability hypothesis. It is not a claim that clinicians ordered too few tests, that a laboratory result was clinically indicated, that missing results represent physiologic stability, or that reducing testing causes deterioration.

The strongest evidence established before fitting is only data availability: the frozen eICU 2.0 snapshot contains ICU-stay keyed laboratory result events, ICU-relative vital observations, infusion and respiratory-care event fields, unit disposition, hospital identifiers, and admission-severity descriptors. Direct source inspection verified the relevant headers and observed the disposition value maps; it did not estimate the hypothesis. The lab table records results, not orders, cancellations, clinician intent, interface uptime, or reasons for a gap.

The primary falsifiable hypothesis is that a process-augmented model will have a larger whole-hospital transport penalty than a matched clinical-state model that uses the same cohort, outcome, value channels, split and follow-up states but omits explicit testing-process features. A secondary, clinically interpretable hypothesis is that among intervals with measured instability and adequate pre/post lab opportunity, an abrupt reduction in recorded testing is associated with subsequent recorded escalation or unit death after adjustment for measured state and observation coverage. The primary claim is about prediction transport and the secondary claim about association with a database-observable process; neither is causal.

The substantive advance over the parent is to test whether apparent clinical risk is partly a property of how care is measured and whether that dependence survives hospital transport. The joint alternative can separate a latent value-based state from hospital-varying observation intensity. If successful, the result could change how an early-warning model is deployed and prompt a prospective audit of monitoring workflows. It cannot by itself establish that testing should be increased or that a patient experienced true physiologic deterioration.

## Population, time origin, and observation-aware outcomes

Use the frozen eICU 2.0 snapshot `[source checksum]`. The source catalog is `[internal dataset path]`, catalog [source checksum]. Sources are read-only; all normalized tables, audit outputs, fits and predictions are workspace artifacts.

Time zero is ICU admission. eICU temporal offsets are minutes relative to ICU admission. Use one patient row per `patientunitstayid`; repeated ICU stays remain separate. Parse patient `age` `> 89` as 90 and retain an age-censored indicator; include age >=18. Exclude negative event offsets from feature and outcome construction. Require nonmissing `patientunitstayid`, `hospitalid`, and `uniquepid`. Keep all stays sharing `uniquepid` in the same split and use a clustered sensitivity; do not merge stays into a single observation.

The primary landmark is 720 minutes. Eligibility requires:

1. The ICU stay has no unit death or alive unit discharge/transfer before 720 minutes, based on the audited unit-disposition rule below.
2. At least one result event with nonempty normalized `labname` in each comparison window [0,360] and [360,720]. Numeric values are not required for the process count, but value-based covariates require separately valid numeric rows.
3. At least two numeric `vitalPeriodic.systemicmean` observations with plausible values 20–200 mmHg in [360,720]. The state model may use additional observed `heartrate`, `respiration` and `sao2` values; their absence is represented as missing/coverage, not physiologic normality.
4. A defensible unit-discharge boundary or a documented boundary-missing sensitivity. A stay is not treated as observed after ICU discharge merely because a hospital-discharge row exists.

The primary outcome interval is (720,2160], i.e. 12–36 hours after ICU admission. Define mutually exclusive first states:

- recorded escalation: the earliest qualifying new vasoactive infusion or new ventilation start after 720;
- unit death: a qualifying ICU unit-discharge death before or at the end of observable follow-up;
- alive unit discharge/transfer or other unit observation loss before a recorded escalation;
- no recorded escalation while the ICU stay remains observable through 2160 minutes.

The primary analysis treats “recorded escalation” as a composite and reports vasoactive and ventilation components separately. A recorded infusion initiation or ventilation start is an operational event, not proof of shock, respiratory failure, clinician intent or disease deterioration.

For each stay, define the primary observable end as `min(2160, unitdischargeoffset)) when a nonnegative `unitdischargeoffset` exists. A unit discharge marked alive is observation loss, not a negative outcome. A unit discharge marked expired or with a death location is unit death. Print complete value maps for `unitdischargestatus` and `unitdischargelocation` before fitting. If disposition timing or status/location is missing, report the count and do not silently impute continued observation. A sensitivity may use hospital discharge fields, but it must be labelled a different, less defensible observation process because the longitudinal laboratory and vital tables are ICU-stay keyed.

A death or qualifying escalation must occur before alive discharge/observation loss to be counted in the primary observable process. A stay reaching 2160 while still in the ICU and with no event is observable no-event follow-up. For competing-risk reporting, provide cause-specific hazards and cumulative incidence for escalation, unit death and unit observation loss. Do not call the endpoint “clinical deterioration,” “organ failure,” or “AKI.”

The reduced-testing secondary analysis uses repeated six-hour risk intervals with landmarks 720, 1080, 1440 and 1800, each predicting the first event in the following 12-hour interval, with 720 primary and later landmarks as replication sensitivities. A landmark is eligible only when its preceding and comparison windows are within the unit-observable interval and the event has not occurred. Repeated intervals from one stay are clustered and all intervals from one `uniquepid` remain in the same split. End-of-life care-plan records are used only for a prespecified sensitivity/exclusion audit, never as proof of treatment limitation.

## Estimand and analysis

The primary transport estimand is the difference in held-out-hospital performance between two pre-landmark models for the same competing-event process. For each model, report hospital-macro-averaged Brier score and calibration slope/intercept over 12–36 hours, plus the paired difference (process-enriched minus clinical-state) with hospital-resampling uncertainty. The directional hypothesis is a larger positive transport penalty for the process-enriched model, defined as worse held-out Brier/calibration relative to its mixed-hospital training/validation performance. Predefine a clinically material threshold for interpretation before fitting, based on a decision-curve or absolute-risk scale rather than selecting it after results. AUROC/AUPRC are secondary because calibration and clinical utility are central to this question.

The secondary association estimand is the standardized 12-hour cumulative incidence of recorded escalation or unit death for an abrupt testing reduction versus no reduction, among the measured-unstable eligible intervals, holding the same pre-interval clinical-value summaries, baseline risk, coverage and hospital context fixed. Report risk difference, risk ratio where estimable, and cause-specific/subdistribution sensitivity estimates. State clearly that standardization is prognostic adjustment, not a causal intervention on testing.

Use 30-minute discrete-time intervals from each landmark to 2160, with cause-specific pooled logistic models and explicit competing states. No feature may use a result, vital, infusion, respiratory or disposition event occurring after the prediction interval begins. A primary process-free baseline contains:

- admission demographics, source and unit context;
- hospital context and training-hospital effect rule;
- six-hour MAP, heart rate, respiration and SaO2 value summaries, counts and coverage;
- numeric lab-value summaries from prespecified analyte families, their value-missing indicators and lab coverage;
- admission severity sensitivity variables.

It excludes explicit lab result counts, distinct-analyte counts, test-free gaps, panel identity, result recency, revision-process indicators and hospital observation-intensity terms. Shared missing-value indicators for clinical values are retained so the comparison does not confound process dependence with an arbitrary complete-case rule.

The nested process-enriched model adds only the prespecified testing features: result-event count, distinct-name count, family count, numeric-result count, longest test-free gap, result recency, revision indicators and panel composition. It is fit with the identical cohort, outcome, observation boundary, clinical values, split and hospital-effect rule. Compare standardized risks at 5%, 10%, 20% and 30% decision thresholds only if those thresholds have adequate event support; otherwise report instability.

For the secondary reduced-testing analysis, fit a prespecified exposure coefficient in the clinical-state-adjusted discrete-time model, then test its interaction with hospital region/teaching/bed category only as exploratory heterogeneity. The primary hospital comparison is held-out performance, not a ranking of hospitals.

### Mechanistic alternative

Fit a joint discrete-time latent-state/observation-intensity model using exactly the same landmark cohort, windows, source rows, feature construction, competing states, patient grouping and hospital holdouts. Use three prespecified latent clinical states—stable, unstable and critical—with transitions driven by the value-based vital summaries, numeric lab values, admission covariates and coverage indicators. Separately model named lab-result counts and distinct-analyte counts using a hurdle or negative-binomial observation-intensity component with hospital-varying effects. Model escalation, unit death and observation loss as state- and hospital-dependent cause-specific transitions.

The required outputs are:

1. state posterior probabilities and transition estimates;
2. hospital-specific observation-intensity estimates;
3. the testing-reduction association conditional on latent state;
4. standardized state-specific and overall 12-hour cumulative incidence;
5. a model diagnostic replacing each hospital’s fitted observation intensity with a common training-hospital reference intensity, without calling this a treatment effect; and
6. held-out-hospital calibration/Brier results for the joint model.

This alternative reveals whether the baseline’s apparent testing signal is compatible with a latent severity trajectory, a hospital-specific measurement process, or both. The scalar baseline cannot distinguish these explanations. Support for a process-dependent transport problem is stronger if the process-enriched model loses calibration/Brier performance at held-out hospitals and the joint model attributes substantial variation to observation intensity while retaining clinically meaningful state information. If the testing coefficient vanishes after latent-state adjustment, the evidence favors confounding by measured/unmeasured clinical state rather than a distinct workflow signal.

A GRU or temporal-convolution model is deferred. It could use the same six-hour binned value and observation channels, but it is not scientifically necessary before the latent observation model tests the proposed mechanism. Revisit only if the joint model converges, leaves reproducible within-hospital temporal residuals in multiple held-out hospitals, and a bounded CPU/GPU probe supports stable calibration under the identical target and split. An unrun learned model cannot support a conclusion.

## Splitting, uncertainty and leakage controls

Freeze the split before fitting: hold out entire hospitals, with a prespecified repeated-hospital sensitivity. Group all stays and repeated landmarks sharing `uniquepid` in the same partition. Fit normalization, analyte-family mapping, imputation, regularization, hospital effects and latent-state parameters using training hospitals only. For an unseen hospital, use the prespecified training population intercept/transport rule and report the site-specific calibration shift; never estimate a fixed effect from held-out outcomes.

Use hospital-level bootstrap/resampling for intervals around transport differences and clustered patient/uniquepid resampling for repeated-landmark association sensitivities. Report the number of hospitals, stays, intervals and events contributed to every estimate. Handle missing values with a frozen rule established from the training data; missing is not normal. Report complete-case and coverage-restricted sensitivities without replacing the primary observation-aware analysis.

Primary sensitivities are: (1) process exposure as count ratio versus binary >=50% reduction; (2) no interpolation of vital values; (3) stricter MAP/vital plausibility ranges; (4) lab result revision handling; (5) numeric-only versus any-named lab events; (6) removal of hospitals with unstable interface-like naming patterns; (7) unit-boundary versus hospital-discharge boundary, labelled as different observability; (8) exclusion or stratification by care-plan EOL record; (9) noninvasive-MAP sensitivity; (10) escalation components separately; and (11) removal of any single hospital or region. A post-landmark lab-complete-case analysis is secondary and explicitly vulnerable to informative testing and discharge.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive transport evidence requires a process-enriched model that performs acceptably or better in mixed hospitals but has a reproducible, clinically material held-out-hospital worsening in calibration or Brier score versus the matched process-free baseline, not merely a small AUROC difference. The pattern must persist across hospital resamples, not be explained by one site, and survive predeclared name normalization, revision, coverage, disposition and complete-case sensitivities. The latent alternative should show whether the effect is attributable to observation intensity and should remain calibrated enough to support interpretation.

Supportive reduced-testing evidence requires a positive adjusted association with subsequent recorded escalation/unit death among measured-unstable intervals, component directionally compatible with the composite, uncertainty excluding the null or clinically material threshold, and replication at later landmarks and held-out hospitals. This still supports only a database-observable association.

Adverse evidence includes equal or better held-out calibration for the process-enriched model, no transport penalty, a null/reversed reduced-testing association, disappearance after coverage/interface-like hospital exclusion or disposition sensitivity, or an association explained by latent state. These results would favor a clinical-state or site-specific observation explanation; they do not prove that monitoring is irrelevant or that reduced testing is safe.

Inconclusive evidence includes too few eligible intervals or events, sparse hospital support, unstable analyte normalization, ventilation onset ambiguity, missing unit-discharge timing, nonconvergence of the joint model, intervals spanning clinically important benefit and harm, or a signal dominated by one hospital/region. Do not convert such results into support or harm.

Computationally checkable claims include source hashes/headers, table and column use, value maps, name normalization, duplicate handling, cohort and interval flow, event onset rules, observation boundaries, split integrity, fitted contrasts, calibration/Brier/AUROC/AUPRC, decision curves, convergence and sensitivity outputs. Clinical adjudication or another study is required for true deterioration, test appropriateness, test orders/cancellations, interface completeness, end-of-life treatment limitation, staffing/workflow causes, patient preferences, causal effects of testing, and any recommendation to change monitoring. External validation requires a new dataset with known observation infrastructure.
