# Provisional eICU hypothesis: monitoring gaps during persistent instability

Status: provisional, untested design checkpoint. No cohort result, causal effect, or clinical recommendation is claimed.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a continuous ICU stay who remain physiologically unstable at a prespecified landmark, a reduction in laboratory-testing intensity during the preceding 12 hours is associated with higher risk of a time-local respiratory or vasopressor/infusion deterioration in the following 12 hours, beyond contemporaneously measured severity and recent support. The association should attenuate when end-of-life documentation, discharge/transfer context, and support de-escalation are handled explicitly. A residual association in held-out hospitals would be a monitoring-process signal, not proof that changing laboratory orders causes deterioration.

## Why this matters

This distinguishes two clinically different explanations: (1) reduced monitoring identifies a patient whose instability is under-observed, versus (2) clinicians reduce testing because the patient is recovering, being transitioned, dying, or in a low-intensity end-of-life trajectory. Resolving which pattern dominates could inform prospective monitoring-process review, while avoiding an unsupported claim that “less testing causes worse outcomes.”

## Falsifiable test and interpretation

At each eligible hourly landmark, define exposure as a prespecified drop in the number/breadth of qualifying laboratory observations over the preceding 12 hours, conditional on a minimum observation history. Define the primary outcome as a prespecified respiratory or vasopressor/infusion deterioration in the next 12 hours. Treat death, discharge/transfer, and documented end-of-life/de-escalation as competing transitions rather than silently censoring them. Compare an adjusted discrete-time baseline with a longitudinal recurrent-event alternative on the same question; the alternative should reveal whether the signal depends on trajectory and observation process rather than a single landmark.

Supportive result: a reproducible exposure-outcome association remains after severity, support, transition, and EOL/documentation adjustment, with directionally consistent held-out-hospital validation and negative-control checks. Adverse result: the association disappears or reverses after these adjustments, or negative controls show the same pattern. Inconclusive result: sparse observations, unstable endpoint definitions, wide uncertainty, or nontransportability prevent a decision. None of these establishes causality; clinical adjudication and prospective validation would be required for intervention claims.
