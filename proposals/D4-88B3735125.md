# Provisional UK Biobank hypothesis: discordant HbA1c rise and weight trajectory

## Status

This is a provisional, explicitly unverified hypothesis and a repair of the prior episode’s restart after the pancreatic-cancer direction failed its zero-event feasibility gate. No full cohort analysis has been run here, and no result is claimed. The exact repeated-measure fields and UKB tables are available in the configured catalog; incident-diabetes event support, ascertainment details, and the required three inspected key works remain to be verified.

## Proposed relationship

Among UK Biobank participants with two assessment-center visits and no diabetes diagnosis recorded before the second visit, a similar rise in HbA1c is associated with higher subsequent incident-diabetes risk when accompanied by weight loss than when accompanied by weight gain. The unresolved clinical question is whether the same biochemical worsening should be interpreted differently when it co-occurs with weight loss, rather than treated as one diabetes-risk state.

This is a predictive/associational question, not a causal claim. It could inform whether a patient’s worsening glycemia merits closer follow-up or evaluation for an underlying illness, but it would not establish that weight loss causes diabetes or that a treatment should be changed.

## Clinical importance and advance

A simple HbA1c-risk model treats a given HbA1c increase as interchangeable regardless of the person’s concurrent weight trajectory. The proposed interaction tests whether weight trajectory changes the clinical meaning of glycemic change. A positive result would identify a subgroup in whom the same biochemical signal may warrant different follow-up intensity; a null result would support treating the HbA1c rise more uniformly in this data. Either outcome is useful for deciding whether a longitudinal risk rule is worth external validation.

The advance over the prior branch is a common, clinically consequential metabolic transition with a prespecified interaction, rather than a rare cancer endpoint that had no observed events in the available outcome field.

## Population and temporal design

- Start at the second assessment-center visit, using the earliest available pair of visits with both HbA1c and BMI/weight measurements and a valid assessment date.
- Exclude participants with a recorded diabetes diagnosis before or on the second-visit date, using the available diagnosis fields and dates; define the exact ICD-10 code list and date rule before fitting.
- Require follow-up after the second-visit date. Define incident diabetes from the earliest post-baseline diagnosis date in the available inpatient/outcome records, with a prespecified lag (for example, exclude events in the first 90 days to reduce reverse-causation from symptoms or recent diagnosis) and censor at death or end of available follow-up.
- Use the same person’s first and second measurements for HbA1c and weight; do not substitute cross-sectional values from unrelated participants.
- Preserve the UKB identifier namespace and join only documented ukb672073 tables on eid. Do not join the Olink ukb671626 namespace by matching IDs.

## Variables and estimand

- Primary exposure: change in HbA1c from visit 1 to visit 2, standardized per SD or prespecified clinical increment, restricted to a range with adequate support.
- Effect modifier: change in weight/BMI over the same interval, categorized only after checking overlap (for example, loss versus gain/stability), or modeled continuously with an interaction.
- Outcome: first incident diabetes diagnosis after the second visit, with the 90-day lag and follow-up rules fixed before looking at estimates.
- Covariates: age, sex, baseline HbA1c, baseline weight/BMI, time between visits, assessment center, smoking/alcohol if available, and relevant medication fields if available. Do not adjust for post-baseline measurements.
- Primary estimand: the difference in adjusted 5-year incident-diabetes risk (or hazard ratio) for a prespecified HbA1c rise between the weight-loss and weight-gain/stability groups, with confidence intervals. Report absolute risks as well as ratios.

## Baseline versus substantive alternative

Simple baseline: a penalized Cox or discrete-time survival model with baseline HbA1c, baseline weight/BMI, age, sex, center, and visit interval, predicting incident diabetes. This estimates the conventional level-based risk and provides calibration and discrimination.

Substantive alternative: a longitudinal two-measure model using each person’s HbA1c change and weight change, with a prespecified interaction and restricted cubic splines or a prespecified spline basis; compare it with the baseline using the same patient-level train/validation/test split, time-dependent censoring, and held-out calibration, discrimination, and decision-curve/absolute-risk outputs. The alternative can reveal whether the direction and size of change carry information that a single baseline level loses, and whether weight trajectory changes the interpretation of glycemic change. It does not by itself identify a mechanism.

A mechanistic interpretation (for example, illness-related weight loss, insulin resistance, or treatment effects) would require additional clinical adjudication, medication history, repeated clinical context, and ideally external validation. It is not inferable from this association alone.

## Analysis and falsification

1. Audit missingness, visit timing, overlap of HbA1c-change and weight-change groups, and the number of incident-diabetes events before fitting. If event support is inadequate, stop rather than extrapolate.
2. Fit the baseline and alternative on the training split only; use validation to choose only prespecified regularization/spline choices, then evaluate once on the held-out test split.
3. Report interaction estimates with confidence intervals, calibration intercept/slope, discrimination, and absolute risk differences. Use bootstrap or cluster-robust uncertainty appropriate to the design.
4. Falsification/negative controls: repeat the analysis with a non-diabetes outcome (for example, a prespecified nonmetabolic inpatient diagnosis) and with a negative temporal control using outcomes before the second visit. A similar interaction for unrelated outcomes would weaken the claim of diabetes-specific information.
5. Sensitivity analyses: vary the 90-day lag, exclude participants with recent medication or illness indicators if available, require longer measurement intervals, and repeat in a complete-case subset. These are sensitivity checks, not opportunities to select a favorable result.

## Interpretation

- Supportive: the held-out interaction is directionally consistent, statistically precise enough to exclude a clinically trivial effect, improves calibration or absolute-risk discrimination over the baseline, and is not reproduced by the negative controls. This would justify external validation and expert review of a longitudinal risk rule.
- Adverse: no interaction, a reversed interaction, poor calibration, or a comparable signal for unrelated outcomes would argue against treating weight trajectory as a meaningful modifier in this population.
- Inconclusive: wide intervals, few events, poor overlap, or unstable results across timing/sensitivity analyses would not refute the hypothesis; it would justify more data or a different design.

The experiment can establish only an association and predictive information in this UKB subset. It cannot establish causality, clinical utility in practice, or a biological mechanism without adjudicated clinical data, medication/treatment context, external validation, and prospective evaluation.
