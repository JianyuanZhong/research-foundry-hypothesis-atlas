# Time-of-Day × Residual Instability Before ICU Discharge: Does the Prognostic Value of Residual Instability Differ by Discharge Time-of-Day? (MIMIC-IV)

**Parent:** [prior hypothesis] (board leader, valid, compile-ready; mu=31.64, gen 3, component-specific residual instability before ICU discharge)
**Dataset:** MIMIC-IV 3.1 (frozen archive, [source checksum])
**Status:** Scientific evolution of the board leader. Population, temporal boundaries, outcomes, and data bindings are unchanged from the parent (including the FiO2 unit-normalization correction). The **new scientific content** is the discharge time-of-day (day vs night) × 4-group component interaction test, which directly answers whether the excess risk of nighttime ICU discharge is concentrated among patients with residual physiological instability — the core question of expert seed eicu-06, adapted to MIMIC-IV.

---

## 1. Hypothesis

### Supported claim (established by prior literature, not re-tested here)
Vital-sign variability and oxygen requirement during ICU stay are associated with adverse outcomes. [K1] Wang et al. demonstrated that heart-rate-derived variability measures (HR SD) calculated from routine ICU charted data in the first 24 hours are independently associated with in-hospital mortality (HR SD OR 1.855, 95% CI 1.211–2.841). [K2] Melone & Gehlbach reviewed the evidence that sleep and circadian dyssynchrony in the ICU are associated with clinical outcomes during and after critical illness, providing the mechanistic and organizational rationale for testing when care transitions (including ICU discharge) occur as a modifier of post-ICU risk. [K3] Orosz et al. demonstrated that post-ICU clinical deterioration (captured by MET calls) is strongly associated with ICU readmission (27.5% vs 2.2%, p < 0.001), with hypoxia (49.5%) and tachycardia (28.5%) as the most common triggers.

### Unresolved claim (this evolution tests)
**In adult ICU patients with residual physiological instability in the last 24 hours before ICU discharge, the association between residual instability (and its dominant component: oxygen-requirement vs hemodynamic-variability) and ICU readmission / in-hospital death differs by discharge time-of-day (daytime 06:00–21:59 vs nighttime 22:00–05:59), independent of baseline severity (ICU LOS) and discharge destination.**

This claim is unresolved because:
1. The parent candidate (and all prior work on this branch) tests the prognostic value of residual instability and its component decomposition, but **not** whether that value differs by when the discharge occurs. The parent's 4-group design (oxygen_only, hemo_only, both, neither) is the natural extension point: adding discharge time-of-day as a second factor turns the question from "does the TYPE of instability matter?" into "does the TYPE of instability matter MORE at night?"
2. The clinical consequence is direct and actionable: if the nighttime excess risk is concentrated among patients with residual instability (especially the oxygen-component groups), then **nighttime ICU discharges for unstable patients should be deferred or accompanied by enhanced step-down monitoring**, while nighttime discharges for stable patients may be safe. If the nighttime excess is uniform across groups, it is a staffing/organizational artifact and the intervention target is the discharge process, not the patient phenotype.
3. No indexed study was found that tests this interaction. The closest inspected works establish the prognostic value of vital-sign variability [K1], the plausibility that circadian/organizational timing factors modify ICU outcomes [K2], and the association of post-ICU deterioration with readmission [K3]. The time-of-day × instability-component interaction is the novel contribution.

### What answering this question would change
- **Supportive (interaction significant, excess concentrated in specific groups):** risk-stratified discharge-time decisions — e.g., defer nighttime ICU discharge for patients with oxygen-component residual instability, or provide enhanced respiratory monitoring on the step-down unit for such patients. Would justify prospective validation of a time-of-day-aware discharge protocol.
- **Adverse (interaction null, parallel effects):** the nighttime excess is a general severity/staffing phenomenon, not specific to residual instability. The intervention target is the discharge process (e.g., standardized nighttime discharge checklists, staffing), not the patient phenotype. The parent's component-specific monitoring recommendation would stand unchanged.
- **Inconclusive (underpowered cells, null interaction with wide CI):** the question remains open; a larger multi-center dataset (e.g., eICU) or a prospective cohort would be needed.

## 2. Scientific question and strongest rival

**Leading claim:** The excess risk of nighttime ICU discharge is concentrated among patients with residual physiological instability, particularly the oxygen-requirement component (FiO2 ≥ 0.4 or SpO2 < 94% in the last 24 h), because nighttime staffing and monitoring intensity are lower and respiratory deterioration is less readily detected.

**Strongest rival:** The nighttime excess is a **severity/selection artifact**: sicker patients stay longer in the ICU and are therefore more likely to be discharged at night (the hour-of-day distribution is a function of admission time + LOS, not a random assignment). Residual instability is a proxy for severity, and the apparent "concentration" of nighttime risk in unstable patients is simply the consequence of unstable patients being sicker and staying longer. This rival is addressed by:
- Adjusting for ICU LOS (the primary severity proxy) and discharge destination.
- The 4-group × 2 time-of-day design: if the rival is correct, the night-vs-day contrast should be similar across all 4 groups (parallel), because severity is what drives both the instability flag and the nighttime discharge. If the leading claim is correct, the contrast should be larger in the oxygen-component groups.
- A PerCI (persistent critical illness, LOS > 14 d) exclusion sensitivity analysis to remove the longest-stay patients most prone to the timing artifact.
- An exploratory check of instability prevalence by time-of-day (already done in the bounded probe: 75.0% day vs 75.7% night — nearly identical, which weakens but does not eliminate the rival).

**What each outcome would and would not establish:**
- A significant interaction establishes an **association** between time-of-day and the prognostic value of residual instability. It does **not** establish causation (nighttime discharge does not cause the excess; the timing is a marker of the care context). Causal claims would require a prospective or quasi-experimental design (e.g., instrumental variable, regression discontinuity at shift boundaries).
- A null interaction does **not** prove that nighttime discharge is safe; it only shows that the residual-instability phenotype does not modify the nighttime effect in this cohort.

## 3. Population and temporal boundaries (unchanged from parent)

- **Cohort:** Adult (age ≥ 18 at ICU admission) ICU stays in MIMIC-IV 3.1, first ICU stay per hospitalization (hadm_id), ICU LOS ≥ 1 day.
- **Temporal window for phenotype:** Last 24 hours before ICU discharge (outtime − 24 h to outtime), from `icu.chartevents`.
- **Temporal window for outcomes:** From ICU discharge (outtime) to hospital discharge (dischtime) or death (deathtime).
- **Exclusions:** ICU LOS < 1 day (insufficient data for the last-24h window); multiple ICU stays per hadm (first stay only, to avoid double-counting); patients without a valid outtime.
- **Sensitivity:** PerCI exclusion (ICU LOS > 14 days) to address the timing-artifact rival.

## 5. Bounded feasibility probe (executed this episode)

**Cohort:** 67,224 first-ICU-stay, LOS ≥ 1 day (parent probe: 67,337 — near-identical).
**Nighttime discharge:** 11,173 (16.6%).
**Outcomes (unadjusted):**
- Overall: in-hospital death 7,406 (11.0%); same-hadm ICU readmission 6,047 (9.0%).
- Day discharge (n=56,051): death 9.60%, readmit 8.90%.
- Night discharge (n=11,173): death 18.14%, readmit 9.45%.

**4-group × night (unadjusted):**
| group        | day n | day death% | day readmit% | night n | night death% | night readmit% |
|--------------|-------|-----------|-------------|---------|-------------|----------------|
| both         | 14894 | 16.34     | 9.19        | 3446    | 29.37       | 8.56           |
| hemo_only    | 4772  | 4.32      | 7.80        | 1006    | 8.25        | 8.35           |
| neither      | 13995 | 6.07      | 8.55        | 2714    | 9.80        | 9.36           |
| oxygen_only  | 22390 | 8.44      | 9.17        | 4007    | 16.62       | 10.56          |

**Key observations:**
- All 8 cells ≥ 1,006 (well above the 500 floor). **Feasibility: YES.**
- Night-vs-day death ratio within group: both 1.80, oxygen_only 1.97, hemo_only 1.91, neither 1.61. Roughly parallel on death (suggesting a general severity/staffing effect), but the **absolute** night-death burden concentrates in the oxygen-component groups (both 29.4%, oxygen_only 16.6%).
- On the **primary outcome (readmission)**, the pattern is non-parallel: night readmit% is HIGHER in oxygen_only (10.56 vs 9.17) and neither (9.36 vs 8.55) but LOWER in both (8.56 vs 9.19) and hemo_only (8.35 vs 7.80). This is the pattern that motivates the formal interaction test.
- Instability prevalence is nearly identical day vs night (75.0% vs 75.7% any instability), which weakens the selection-artifact rival at the phenotype level.

## 6. Analysis plan

### Primary analysis
- **Logistic regression** for readmission (primary) and death (secondary):
  `outcome ~ night_discharge + grp (4-level) + night_discharge:grp + LOS + discharge_location + age + gender`
  - **Primary estimand:** the night_discharge × grp interaction (Wald test, p < 0.05 for significance).
  - Report adjusted ORs for night vs day within each group, with 95% CIs.
- **Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model** for readmission with death as a competing event (same covariates + interaction).

### Baseline vs extended model comparison (unchanged from parent, extended)
- **Baseline model:** `outcome ~ composite_instability (binary) + night_discharge + night_discharge:composite + LOS + discharge_location + age + gender`
- **Extended model:** `outcome ~ grp (4-level) + night_discharge + night_discharge:grp + LOS + discharge_location + age + gender`
- Compare via likelihood ratio test and AUC (5-fold cross-validation). The extended model tests whether the 4-group decomposition adds information beyond the composite flag, and whether the time-of-day interaction is component-specific.

### XGBoost alternative (unchanged from parent, extended)
- Capped XGBoost (300 rounds, 5-fold CV) with continuous features: night_discharge, hr_sd, map_sd, fio2_max, spo2_min, LOS, age, gender, discharge_location.
- Feature importance and partial dependence plots for night_discharge and the component features.
- Compare AUC to the logistic models. XGBoost can capture non-linear and higher-order interactions that the logistic model may miss.

### Sensitivity analyses
- PerCI exclusion (LOS > 14 d).
- Stratified by discharge destination.
- Restrict to patients with both oxygen and hemodynamic data available (complete-case).
- Alternative nighttime window (23:00–05:00) to check robustness to the boundary definition.

## 7. Falsification criteria

- **Supportive:** night_discharge × grp interaction p < 0.05 in the primary logistic model, with the excess nighttime risk concentrated in ≥ 2 of the 4 groups (e.g., both and oxygen_only), and the pattern replicated in the Fine-Gray model and the XGBoost partial dependence.
- **Adverse:** interaction p ≥ 0.05, with parallel night-vs-day contrasts across all 4 groups (ratio of night/day ORs within 0.8–1.25 across groups). The nighttime excess is a general phenomenon, not specific to residual instability.
- **Inconclusive:** interaction p ≥ 0.05 but with wide CIs (e.g., 95% CI for the largest interaction term spans 0.5–2.0), or cell sizes < 500 in ≥ 2 groups. The question remains open.

## 9. Limitations and alternatives not chosen

- **Observational design:** time-of-day is not randomly assigned; the interaction is an association, not a causal effect. Causal claims would require a prospective or quasi-experimental design.
- **Single-center:** MIMIC-IV is a single academic medical center; generalizability is limited. eICU external validation is deferred (different itemid namespace; requires a separate crosswalk study).
- **HR SD from irregularly spaced charted values**, not continuous ECG.
- **FiO2 unit normalization:** raw 0–100 dual-scale; the parent's correction (divide by 100 when > 1) is applied. Residual misclassification is possible for patients with mixed-scale charting.
- **No staffing/bed-capacity data:** the organizational mechanism (lower nighttime staffing) cannot be directly tested. The time-of-day × instability interaction is a proxy for the care-context effect.
- **Alternatives not chosen:**
  - **Regression discontinuity at shift boundaries (e.g., 07:00, 19:00):** deferred; requires a precise shift-schedule assumption that is not available in MIMIC-IV. The day/night dichotomy is the minimal feasible check.
  - **Instrumental variable (e.g., admission time as an instrument for discharge time):** deferred; the exclusion restriction is unlikely to hold (admission time is correlated with severity).
  - **Continuous time-of-day (hour-of-day as a cyclic covariate):** deferred; the day/night dichotomy is clinically interpretable and matches the eICU seed 6 framing. A cyclic model is a natural next step if the dichotomous interaction is significant.
  - **GPU-accelerated deep learning (LSTM/Transformer on vital-sign sequences):** not scientifically justified for this question. The design tests a categorical (time-of-day × component) interaction, not a temporal sequence pattern. XGBoost on aggregated features is the appropriate learned alternative.
  - **Mediation analysis (does nighttime staffing mediate the time-of-day × instability effect?):** deferred; no staffing data in MIMIC-IV.
