# Provisional eICU monitoring-gap hypothesis

## Proposed relationship

Among adult ICU stays with at least two qualifying laboratory observations, an abrupt reduction in the frequency of clinically relevant laboratory testing during persistent physiologic instability is associated with higher subsequent 24-hour risk of acute deterioration, after accounting for contemporaneous severity and recent testing history. Hospital-level variation is secondary and descriptive, not causal.

This is a provisional observational hypothesis. The available evidence establishes that eICU contains longitudinal laboratory and physiologic measurements; it does not establish that reduced testing causes deterioration.

## Why it matters

If robust and not explained by the strongest rival below, this could identify a monitoring-process signal worth prospective review. If it disappears after longitudinal adjustment, the apparent gap is likely severity, timing, discharge, or documentation behavior. A negative or imprecise result would constrain claims that “less testing means worse outcomes” and support a measurement/process study rather than a treatment intervention.

## Population and temporal design

- Unit: one ICU stay, `patientunitstayid`; repeated-stay sensitivity uses `uniquepid`.
- Eligibility: adult age representation in `patient.age`, at least two qualifying laboratory observations, and a prespecified index at least 12 hours after ICU admission and 12 hours before discharge/transfer. Fix the adult threshold and minimum laboratory count before outcome inspection.
- Index: a 12-hour rolling landmark, repeated only while the patient remains in ICU and has no qualifying deterioration in the preceding 12 hours. This avoids conditioning on a future outcome.
- Exposure: in the 12 hours before each index, count qualifying laboratory rows and derive the inter-test interval. “Abrupt reduction” is a prespecified contrast with the patient’s preceding interval/history; the threshold must be fixed from the exposure distribution before looking at deterioration. Prefer a continuous interval model.
- Outcome: a prespecified 24-hour deterioration endpoint after the index, using available physiologic measurements and discharge/transfer status. Define it before analysis, for example new/worsening hypotension, hypoxemia, respiratory-support, or ICU-discharge event; final operationalization requires clinical adjudication of cross-hospital comparability.
- Competing events: ICU discharge, transfer, death, and end-of-life documentation are handled explicitly as competing or censoring events. They are not ordinary follow-up.
- Follow-up: no outcome observation after discharge/transfer; index times near discharge are excluded or censored as specified.

## Strongest rival explanation

The strongest rival is that apparent monitoring gaps mark changing clinical state, care transition, or documentation practice: a patient may be stabilized, transferred, discharged, near death, or subject to a local ordering/interface workflow that changes testing frequency. Clinicians may also reduce testing because they judge the patient stable, making exposure a consequence rather than a cause. The design compares each patient’s prior testing history with current testing, conditions on contemporaneous severity and recent trajectory, excludes/censors discharge and transfer, and treats hospital variation as descriptive. A robust adjusted association still would not identify a mechanism or prove that increasing tests improves outcomes.

## Baseline versus longitudinal alternative

Baseline: a prespecified conventional model using current 12-hour laboratory count/interval, current vital-sign summary, age, admission type, unit type, and hospital as fixed covariates to estimate the 24-hour deterioration association. It answers whether the contemporaneous gap is associated after basic severity adjustment, but loses the patient’s prior monitoring trajectory and can conflate a one-off gap with a sustained change.

Longitudinal alternative: a time-aware model or discrete-time survival analysis using preceding laboratory intervals, recent physiologic trajectories, elapsed ICU time, prior index landmarks, and hospital random effects. It can reveal whether the signal is specific to an abrupt change from a patient’s own history, whether it persists after accounting for evolving severity, and whether it varies with time since admission. It may expose informative observation and competing-event structure that the baseline discards.

The alternative is scientifically useful only if it changes interpretation: persistence after own-history and severity adjustment strengthens a monitoring-process signal; disappearance favors the rival explanation. The deliverable is the fitted association and uncertainty, not a small predictive improvement.

## Falsification and interpretation

- Supportive: a prespecified positive association for abrupt testing reduction persists in the longitudinal model, is not confined to discharge/transfer or EOL strata, and remains directionally consistent across hospital-held-out validation. This supports an association worth clinical review, not causality.
- Adverse: the association reverses, becomes null with adequate precision, or is explained by prior trajectory, discharge/transfer, EOL, or hospital workflow. This argues against interpreting a raw testing gap as a monitoring-process signal.
- Inconclusive: wide uncertainty, sparse eligible landmarks, or inability to define a comparable deterioration endpoint. This does not refute the hypothesis; it identifies the need for a narrower endpoint, more data, or clinical adjudication.
- Falsification checks: compare with a placebo exposure window before the index time, test whether the association appears for a prespecified nonclinical documentation proxy, and assess whether results change when using laboratory count versus inter-test interval. A result only present in the placebo or proxy analysis is evidence against the proposed interpretation.
