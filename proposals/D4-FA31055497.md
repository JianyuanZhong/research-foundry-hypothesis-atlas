# Earlier natural menopause and cardiac structural remodelling beyond measured haemodynamic load

## Scientific question and clinical importance

Among UK Biobank women with self-reported natural menopause, does earlier age at menopause remain associated with left-ventricular myocardial mass and left-atrial structure/function after adjustment for age, body composition, and measured blood-pressure load?

The clinically important unresolved question is whether menopause timing marks a subgroup with earlier subclinical cardiac structural change, or whether the apparent association is explained by residual haemodynamic load and shared metabolic ageing. This matters because menopause timing itself is not modifiable, but if it identifies a subgroup with earlier cardiac remodelling, it could motivate earlier cardiovascular surveillance and risk-factor control.

The leading explanation is that earlier natural menopause is associated with higher cumulative blood pressure and/or adverse body composition, which in turn drives left-ventricular hypertrophy and left-atrial remodelling. The strongest rival is a sex-hormone-related biological transition: loss of oestrogen may independently alter cardiac structure. The available data cannot distinguish these mechanisms causally, but the adjustment hierarchy can test whether the association persists after accounting for measured haemodynamic and metabolic factors.

## Evidence and closest prior work

[K1] The closest prior work is a large cohort study examining the association between age at menopause and cardiovascular disease outcomes. It establishes that earlier menopause is associated with higher cardiovascular risk, but does not specifically examine cardiac structural remodelling as measured by cardiac MRI. Our proposal adds a direct examination of left-ventricular myocardial mass and left-atrial structure/function, which are subclinical markers that may precede clinical cardiovascular events.

[K2] A second relevant work examines the association between body composition and cardiac structure in postmenopausal women. It establishes that visceral fat is associated with left-ventricular hypertrophy, but does not specifically examine the role of menopause timing. Our proposal adds the explicit adjustment for body composition and tests whether the menopause timing association persists after accounting for these factors.

[K3] A third relevant work examines the association between blood pressure and cardiac structure in a large population-based cohort. It establishes that higher blood pressure is associated with left-ventricular hypertrophy, but does not specifically examine the role of menopause timing. Our proposal adds the explicit adjustment for measured blood-pressure load and tests whether the menopause timing association persists after accounting for these factors.

## Study design

### Population

UK Biobank women with self-reported natural menopause (field 2724 = 1, field 3581 available). Exclusion criteria: age < 40 at assessment, missing cardiac MRI data, missing blood pressure data, or missing body composition data.

### Temporal boundaries

Baseline is the date of attending assessment centre (field 53). Menopause timing is self-reported at baseline. Cardiac MRI measures are obtained at baseline. Follow-up is not required for the primary analysis, which is cross-sectional. Sensitivity analyses will examine whether the association differs by time since menopause.

### Variables

**Exposure:** Age at natural menopause (field 3581), continuous, analysed per 5-year earlier menopause.

**Outcomes:**
- Left-ventricular myocardial mass (field 3582, cardiac MRI)
- Left-atrial volume (field 3582, cardiac MRI)
- Left-atrial emptying fraction (field 3582, cardiac MRI)

**Covariates:**
- Age (field 51)
- Height (field 502)
- Weight (field 52)
- BMI (field 40)
- Visceral fat volume (field 21085, body MRI)
- Systolic blood pressure (field 52)
- Diastolic blood pressure (field 53)
- Heart rate (field 58)
- HRT use (field 723)
- HRT start age (field 3536)
- HRT stop age (field 3546)
- Smoking status (field 718)
- Alcohol consumption (field 797)
- Physical activity (field 1003)
- Diabetes (field 48)
- Hypertension (field 60)
- Medications (field 809)

### Adjustment hierarchy

**Model 0:** Unadjusted

**Model 1:** Age, height, weight

**Model 2:** Model 1 + BMI, visceral fat volume

**Model 3:** Model 2 + systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate

**Model 4:** Model 3 + HRT use, HRT start age, HRT stop age, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, medications

### Analysis

Primary analysis: linear regression of each cardiac outcome on age at menopause (per 5-year earlier), with the adjustment hierarchy above. Report coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values for each model.

Sensitivity analyses:
- Restrict to women with time since menopause > 5 years
- Restrict to women without HRT use
- Restrict to women without diabetes or hypertension
- Use restricted cubic splines to examine nonlinear associations
- Use gradient-boosted models with held-out evaluation to test for nonlinear effects and interactions

### Falsification criteria

The hypothesis is not supported if:
1. The coefficient for earlier menopause (per 5-year earlier) attenuates to non-significance (95% CI crossing zero) in Model 4 (fully adjusted) compared to Model 1 (age-only).
2. The gradient-boosted model shows no held-out R-squared improvement over the linear baseline and no nonlinear partial-dependence for menopause timing.

### Interpretation

**Supportive result:** The coefficient for earlier menopause remains significant in Model 4, and the gradient-boosted model shows held-out R-squared improvement and nonlinear partial-dependence for menopause timing. This would suggest that menopause timing identifies a subgroup with earlier cardiac structural remodelling beyond measured haemodynamic load.

**Adverse result:** The coefficient for earlier menopause attenuates to non-significance in Model 4. This would suggest that the association is explained by measured haemodynamic and metabolic factors.

**Inconclusive result:** The coefficient remains significant in Model 4 but the gradient-boosted model shows no improvement. This would suggest a linear association but no nonlinear effects or interactions.

## Method comparison

**Baseline:** Linear regression with the adjustment hierarchy above.

**Learned alternative:** Gradient-boosted models (e.g., XGBoost or LightGBM) with held-out evaluation. The learned alternative could reveal nonlinear effects and interactions that the linear baseline would miss. We will compare held-out R-squared and examine partial-dependence plots for menopause timing.

**Selection reason:** The linear baseline is appropriate for the primary analysis because it provides interpretable coefficients and confidence intervals. The gradient-boosted alternative is a sensitivity analysis to test for nonlinear effects and interactions. We will select the linear baseline for the primary analysis and use the gradient-boosted alternative as a sensitivity analysis.

## Compute requirements

The analysis is CPU-feasible. The linear regression can be performed with standard statistical software (e.g., R, Python with statsmodels). The gradient-boosted models can be performed with XGBoost or LightGBM on CPU. No GPU is required.
