# Scientific Proposal: Proteomic Profiles Distinguishing Infection Prevention from Severe Disease Prevention

## Hypothesis to Test

**Primary Hypothesis**: Among UK Biobank participants without baseline infection, proteomic profiles that predict incident hospitalization for infection (onset) also predict subsequent progression to infection-associated organ damage, but only when accounting for pre-existing comorbidities. This would distinguish biomarkers for infection prevention from those for severe disease prevention.

**Specific Predictions**:
1. Proteins predicting incident infection will have attenuated or no association with infection-associated organ damage after controlling for baseline comorbidities
2. Proteins predicting infection-associated organ damage will show independent associations beyond those predicting infection onset
3. The magnitude of association with organ damage will be greater than with infection onset for a subset of proteins

## Scientific Rationale

### Unresolved Question
Current biomarker research predominantly focuses on static baseline measurements to predict disease risk. However, the distinction between biomarkers that predict infection *onset* versus those that predict *organ damage after infection* remains unclear. This matters clinically because:
- Prevention strategies targeting infection onset (e.g., antimicrobial prophylaxis, vaccination) differ from those targeting organ damage (e.g., supportive care, organ-protective therapies)
- Current biomarkers may be capturing different biological processes (pathogen recognition vs. host response to tissue injury)
- Misinterpreting these biomarkers could lead to inappropriate prevention strategies

### Leading Claim vs. Rival Explanation
**Leading claim**: Proteomic signatures predicting infection onset are distinct from those predicting organ damage, with some proteins (e.g., acute-phase reactants) predicting both, while others (e.g., pathogen-specific antibodies) predict only onset.

**Rival explanation**: Proteomic profiles predicting infection onset are merely proxies for baseline health status, and any association with organ damage reflects underlying disease burden rather than causal pathways from infection.

### Key Difference
The leading claim predicts that after adjusting for baseline comorbidities, the association between proteomic profiles and organ damage will attenuate for onset-predicting proteins but remain for organ-damage-specific proteins. The rival predicts no such pattern.

## Baseline Approach

**Method**: Cox proportional hazards regression with static baseline proteomic profiles

**Model**:
```
hazard(t) = h0(t) * exp(β1*Protein1 + β2*Protein2 + ... + βk*Protein_k + γ*Comorbidities)
```

**Outcomes**:
1. Time to infection onset (any infection hospitalization)
2. Time to infection-associated organ damage

**Analysis**:
- Compare hazard ratios between high vs. low proteomic risk groups
- Test interaction between proteomic risk and baseline comorbidities
- Evaluate whether proteins predicting infection onset also predict organ damage

**Limitations of Baseline**:
- Cannot distinguish temporal dynamics of proteomic changes
- Assumes static risk profiles
- May miss proteins whose predictive value depends on proteomic trajectories

## Alternative Approach: Temporal Proteomic Dynamics

**Method**: Hidden Markov Model (HMM) or trajectory-based analysis

**Model**:
- Identify latent proteomic trajectory states over time
- Characterize trajectories by:
  - Rate of change (slope)
  - Variability (inter-measurement variance)
  - Temporal clustering (patterns of change)
- Test association of trajectory states with infection onset vs. organ damage

**Key Advantage**:
- Captures unique temporal information that static profiles miss
- Can identify proteins whose predictive value is time-dependent
- Tests whether proteomic instability (not just absolute levels) predicts outcomes

**Evaluation**:
- Compare AUC/HR between baseline and trajectory-based models
- Test whether trajectory-based predictions are independent of static profiles
- Identify proteins whose predictive value is specific to trajectory patterns

## Falsification Criteria

**Hypothesis would be falsified if**:
1. After adjusting for baseline comorbidities, proteomic profiles predicting infection onset show NO association with infection-associated organ damage (HR ≈ 1.0, p > 0.05)
2. Proteomic trajectories provide NO additional predictive value beyond static profiles (ΔAUC < 0.02, p > 0.05)
3. The same subset of proteins predicts both infection onset and organ damage in >90% of cases, suggesting a common pathway rather than distinct processes

**Hypothesis would be supported if**:
1. Proteins predicting infection onset show attenuated associations with organ damage after adjusting for comorbidities (HR < 1.2, p < 0.01)
2. Proteins predicting organ damage show independent associations beyond those predicting infection onset
3. Proteomic trajectories provide significant additional predictive value (ΔAUC > 0.05, p < 0.01)

**Hypothesis would be inconclusive if**:
1. Results are mixed (some proteins predict both, others predict only one)
2. Sample size limits power to detect modest effects
3. Confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out

## Clinical Significance

**Potential Impact**:
1. **Targeted prevention**: Distinguish biomarkers for infection prevention (e.g., antimicrobial strategies) from those for severe disease prevention (e.g., organ-protective therapies)
2. **Risk stratification**: Identify individuals at high risk for severe infection outcomes who may benefit from intensive monitoring
3. **Treatment selection**: Guide choice of prevention strategies based on which pathway is at risk

**Limitations Requiring Clinical Adjudication**:
1. Diagnostic codes may not capture disease severity accurately
2. Disease tolerance cannot be measured directly
3. Reverse causation (pre-existing disease affecting proteome and infection risk)
4. Need for external validation in different populations
5. Causal inference requires Mendelian randomization or experimental data
