# Does laboratory documentation add portable clinical information—or site-specific transport risk?

Status: Episode 4 successor of `[prior hypothesis]`; adaptation of expert seed `[starting question]), not a reproduction of any demonstration paper.

## Scientific question, hypothesis, and advance

The unresolved clinical question is whether early laboratory documentation patterns provide portable information about patient state or instead encode local testing workflow. A score that uses laboratory missingness, frequency, timing, or revision patterns may look accurate in its development ICUs but miscalibrate escalation, transfer, monitoring, or bed-allocation decisions in a new ICU.

Primary falsifiable hypothesis:

> In adult initial ICU stays that are alive and observable at six ICU hours, a model that adds early laboratory testing-process features to the same measured clinical values has a larger development-to-new-hospital transport penalty than the corresponding values-only model. The excess penalty will be attenuated, but not eliminated, after residualizing process features against measured acuity and after accounting for the general vital-monitoring process.

This is a prediction/transportability hypothesis, not a claim that testing causes death or that any hospital has inferior care. Its substantive advance is to separate three explanations that are usually conflated: measured physiology, laboratory workflow, and general observation intensity. It also makes the clinical risk concrete through calibration and threshold decision summaries rather than AUROC alone.

The strongest pre-experiment claim supported by the inspected material is only that this eICU snapshot contains patient- and hospital-linked ICU stays, ICU-relative laboratory and vital observations, and laboratory revision timestamps with heterogeneous recording. A prior bounded audit recorded 140,801 eligible adult initial stays across 207 hospitals, 2,339,035 early laboratory rows, and at least one eligible early laboratory row in 117,368 stays. Those are feasibility observations, not evidence that the hypothesis is true. No fitted model result is being claimed.

The index is ICU admission at offset 0. The feature window is [0, 360] ICU minutes. The primary endpoint is a competing-state outcome observed from 360 through 3,240 minutes (48 hours after the landmark):

1. ICU death before or at 3,240 minutes (`unitdischargestatus` indicates death and `unitdischargeoffset <= 3240`);
2. alive ICU exit/discharge before or at 3,240 minutes (the competing state, including transfer/discharge when the status is not death);
3. still in the ICU at 3,240 minutes or follow-up not observable (censored/still-ICU state).

The primary statistical target is the difference-in-differences in transport penalty for ICU death: for each feature set, estimate internal hospital-held-out performance and new-hospital performance, then compare
`(internal performance - external performance)_process+values -
(internal performance - external performance)_values-only`.
Report the multinomial D/C/still-ICU state analysis and a sensitivity treating alive ICU exit as censoring, but do not call either a causal effect or a safe-discharge policy.

## Baseline and substantive alternative

The primary, transparent baseline is an L2-regularized logistic model for ICU death by 3,240 minutes. Its values-only vector contains prespecified first/last/median/minimum/maximum and linear-slope summaries for the top 20 analytes by training-fold availability, plus the same summaries for the named vital columns above. Numeric values are standardized and training-fold medians are used for missing value imputation; there are no laboratory presence indicators in this baseline. A multinomial logistic version predicts death, alive exit, and still-ICU/censored state.

The process-augmented model adds, for each selected `labname`, count, first/last observation time, time since last result, median inter-result interval, fraction of the 0--360 minute window observed, and revision-delay summaries. Add total laboratory-row count and the analogous counts/intervals for `vitalPeriodic` and `vitalAperiodic`. The laboratory process block is fit in three prespecified forms: raw; residualized using an outer-training regression on the values-only vector and hospital descriptors; and residualized plus the vital-process control. The comparison of transport penalties is the scientific deliverable, not a search for the largest predictive gain.

The substantive learned alternative is a one-layer GRU (hidden size 64, fixed 30-minute bins over [0,360]) trained on the same values-only and values-plus-process inputs, with masking/delta-time channels fit only on training hospitals. It can reveal whether any transport instability is caused by temporal ordering, within-window interactions, or irregular observation timing that the additive summaries lose. It is secondary: identical hospital folds, outcome, calibration, uncertainty, and falsification rules apply. Approximate budget is <=2 hours on 4 CPU cores and 16 GiB RAM (or one preloaded GPU if the compiler’s declared environment makes that necessary), with a fixed small hyperparameter set and early stopping on a validation hospital fold. No model will be selected for clinical use from a small performance difference.

Use deterministic five-fold whole-hospital splits, assigning hospitals—not rows—after excluding hospitals with no eligible outcome support. Within each outer fold, reserve training hospitals for fitting and validation, then score every eligible stay in held-out hospitals. Report both macro-hospital and patient-weighted summaries. The central uncertainty interval is a hospital-cluster bootstrap of the paired fold/hospital differences; supplement with patient bootstrap only as a sensitivity. Report AUROC, AUPRC, Brier/log loss, calibration intercept/slope and calibration plots, plus decision-curve/net-benefit summaries at a predeclared threshold based on the training-fold outcome prevalence. These summaries are descriptive decision-analytic consequences, not proof of clinical utility.

## Falsification and interpretation

Before seeing results, run:

- outcome-label permutation within the training and test partitions: all models should collapse toward chance and no transport contrast should remain;
- within-hospital permutation of process blocks across patients while preserving hospital-level process distributions: a patient-specific process signal should disappear;
- temporal permutation of lab observation order while retaining values: a GRU advantage that survives this test is likely process/count driven rather than temporal physiology;
- the vital-process-only augmentation: if laboratory-process instability is merely general monitoring intensity, its excess penalty should resemble the vital-process control;
- a revision-safe versus naive-revision sensitivity: a material difference flags leakage susceptibility, not a biological discovery.

Supportive results are an excess process-model transport penalty with a confidence interval excluding zero, worsening calibration or decision summaries on held-out hospitals, attenuation after acuity residualization/general-monitoring control, and falsification outputs behaving as prespecified. Adverse results are equal or smaller process-model transport penalty, robust process gains on new hospitals after controls, or failure of the process signal under a non-leakage analysis; that would refute the proposed instability and could instead support local portability of the recorded process. Results are inconclusive when held-out hospital counts are inadequate, outcome separation fails, confidence intervals are wide, or revision/ascertainment sensitivity changes the conclusion.

Computationally checkable claims are cohort counts, feature availability, leakage compliance, fitted-model convergence, predictions, paired metrics, uncertainty intervals, calibration, and permutation behavior. Clinical adjudication is still required to decide whether a recorded ICU death/discharge label corresponds to the intended clinical state, whether a testing pattern reflects workflow rather than unmeasured acuity, and whether any threshold summary would change care. The dataset lacks raw waveforms, images, reliable narrative notes, pre-ICU course, staffing/bed-capacity data, and a treatment assignment; therefore the experiment cannot establish causality, clinical benefit, hospital quality, or a safe monitoring policy. Those stronger claims require chart adjudication, richer operational data, prospective or external validation, and ideally an interventional study.
