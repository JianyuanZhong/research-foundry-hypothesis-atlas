# Bone-muscle discordance and incident fracture risk: a temporally explicit two-pathway hypothesis

## Scientific question and clinical importance

Among UK Biobank participants aged ≥50 with valid DXA BMD and grip strength, does a standardized grip-strength residual (grip strength unexplained by BMD, age, sex, and BMI) predict incident fracture risk independently of BMD, and does this excess risk differ by baseline fall history?

The clinically consequential unresolved question is whether low BMD and low grip strength represent a single latent frailty process (where muscle weakness is merely a marker of overall biological ageing) or two separable pathways: (1) bone fragility driving fracture independent of muscle, and (2) muscle weakness driving falls that then cause fracture. This distinction has direct implications for screening and prevention: if the two pathways are separable, then dual anti-resorptive + resistance-exercise interventions would be more effective than bone-targeted therapy alone for the discordant subgroup.

The leading explanation (single latent process) is that low BMD and low grip strength are both downstream of a shared ageing/frailty trajectory, so the grip-strength residual adds no independent fracture risk beyond what BMD alone predicts after adjustment. The strongest rival (two-pathway hypothesis) is that muscle weakness contributes an independent fall-related fracture pathway that is not captured by BMD, so the discordant subgroup (low BMD + low grip residual) has a super-additive fracture risk.

## Exact discordance estimand

**Primary estimand: standardized grip-strength residual (SGR)**

1. Fit a linear regression of dominant-hand grip strength (field 46, kg) on BMD (field 23204, g/cm², per SD), age (field 34, years), sex (field 31), and BMI (field 40, kg/m², per SD) within the analytic cohort.
2. Define SGR = (observed grip − predicted grip) / SD of residuals, computed separately for men and women.
3. The primary exposure is SGR (continuous, per SD) and a binary discordance indicator: SGR < −1.0 (low grip beyond what BMD predicts) AND BMD T-score ≤ −1.0 (osteopenia/osteoporosis).

**Secondary estimand: BMD × SGR interaction**

- Cox model with BMD (continuous, per SD) × SGR (continuous, per SD) interaction term.
- The interaction hazard ratio (HR) quantifies whether the fracture risk associated with low grip is modified by BMD level.

**Why SGR rather than raw grip or categorical groups?**
- Raw grip strength is strongly correlated with BMD, age, sex, and BMI. The residual isolates the component of grip strength that is *not* explained by these factors, directly testing whether muscle weakness adds information beyond bone density.
- Categorical groups (low BMD × low grip) are sensitive to arbitrary cutoffs. The continuous SGR estimand is more precise and avoids the small-cell problem in the discordant subgroup.
- The SGR is a standardized residual, so it is interpretable as "grip strength unexplained by bone density and demographics."

## Population and temporal boundaries

- **Source**: UK Biobank assessment-center data, `ukb672073_Assessment_Center.csv` (Parquet: `[UKB data file]`) and `ukb672073_Health_Related_Outcomes.csv` (Parquet: `[UKB data file]`), joined on `eid`.
- **Population**: Participants aged ≥50 at baseline assessment with valid DXA (L1-L4 BMD field 23204 and/or femoral neck BMD field 23208), valid grip strength (fields 46/47), and no prior fracture diagnosis in the 2 years before baseline.
- **Baseline**: Date of attending assessment centre (field 53).
- **Follow-up**: From baseline to first incident fracture diagnosis (ICD10 field 41270, date field 41280), censoring at study end, loss to follow-up, or death. Maximum follow-up 10 years.

Falls (field 2296) are baseline-reported (last 12 months), so they are a baseline covariate, not a time-varying mediator.

## Adjustment hierarchy and temporal ordering

**Model 1 (unadjusted)**: Cox with SGR (per SD) only.

**Model 2 (demographic adjustment)**: Cox with SGR + age + sex.

**Model 3 (full adjustment)**: Cox with SGR + age + sex + BMI + BMD (continuous, per SD) + falls (field 2296).

**Model 4 (interaction)**: Cox with BMD × SGR interaction + age + sex + BMI + falls.

**Model 5 (falls stratification)**: Cox with SGR + BMD, stratified by baseline falls (yes/no).

The adjustment hierarchy is pre-specified: Model 3 is the primary adjusted model. Falls are included in the primary model because they are a baseline-reported covariate (last 12 months) and are a potential mediator of the muscle-weakness pathway. However, because falls are measured at baseline (not time-varying), they cannot be used for causal mediation analysis. The falls stratification (Model 5) is a sensitivity analysis to test whether the SGR effect differs by fall history.

**Why not adjust for falls in the primary model?**
- Falls are a baseline-reported covariate, not a time-varying mediator. Adjusting for them in the primary model is appropriate for confounding control but does not establish mediation.
- The primary estimand (SGR) is defined without falls adjustment. Falls adjustment is a sensitivity analysis.

## Competing explanations and falsification

**Single latent process (null hypothesis)**: After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, BMD, and falls, the SGR is not significantly associated with incident fracture (HR per SD ≈ 1.0, 95% CI includes 1.0, p > 0.10). Additionally, the BMD × SGR interaction HR is not significantly different from 1.0 (p > 0.10 for interaction).

**Two-pathway hypothesis (alternative)**: The SGR is significantly associated with incident fracture (HR per SD > 1.0, 95% CI lower bound > 1.0, p < 0.05), and the BMD × SGR interaction HR is significantly > 1.0 (p < 0.05, 95% CI lower bound > 1.0), indicating super-additive risk. Additionally, the SGR effect is stronger in the falls-positive subgroup (Model 5).

**Inconclusive**: SGR HR > 1.0 but 95% CI includes 1.0, or interaction HR > 1.0 but 95% CI includes 1.0.

**Falsification criteria (summary)**:

| Result | Interpretation |
|--------|---------------|
| SGR HR > 1.0, 95% CI > 1.0, interaction HR > 1.0, 95% CI > 1.0 | Supports two-pathway hypothesis |
| SGR HR ≈ 1.0, 95% CI includes 1.0 | Supports single latent process |
| SGR HR > 1.0 but 95% CI includes 1.0, or interaction HR > 1.0 but 95% CI includes 1.0 | Inconclusive; needs larger sample or different analysis |
| SGR HR < 1.0, 95% CI < 1.0 | Contradicts two-pathway hypothesis; suggests muscle weakness is protective or the SGR is mismeasured |
| Boosted C-index > Cox C-index by > 0.02 with non-rectangular PD plots | Suggests complex interaction beyond multiplicative model |

## Method comparison

### Baseline: Cox proportional hazards regression
- Model 1: Cox with SGR (continuous, per SD) only
- Model 2: Cox with SGR + age + sex
- Model 3: Cox with SGR + age + sex + BMI + BMD + falls (primary adjusted model)
- Model 4: Cox with BMD × SGR interaction + age + sex + BMI + falls
- Model 5: Cox with SGR + BMD, stratified by baseline falls
- Estimand: HR for SGR (per SD) and HR for BMD × SGR interaction

### Learned alternative: Gradient-boosted survival model
- Algorithm: XGBoost with survival objective (or gradient-boosted trees with Cox partial likelihood)
- Features: BMD, grip strength, BMD × grip interaction, SGR, age, sex, BMI, falls, plus up to 10 additional covariates (smoking, alcohol, physical activity)
- Training: 80% train / 20% held-out test split (stratified by fracture status)
- Evaluation: Concordance index (C-index) on held-out data; comparison with baseline Cox C-index
- Additional: Partial dependence plots for BMD and grip strength to visualize interaction

### What the learned model reveals that the baseline loses
The Cox model assumes proportional hazards and a specific parametric form for the interaction. The gradient-boosted model can capture non-linear and higher-order interactions between BMD and grip strength that a two-way Cox interaction cannot. If the boosted model shows substantially better held-out C-index and the partial dependence plots reveal a non-rectangular interaction pattern, this would suggest the two-pathway hypothesis is more complex than a simple multiplicative interaction.
