# Provisional hypothesis: reduced laboratory monitoring during persistent instability

## Proposed relationship

Among adults in the ICU who remain physiologically unstable, an abrupt reduction in the frequency of laboratory testing over the preceding 12 hours is associated with a higher probability of a subsequent deterioration event in the next 12 hours than a stable or increasing testing frequency, after accounting for measured current physiologic state and hospital. The association is expected to be stronger when the patient has recent organ-support escalation or worsening vital signs, but this interaction is exploratory.

This is a provisional, observational hypothesis. It does not claim that reducing tests causes deterioration. The leading rival explanations are (1) clinicians appropriately reduce testing because they judge recovery, impending discharge/transfer, or end-of-life limitation; (2) interface or documentation interruptions create apparent gaps; and (3) sicker patients have more tests, so testing frequency is a proxy for unmeasured severity.

## Clinical importance and unresolved claim

The strongest evidence currently available is only that eICU contains longitudinal laboratory, vital-sign, respiratory, medication/infusion, and discharge records with patient-unit-stay and hospital identifiers; the expert seed proposes that reduced testing during instability may reflect monitoring that is not aligned with patient need. The unresolved claim is whether a *dynamic change* in testing frequency contains information about near-term deterioration beyond the patient’s contemporaneous measured severity and care context.

Resolving this would distinguish a potentially actionable warning signal from a documentation artifact or a marker of clinical trajectory. It would not establish that more testing improves outcomes; that requires a prospective or carefully identified treatment study.

## Proposed experiment

1. Construct an exposure at each index hour: number of distinct analytes and number of testing episodes in the preceding 12 hours, plus the change from the preceding 12-hour interval. Define a reduction prespecified as a drop to zero from a positive prior interval or a reduction of at least 50% when the prior interval has at least two testing episodes; sensitivity analyses use continuous change and analyte-specific counts.
2. Estimate the association with the 12-hour deterioration outcome using a prespecified baseline model with current vitals, respiratory state, recent support, age/sex, unit type, and hospital fixed/clustered effects. Compare it with a substantive longitudinal alternative: a discrete-time hazard or recurrent-event model with time-varying testing intensity and physiologic state, clustered by patient stay and hospital. This alternative can reveal whether the signal is transient, persistent, or preceded by a trajectory, information lost by a single index-hour comparison.
3. Split by hospital for transport validation where feasible; do not use future outcomes or post-index measurements in features. Report calibration and discrimination, absolute risk differences, uncertainty intervals, and hospital heterogeneity. A simple logistic baseline and the longitudinal model must use the same outcome and eligible index times.
4. Falsification/negative controls: permute testing intervals within hospital while preserving stay length and outcome; compare with an exposure defined from post-index testing; and compare with a non-clinical documentation timestamp control if available. A positive result that disappears under these controls supports documentation/selection rather than a monitoring signal.

Supportive results would be a reproducible, calibrated association after state adjustment that persists in held-out hospitals and is not reproduced by the negative controls. Adverse results would be no association, instability across hospitals, or disappearance after accounting for support/EOL context. Inconclusive results would be wide intervals, sparse outcomes, or inability to distinguish interface interruption from clinical intent; these justify a measurement audit or another study, not a clinical recommendation.

## Missing evidence and limitations

The catalog does not provide a direct, adjudicated “interface interruption” flag, staffing/bed-capacity data, complete pre-ICU history, or a clinical adjudication of deterioration. Nutrition, medication administration, and some respiratory fields may be sparse; missingness must be inspected and not treated as absence. The study can test an association and a transportability/measurement question, but cannot establish that increasing testing improves care or identify a causal mechanism. Clinical adjudication of deterioration and review of end-of-life decisions are required for stronger conclusions.

## Method alternatives and compute

The simple baseline is a prespecified logistic/discrete-time model with current state and hospital effects, suitable for CPU. The substantive alternative is a longitudinal recurrent-event/discrete-time hazard model with time-varying testing intensity and physiologic trajectories; it can identify persistence and trajectory effects that a single index model loses. Both use the same eligible index times, outcome, hospital split, uncertainty intervals, and calibration evaluation. A GPU is not required for this first study; the future solver envelope is 16 CPUs, 262,144 MiB, up to 2 GPUs and 28,800 seconds, but no full fit has been run in discovery.

## Demonstration disposition

- Delphi: not selected for this branch because its disease-history tokenization and external validation are not needed to answer the narrower eICU monitoring question; a future longitudinal adaptation could be revisited if trajectory learning proves informative.
- ALADYNOULLI: not selected because the genetic inputs and latent disease-signature assumptions are not required; an EHR-only latent trajectory model would be a different adaptation and is deferred.
- Oncoformer: not selected because the full multimodal chest-X-ray study is unavailable and image modality is not central to this question; a lab-only adaptation would add complexity without resolving the monitoring-versus-severity uncertainty.

This draft is provisional and has not been validated by a full literature review, source-row audit, or fitted analysis.
