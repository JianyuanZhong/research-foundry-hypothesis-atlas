# Provisional HCC hypothesis (early draft)

## Proposed relationship
Among adults with a documented liver cancer diagnosis in this HCC hospital dataset, the presence of a recorded major liver-directed treatment during the early post-diagnosis interval is associated with lower 1-year all-cause mortality than no recorded treatment, after accounting for baseline age, sex, comorbidity burden, and documented disease stage when available.

This is an observational prognostic/treatment-association hypothesis, not a causal claim. The key unresolved question is whether the apparent survival advantage reflects treatment benefit or is explained by healthier treated patients, stage/extent of disease, access and documentation intensity, or informative loss to follow-up.

## Grounding and evidence limits
The dataset card confirms that HCC contains read-only encounter, diagnosis, laboratory, examination, medication, procedure, pathology, clinical-document and vital-sign tables, with exact source files and table schemas under `datasets/hcc/`. The diagnosis table can define the index liver-cancer population; procedure and medication tables can identify liver-directed interventions; encounter and diagnosis fields can support baseline covariates and follow-up. I have not yet inspected row-level values or verified treatment-code coverage, stage completeness, or mortality ascertainment, so no effect estimate or clinical conclusion is claimed here.

## Falsifiable test
Define an index date from the first qualifying liver-cancer diagnosis. Compare patients with and without a recorded liver-directed procedure/medication in a prespecified early window (for example, 0–30 days), with 1-year mortality as the primary outcome. Use patient-level time-zero covariates only, censor at discharge/last documented contact or loss to follow-up, and report standardized mortality risk differences with confidence intervals. A prespecified negative-control or timing analysis (treatment recorded after index but before the outcome window) and sensitivity analyses excluding undocumented stage or short-stay patients will test selection and documentation explanations.

Supportive result: a reproducible adjusted survival difference with plausible timing and robustness to missingness. Adverse result: no difference, reversal, or dependence on treatment selection/documentation proxies. Inconclusive result: wide intervals, sparse treatment, poor stage coverage, or non-identifiable follow-up. None of these outcomes establishes causality without clinical adjudication and stronger treatment-assignment evidence.
