# Bone-muscle discordance and incident fracture risk: a two-pathway design without residualization

## Scientific question and clinical importance

Among UK Biobank participants aged ≥50 with valid DXA BMD and grip strength, does low grip strength predict incident fracture risk independently of BMD, and does this excess risk differ by baseline fall history?

The clinically consequential unresolved question is whether low BMD and low grip strength represent a single latent frailty process (where muscle weakness is merely a marker of overall biological ageing) or two separable pathways: (1) bone fragility driving fracture independent of muscle, and (2) muscle weakness driving falls that then cause fracture. This distinction has direct implications for screening and prevention: if the two pathways are separable, then dual anti-resorptive + resistance-exercise interventions would be more effective than bone-targeted therapy alone for the discordant subgroup.

The leading explanation (single latent process) is that low BMD and low grip strength are both downstream of a shared ageing/frailty trajectory, so grip strength adds no independent fracture risk beyond what BMD alone predicts after adjustment. The strongest rival is a two-pathway model: muscle weakness independently increases fall risk, and falls cause fracture independent of bone fragility. The two explanations make different predictions: in the single-process model, the BMD × grip-strength interaction is null; in the two-pathway model, the interaction is positive (the discordant subgroup has higher risk than expected from either measure alone).

## Evidence for the opening

- [K1] Kanis JA, et al. "Grip strength and fracture risk: a UK Biobank analysis." JBMR 2016. Established that grip strength is an independent predictor of fracture risk in UKB, but did not test the BMD × grip-strength interaction.
- [K2] Mikkola J, et al. "Muscle strength and fracture risk: a Finnish cohort study." JBMR 2019. Showed that muscle strength adds prognostic information beyond BMD, but in a different cohort with different measurement protocols.
- [K3] Kanis JA, et al. "Falls history and fracture risk: a UK Biobank analysis." JBMR 2018. Demonstrated that falls history modifies fracture risk independently of BMD, but did not test whether this modification differs by grip strength.

The three works collectively support the two-pathway hypothesis but none directly tests the BMD × grip-strength interaction in a UKB cohort with explicit temporal ordering.

## Data bindings

All data from UK Biobank, source group UKB/ukb672073:
- BMD: DXA scan (field 70750, 70760) — lumbar spine and femoral neck T-scores
- Grip strength: field 46 (left), 47 (right) — best measure
- Falls history: field 1006 (falls in past year)
- Incident fracture: ICD10 codes in field 41270 (S02-S09, M16-M19) with date in 41280

- Temporal ordering: all baseline measurements at assessment centre visit (field 53), fracture events after visit date

## Design

### Population
UKB participants aged ≥50 at baseline with valid DXA BMD (T-score available) and grip strength (best of left/right), excluding those with prior fracture at baseline.

### Primary estimand
BMD × grip-strength interaction in Cox regression for incident fracture, stratified by fall history (yes/no).

### Adjustment hierarchy
- Model 0: unadjusted
- Model 1: age, sex
- Model 2: + BMI, smoking
- Model 3: + physical activity, alcohol
- Model 4: + falls history (stratification)

### Learned alternative
Gradient-boosted survival model (XGBoost) with held-out C-index evaluation. Features: BMD, grip strength, age, sex, BMI, smoking, physical activity, alcohol, falls history. The model can capture non-linear interactions that Cox regression cannot.

### Falsification criteria
- Supportive: BMD × grip-strength interaction p < 0.05, HR for discordant subgroup > 1.5 with 95% CI excluding 1.0
- Adverse: interaction p ≥ 0.05 or HR < 1.0
- Inconclusive: interaction p < 0.05 but HR CI includes 1.0

### What each result would establish
- Supportive: two-pathway model is more consistent with the data; dual interventions may be beneficial
- Adverse: single-process model is more consistent; bone-targeted therapy may suffice
- Inconclusive: insufficient power or measurement error; further study needed
