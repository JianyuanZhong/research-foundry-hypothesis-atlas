TITLE
Does waiting 61–90 rather than 29–60 days after preoperative TACE permit safer hepatic recovery before first HCC resection?

CLINICAL QUESTION AND FALSIFIABLE HYPOTHESIS
For adults with a documented hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) diagnosis who undergo their first recorded liver resection after TACE, is a shorter interval from the last TACE to resection associated with poorer early postoperative hepatic recovery? The prespecified hypothesis is that resection 29–60 days after the last TACE, versus 61–90 days, is associated with a higher day-5-to-7 total-bilirubin ratio relative to the last preoperative value. This is a treatment-readiness/timing hypothesis, not a claim that postponement is causally beneficial.

Clinical importance: TACE can injure non-tumor liver, while unnecessary postponement can permit tumor progression. The decision is therefore consequential and currently individualized. A recent single-center cohort of huge HCC found higher morbidity,/pleural effusion and more Clavien–Dindo III–IV complications among patients receiving preoperative TACE, without better overall or recurrence-free survival; its stated indications included delayed surgery and comorbidity evaluation, illustrating strong confounding by indication and unresolved timing (Na et al., 2025, PMID 41000227, PMCID PMC12457797, DOI 10.4174/astr.2025.109.3.194; full text inspected from frozen source [source checksum]). Existing evidence supports only that preoperative TACE is selected nonrandomly and may correlate with morbidity. It does not establish an optimal interval.

Strongest claim already supported: the snapshot contains a large longitudinal group with explicit HCC, dated TACE-like procedures, dated liver resections, and paired perioperative bilirubin/INR measurements, so the timing–recovery association is computable. Untested claim: conditional on measured readiness and case-mix variables, 29–60-day resections have worse early hepatic recovery than 61–90-day resections. A causal recommendation to delay surgery would require stronger adjustment, clinical adjudication, and preferably a prospective target-trial or randomized evaluation.

ACTUAL FEASIBILITY PASS
Read all 105,044 encounter rows (43,815 patients; 105,044 unique patient/encounter keys), all 1,810,646 diagnosis rows, all 338,040 procedure rows, and streamed the complete 2.2-GB lab file for the timing cohort. There were 344,743 HCC-matching diagnosis rows and 25,719 patients with a first qualifying resection from 2010-04-25 through 2025-12-31. Among them, 1,090 had a prior TACE-like procedure; 605 had last-TACE intervals >28 and <=90 days: 381 in >28–60 days and 224 in >60–90 days. (A preliminary integer-day tabulation using 29–60 and 61–90 yielded 377 and 218; the executable rule must use exact elapsed time >28–60 and >60–90, explaining the four/six boundary cases.)

In the preliminary 29–90 integer-day sample of 601, total bilirubin was present in 598, INR in 599, and 403 had all four measurements required for the paired primary analysis (last preoperative bilirubin and INR during day -7 to <0, and postoperative bilirubin and INR during days 5–7). In the exact-name assays, 455 had routine bilirubin and 479 INR measured on days 5–7. Median bilirubin was 18.2 and INR 1.15 across retained assay rows, supporting face-valid numeric parsing. These are feasibility counts, not hypothesis results. Two patients met the very stringent exploratory day-5-to-7 bilirubin >50 plus INR >1.5 proxy, so that binary proxy is underpowered and must not be primary.

POPULATION AND TEMPORAL BOUNDARIES
Unit: patient, one index operation.
Include: age >=18 at the index encounter; explicit HCC diagnosis; first recorded qualifying liver resection; at least one qualifying TACE before resection; exact last-TACE-to-resection interval >28 through 90 days; index admission and operation within source date range; last bilirubin in [-7 days, 0) and postoperative bilirubin in [5, 7] days relative to operation.
Exclude: liver transplant/donor procedures at index; TACE in the same operative encounter unless manually confirmed as a genuinely preceding staged procedure; impossible/duplicate timestamps; baseline bilirubin <=0; operation before recorded TACE; no numeric paired bilirubin. Main complete-case estimand additionally requires paired INR; bilirubin-only analysis is a secondary missingness sensitivity.
Exposure: E=1 for interval >28 to <=60 days; E=0 for >60 to <=90 days. Exact elapsed hours/24, not rounded calendar dates. Also model interval continuously with restricted cubic splines (knots 35, 50, 70, 85 days) and extend to >90–180 days in a sensitivity analysis, while acknowledging increasing selection differences.
Baseline window: latest result in [-7,0) days. Outcome window: maximum result in [5,7] days, chosen because the accepted ISGLS post-hepatectomy liver-failure concept evaluates bilirubin and INR on/after postoperative day 5. This EHR study cannot automatically establish clinical ISGLS grade because treatment escalation and full clinical context are incompletely structured.

OUTCOMES
Primary: log(maximum day-5-to-7 total bilirubin / last day-7-to-0 preoperative total bilirubin). This estimates relative biochemical hepatic recovery and avoids the sparse binary proxy.
Key secondary: change in INR (maximum day 5–7 minus baseline); joint worsening of both bilirubin and INR; day-5-to-7 albumin change; discharge length of stay; biochemical recovery trajectories over postoperative days 1–7 using a mixed model.
Exploratory clinically adjudicated outcomes: ISGLS PHLF and grade, encephalopathy, new/worsened ascites, biliary complication, infection/sepsis, reoperation, ICU escalation, and in-hospital death, abstracted from index clinical documents, diagnoses, orders/procedures, and medication context by blinded clinical reviewers. LOS alone is not a complication diagnosis.

ESTIMAND, BASELINES, AND ANALYSIS
Primary estimand is an adjusted observational association: the overlap-population mean difference in log bilirubin ratio for >28–60 versus >60–90 days among patients who actually underwent resection and had paired labs. It is not an effect among all patients receiving TACE, because those who progressed, died, or were never resected are absent (conditioning-on-resection/immortal-time selection).

Report unadjusted medians and standardized mean differences. Estimate propensity for earlier surgery using age, sex, calendar year, department, exact baseline bilirubin, INR, albumin, creatinine, platelets, ALT, hemoglobin, interval from admission to operation, number of prior TACE records, prior HCC treatment categories, resection extent from surgery (major/anatomic/partial; open/laparoscopic), and diagnosis flags for cirrhosis, portal hypertension, ascites, viral hepatitis, diabetes, and renal disease. Use overlap weights, check positivity and effective sample size, then weighted robust linear regression clustered by calendar month/operative service if identifiable. Do not adjust for post-exposure variables. Multiple imputation may be used for covariates, never the primary paired outcome. Report adjusted ratio of geometric mean bilirubin ratios (exp coefficient), 95% CI, absolute group medians, INR result, missingness by group, and E-values only as sensitivity descriptors, not proof.

Essential tumor-case-mix sensitivity: add reviewer-validated or validated extraction of maximum tumor diameter, number, macrovascular invasion, rupture, ascites/cirrhosis, and interval imaging response from the most recent examination before surgery. If extraction PPV and sensitivity are each <0.90 in a stratified blinded sample of at least 200 reports, do not use automated labels as definitive covariates; report the structured-only result as confounded/inconclusive and seek full adjudication.

Baselines: (i) unadjusted >28–60 vs >60–90; (ii) conventional multivariable regression; (iii) overlap-weighted model; (iv) continuous spline; (v) contextual no-prior-TACE and >90–180 groups, explicitly non-comparable and not causal controls.

NEGATIVE CONTROLS AND BIAS CHECKS
1. Negative-control outcome: postoperative day-1-to-3 hemoglobin nadir adjusted for baseline hemoglobin and operation type. Recovery time from hepatic TACE should not directly determine immediate operative hemoglobin loss; a material timing association (same-direction standardized effect >=0.20 or p<0.01) indicates residual surgical-complexity/selection bias and downgrades the hepatic result.
2. Falsification window: repeat the primary construction using a pseudo-outcome from two measurements both before surgery (-7 to -4 versus -3 to <0). A strong apparent “postoperative” timing effect before surgery indicates differential readiness trends/confounding.
3. Assay/calendar control: tabulate assay-name distributions and outcomes by year, and rerun within 2018–2025 and using routine (non-emergency) names only.
4. Timing-placebo cuts: evaluate adjacent alternative cut points (55 and 65 days). An isolated threshold finding with no coherent continuous trend is interpreted as unstable.
5. Missingness: compare exposure and covariates between paired-outcome and excluded patients; inverse-probability-of-observation sensitivity. If fewer than 100 effective weighted patients per arm or overlap weights are dominated by <=10 observations, stop and call inconclusive.

FALSIFICATION AND INTERPRETATION RULES
Supportive: the adjusted geometric-mean bilirubin ratio is >=1.15 for >28–60 versus >60–90 days, its two-sided 95% CI excludes 1.00, INR changes in the same adverse direction, the spline is broadly monotonic over 29–90 days, and neither negative control fails. This supports a clinically relevant association and motivates a readiness protocol/prospective study; it still does not prove that waiting prevents failure.
Adverse/opposite: adjusted ratio <=0.87 with CI excluding 1.00, or convincingly lower INR worsening in the early group, suggests that longer waiting is not biochemically safer and may select less-ready patients; investigate progression and indication mechanisms rather than recommend acceleration.
Null/inconclusive: CI includes both 1.00 and 1.15 (or both 1.00 and 0.87), effective sample/overlap is inadequate, tumor case-mix extraction is invalid, negative controls show bias, missingness differs materially, or bilirubin and INR conflict. A precise CI contained within 0.87–1.15 supports absence of a clinically material association in this selected resected population, not equivalence for all TACE recipients.

CLINICAL ADJUDICATION AND UNAVAILABLE EVIDENCE
A hepatobiliary surgeon/interventional radiologist must validate TACE and resection lexicons, identify emergency/ruptured-HCC embolization versus planned neoadjuvant TACE, adjudicate resection extent, and review why surgery was delayed. Two blinded clinicians should adjudicate ISGLS PHLF and major complications with disagreement resolution. The snapshot lacks verified laboratory units, raw imaging, future-liver-remnant volumetry, ICG clearance, anesthesia flowsheets/blood loss, transfusion units, performance status, standardized BCLC/Child-Pugh/ALBI fields, treatment intent, outside-hospital TACE, and complete mortality/follow-up. Therefore automated verification can check cohort construction, dates, values, models, uncertainty, and whether conclusions follow from outputs; it cannot establish clinical readiness, causal benefit, true PHLF grade, or safety recommendations.
