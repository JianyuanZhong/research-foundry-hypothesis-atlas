# Residual physiologic instability at ICU discharge and post-ICU failure

## Scientific question and hypothesis

At the first ICU discharge of an adult hospitalization, does residual oxygen/support burden or short-term vital-sign instability identify patients at increased risk of ICU readmission or death after discharge, even when final recorded values appear acceptable?

Primary falsifiable hypothesis: among adults discharged alive from their first ICU stay, persistent high oxygen/support burden, fluctuating vital signs, or both during the final 6 hours will be associated with higher risk of the composite of ICU readmission or hospital death after discharge than neither phenotype, after adjustment for prespecified severity and discharge context. The phenotype contrasts and oxygen-by-vital-instability interaction are prespecified.

Secondary information hypothesis: a time-aware model using the full irregular 24-hour trajectory will improve calibrated post-discharge risk estimation, especially among patients whose last values are reassuring but whose preceding trajectory is unstable, compared with an interpretable model using last values and window summaries. This is a substantive question about dynamic information, not a request for a marginal predictive gain.

This is untested. Expert seed [starting question] proposed residual instability before ICU discharge; its imported candidate was assessed valid but underspecified. This proposal supplies operational definitions, timing, estimands, bindings, analysis, and limitations.

## Clinical importance and advance

ICU discharge is a consequential transition where ward monitoring and respiratory-support decisions are made with incomplete information about durable recovery. A useful result could identify a reproducible subgroup for enhanced monitoring or prospective discharge-readiness evaluation. The advance is testing whether persistence and fluctuation add information beyond a single last observation and routine discharge context, and whether the domains have distinct or synergistic risk patterns. A null result would argue against using these structured signals alone to justify altered discharge decisions.

Available evidence supports only that MIMIC-IV records repeated ICU measurements, ICU boundaries, support-related charting, and hospitalization outcomes. It does not establish that this phenotype predicts failure, causes readmission, or that changing discharge timing improves outcomes.

## Phenotype

Bin measurements into hourly bins ending at T0. Use within-bin median for numeric variables, retaining count and time since last observation. Do not use storetime to move observations across charttime boundaries.

Persistent oxygen/support burden means at least two of the final six bins with FiO2 >=0.40, or O2 flow >=4 L/min, or positive/active mechanically ventilated/ventilator-status record. Vital instability means, across the final six bins, at least two of: HR range >=30 bpm, MAP range >=20 mmHg, RR range >=10 breaths/min; each component requires at least three observed bins. Use arterial MAP when present and otherwise non-invasive MAP. Stable means adequate coverage and neither domain; both means both domains; unclassifiable means insufficient coverage and is excluded from the primary contrast but reported and included in a missingness sensitivity analysis. Fix value parsing and thresholds before inspecting outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses use continuous hours above thresholds, FiO2/O2-flow burden, and variability plus alternate thresholds, labeled secondary.

## Analysis and confounding

The primary estimand is adjusted 7-day risk difference and risk ratio for each phenotype versus stable, plus the interaction contrast for both domains. This is prognostic/associational, not a causal effect of discharge or oxygen reduction.

Adjust for information available by T0: age, sex, admission type/location, first/last ICU care unit, ICU length of stay, time from hospital admission to discharge, pre-T0 ventilation/intubation/extubation history, validated fluid/vasoactive exposure, urine output, and final-24-hour laboratory severity. Do not adjust for post-T0 variables, discharge summaries, or future charting. Check overlap and standardized differences.

Fit logistic regression for the 7-day composite, with splines for continuous severity where support permits, calibration intercept/slope, Brier, AUROC/AUPRC, and bootstrap 95% intervals. Use complete-case and missingness-indicator sensitivity analyses. For time-to-event outcomes use cause-specific Cox and competing-risk analyses. Report cohort flow, missingness, item coverage, event counts, and care-unit stratified estimates.

Split by subject_id: no subject across train/validation/test. Within accessible participants, fixed hash-based 70/15/15 split; hold out test until selection. Catalog participant partition 80-99 is inaccessible and is not external validation.

## Baseline and learned alternative

Interpretable baseline: one row per index stay with age, sex, care unit, ICU length, last FiO2/O2 flow/ventilation status, last HR/MAP/RR, six-hour ranges and abnormal-bin counts, labs, urine output, observation counts, and prespecified context. It tests whether the phenotype and simple summaries explain risk.

Substantive learned alternative: time-aware GRU or temporal-convolution model over 24 hourly bins containing the same numeric values, support indicators, missingness masks, and delta-time channels. Fit on training patients with validation early stopping and predict the same 7-day composite. It retains ordering, persistence, transitions, and informative observation patterns that summaries lose. Require comparison of held-out Brier, calibration, AUROC/AUPRC and bootstrap intervals; learned-model ablation without masks/delta-time; full-24-hour versus last-6-hour model; and the subgroup with reassuring last values but preceding instability. Require phenotype-stratified observed/expected plots and risks, not only global discrimination.

## Falsification and interpretation

Support requires the prespecified direction, a clinically meaningful adjusted primary contrast with a 95% CI excluding the null, adequate calibration, and persistence after missingness and care-unit sensitivity analyses. It supports risk association/monitoring stratification only, not that delaying discharge or changing oxygen improves outcomes.

The hypothesis is adverse/falsified if estimates are near null with adequate precision, reverse direction, or disappear after sensitivity analyses. If learned improvement disappears after removing missingness channels, the claim that robust trajectory information adds beyond summaries is falsified. Null learned-versus-baseline performance is scientifically informative.

Results are inconclusive if event counts are inadequate, intervals are wide, overlap is poor, item parsing unreliable, or splits are unstable. Do not call this support.

Computationally checkable: cohort/feature-window compliance, phenotype and event counts, leakage checks, adjusted estimates, calibration/discrimination, ablations, and uncertainty. Clinical adjudication remains necessary for bedside readiness, discharge appropriateness, goals-of-care limitations, exact oxygen device, ward monitoring, discharge reasons, and whether a monitoring change improves care. These missing data preclude causal and implementation claims and require expert review, prospective validation, or another study.
