# HCC episode 1 working note

## Scientific opening
HCC contains longitudinal inpatient encounters, diagnoses, labs, orders, medications, procedures, pathology and clinical documents. A consequential unresolved question is whether early, routinely available inpatient signals can identify patients whose apparent initial improvement is followed by clinically important deterioration, and whether that signal is clinically useful beyond current severity.

This is not merely a prediction task. The leading claim is that a time-varying recovery/deterioration signature carries information about subsequent clinical instability. The strongest rival is that the signature is an artifact of current severity, coding/documentation intensity, or a delayed label for care already being escalated. A useful experiment must separate these explanations.

## Candidate direction
Use HCC encounters to define a baseline period and a subsequent observation period. Identify patients with an early improvement pattern, then test whether a learned longitudinal representation or a simple severity-adjusted baseline predicts a clinically meaningful deterioration event. The outcome should be explicit and temporally after prediction: for example, new ICU-level escalation, organ-support orders/procedures, or a documented acute deterioration, with exact definitions checked against available tables.

## Method comparison
Baseline: a severity-adjusted logistic/Cox model using a fixed snapshot of current labs, vitals, diagnoses and care intensity, with explicit time-to-event handling.

Alternative: a temporal model or sequence representation fitted on the same encounter-level data to capture trajectory shape and change from the early improvement period. It can reveal whether the direction/slope of recovery, rather than the level of severity, adds information. This is a substantive learned alternative, not a performance race.

## Falsification and interpretation
Supportive: the temporal signal improves held-out prediction after controlling for current severity and care intensity, with calibration and uncertainty reported.
Adverse: no incremental signal, or the signal is explained by current severity/documentation intensity.
Inconclusive: insufficient event density, missing timestamps, or unstable calibration.

This is not causal evidence. A positive result would justify a prospective validation or targeted clinical study; it would not by itself establish that the signal causes deterioration or that acting on it improves outcomes.
