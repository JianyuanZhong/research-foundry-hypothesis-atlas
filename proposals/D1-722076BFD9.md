# Abrupt Reduction in Laboratory Monitoring and Subsequent Clinical Deterioration in ICU Patients

## A Falsifiable, Data-Bound Hypothesis for eICU-CRD (evolved from expert seed eicu-08)

### 1. Hypothesis

In ICU patients with sustained physiological instability (abnormal vital signs or laboratory values in the 6 hours preceding a monitoring gap), an abrupt reduction in the frequency of laboratory testing — defined as a gap exceeding 3× the patient's own median inter-test interval — is associated with a higher risk of subsequent clinical deterioration (worsening of vital signs or key laboratory values within 24 hours after the gap), after adjusting for illness severity, time since ICU admission, and hospital.

**Leading claim (to be tested):** The association between abrupt monitoring gaps and subsequent deterioration is concentrated among patients who are physiologically unstable at the time of the gap. In stable patients, monitoring gaps do not predict deterioration (the gap is a marker of appropriate de-escalation).

**Strongest rival:** Abrupt monitoring gaps are a consequence of clinical improvement (appropriate de-escalation of care in recovering patients), not a cause of deterioration. In this account, the gap–deterioration association is driven by reverse causation: patients who are about to deteriorate are less likely to have been tested recently (because the team has already reduced monitoring in anticipation of discharge or transfer), and the deterioration is the cause, not the consequence, of the reduced testing.

### 2. Scientific Opening

**Established:**
- ICU patients are monitored with serial laboratory tests and vital signs to detect early deterioration. [K2]
- The Choosing Wisely initiative recommends reducing low-value diagnostic testing in ICUs, including routine laboratory panels. The WICH-ICU trial (Larsson 2026) is a stepped-wedge cluster RCT testing whether reduced laboratory testing frequency is safe, with 30-day mortality as the primary outcome. [K1]
- Electronic early warning/track-and-trigger systems use vital signs and laboratory values to detect clinical deterioration, but their performance is limited by heterogeneous prediction targets and inconsistent time horizons. [K2]

**Unresolved:**
- Whether the *pattern* of monitoring (specifically, abrupt gaps in testing frequency) carries prognostic information beyond the level of monitoring intensity. The WICH-ICU trial tests a policy-level intervention (reducing testing frequency across the unit), not the within-patient temporal pattern of testing.
- Whether abrupt monitoring gaps in unstable patients are associated with subsequent deterioration, or whether they are a benign marker of appropriate care de-escalation.
- Whether the association varies across hospitals (the expert seed explicitly notes "variation across hospitals").

**Leading claim:** In unstable ICU patients, abrupt monitoring gaps (interval >3× patient-specific median) are associated with higher odds of subsequent deterioration within 24 hours, independent of severity and hospital. The association is attenuated or absent in stable patients.

**Strongest rival:** The gap–deterioration association is an artifact of reverse causation: patients who are clinically improving have their monitoring reduced (appropriate de-escalation), and the "deterioration" observed after the gap is actually the baseline variability in patients who are recovering. Alternatively, the gap is a proxy for time-of-day effects (nighttime gaps are more common) or for discharge preparation.

### 3. Study Design

#### 3.1 Population
- **Source:** eICU-CRD v2.0 [K3]
- **Inclusion:**
  - Adult ICU stays (age ≥ 18)
  - ≥ 20 key laboratory draws (creatinine, WBC, Hgb, glucose, potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, BUN)
  - Lab span ≥ 24 hours (first to last key lab draw)
  - Valid ICU length of stay (unitdischargeoffset > 0)
- **Exclusion:**
  - Stays with < 5 key labs (insufficient to compute median interval)
  - Stays where the first big gap occurs in the last 6 hours of the stay (no post-gap observation window)
- **Expected cohort:** ~120,000–150,000 stays (based on verified data: 124,435 stays with lab span ≥ 48h; 151,145 with ≥ 24h)

#### 3.2 Exposure: Abrupt Monitoring Gap
- **Definition:** For each patient, compute the sequence of key lab draw times (labresultoffset, in minutes from ICU admission). Calculate the median inter-test interval. A "big gap" is an interval exceeding 3× the patient's own median interval.
- **Index gap:** The first big gap occurring after the first 6 hours of the stay (to allow baseline interval estimation) and at least 6 hours before the last lab draw (to allow post-gap observation).
- **Pre-gap instability:** In the 6 hours before the index gap, compute a composite instability score:
  - Abnormal vitals: HR > 120 or < 50, SpO2 < 90%, SBP < 90 or > 180, RR > 30 or < 8, Temp > 38.5 or < 36.0 (from vitalPeriodic)
  - Abnormal labs: Creatinine > 1.5× baseline, WBC > 12 or < 4, Hgb < 7, Glucose < 60 or > 300, K > 5.5 or < 3.0, Bicarb < 18 (from lab)
  - Instability score = number of abnormal parameters (0–10)
- **Stable vs. unstable:** Patients with instability score ≥ 2 are "unstable"; score 0–1 are "stable."

#### 3.3 Outcome: Subsequent Deterioration
- **Primary outcome:** Clinical deterioration within 24 hours after the index gap, defined as:
  - New or worsening abnormal vital signs (HR > 130 or < 40, SpO2 < 85%, SBP < 80, RR > 35) in vitalPeriodic records in the 24h post-gap window, OR
  - Worsening of key labs (creatinine increase > 0.5 mg/dL, WBC increase > 3, Hgb decrease > 2 g/dL, new glucose < 50 or > 400, K > 6.0 or < 2.5, bicarb < 15) in lab records in the 24h post-gap window
- **Secondary outcome:** ICU mortality (unitdischargestatus = "Expired")
- **Tertiary outcome:** ICU length of stay (unitdischargeoffset)

#### 3.4 Covariates
- Age, gender (patient)
- APACHE II score (apachePatientResult)
- Time since ICU admission at the index gap (hours)
- Hospital (hospitalid, random effect)
- Pre-gap instability score (continuous and categorical)
- Number of key labs before the gap (monitoring intensity)
- Unit type (unittype)

#### 3.5 Analysis Plan

**Primary analysis:**
1. **Descriptive:** Distribution of gap characteristics, instability scores, and outcomes by gap status. Hospital-level variation in gap frequency.
2. **Multilevel logistic regression** (hospital random intercept):
   - Model 1 (baseline): Deterioration ~ instability score + APACHE II + age + gender + time + hospital RE
   - Model 2 (full): Deterioration ~ instability score + APACHE II + age + gender + time + hospital RE + **big gap indicator** + **instability × big gap interaction**
   - Primary estimand: Interaction term (instability × big gap) — tests whether the gap–deterioration association is concentrated in unstable patients
   - Secondary estimand: Main effect of big gap in unstable patients (instability ≥ 2)
3. **Model comparison:** AIC, BIC, likelihood ratio test (Model 1 vs. Model 2)
4. **Sensitivity analyses:**
   - Gap threshold: 2× and 5× median interval (instead of 3×)
   - Pre-gap window: 3h and 12h (instead of 6h)
   - Post-gap window: 12h and 48h (instead of 24h)
   - Exclude last 12h of stay from gap definition
   - Leave-one-hospital-out (top 10 hospitals by volume)
   - Restrict to stays ≥ 48h
   - Use only vital signs (no labs) for instability and deterioration
   - Use only labs (no vitals) for instability and deterioration

**Alternative analysis (XGBoost):**
- Train XGBoost classifier to predict deterioration within 24h after a gap
- Features: all covariates + gap characteristics (gap size in hours, gap/median ratio, time since admission, pre-gap instability)
- Evaluate: AUROC, AUPRC, calibration
- Compare: XGBoost with gap features vs. XGBoost without gap features (tests whether gap information adds predictive value beyond clinical state)
- Whole-hospital holdout validation (leave-one-hospital-out)

**Hospital variation analysis:**
- Compute hospital-level gap frequency and hospital-level deterioration rate
- Test correlation between hospital gap frequency and deterioration rate (Spearman)
- Random effects variance from multilevel model (between-hospital heterogeneity)

#### 3.6 Falsification Criteria

- **Supportive:** The interaction term (instability × big gap) is positive with 95% CI excluding 1 (aOR > 1), AND the main effect of big gap in unstable patients is positive (aOR > 1), AND Model 2 fits better than Model 1 (LRT p < 0.05), AND XGBoost with gap features has higher AUROC than without (ΔAUROC > 0.01), AND the association is consistent across ≥ 80% of hospitals in leave-one-hospital-out analysis.
- **Adverse:** The interaction term is not significant (CI includes 1), OR the main effect of big gap in unstable patients is ≤ 1 (aOR ≤ 1), OR Model 2 does not fit better than Model 1, OR XGBoost with gap features does not improve AUROC.
- **Inconclusive:** Wide CIs (due to low event rate or small effective sample), substantial hospital heterogeneity (I² > 75%), or discordance between regression and XGBoost results.

#### 3.7 Limitations
- **Observational design:** Cannot establish causation. The gap–deterioration association may be driven by reverse causation (improving patients have monitoring reduced).
- **No staffing data:** Cannot distinguish between "appropriate de-escalation" and "inappropriate reduction in monitoring."
- **No continuous monitoring:** eICU-CRD has periodic vitals (every 1–4 hours typically), not continuous arterial line data. Gaps in lab testing may not reflect gaps in overall monitoring.
- **Lab-only exposure:** The gap is defined by laboratory testing only, not by vital sign monitoring or nursing assessments.
- **Composite outcome:** Deterioration is a composite of vital signs and labs; the specific mechanism (which parameter deteriorates) is not specified a priori.
- **Single time period:** 2014–2015 data; monitoring practices may have changed.
- **US-only:** 208 US hospitals; generalizability to other settings is uncertain.

### 5. Method Comparison: Baseline vs. Learned Alternative

**Baseline (multilevel logistic regression):**
- Interpretable, directly tests the interaction hypothesis
- Handles hospital clustering via random intercept
- Provides adjusted odds ratios with CIs
- Limitation: assumes linear effects, may miss non-linear interactions

**Learned alternative (XGBoost):**
- Captures non-linear interactions between gap size, instability, and time
- Whole-hospital holdout tests transportability
- Feature importance reveals which gap characteristics matter most
- Limitation: less interpretable, requires careful hyperparameter tuning

**What the alternative reveals that the baseline loses:**
- Whether the gap–deterioration relationship is non-monotonic (e.g., moderate gaps are worse than very large gaps)
- Whether specific gap characteristics (size, timing, ratio to median) interact with specific instability parameters
- Whether the model's predictive performance degrades when transported to a new hospital (testing the expert seed's "variation across hospitals" hypothesis)

**Selection rationale:** Both methods are run. The regression provides the primary inferential test (interaction term), while XGBoost provides a complementary predictive evaluation and transportability check. They answer different questions: "Is there an association?" (regression) vs. "Does gap information add predictive value beyond clinical state?" (XGBoost).

### 7. What Would Change Our Understanding

- **If supportive:** An abrupt reduction in laboratory monitoring in unstable ICU patients is associated with subsequent deterioration, independent of severity. This would suggest that the *pattern* of monitoring (not just the level) matters for patient safety, and that monitoring gaps in unstable patients may represent a modifiable risk factor. It would inform the WICH-ICU trial's interpretation: reducing testing may be safe in stable patients but risky in unstable patients.
- **If adverse:** Abrupt monitoring gaps are not associated with subsequent deterioration in unstable patients, after adjusting for severity. This would suggest that the gap is a benign marker of appropriate care de-escalation, and that the WICH-ICU trial's policy of reducing testing may be safe even in unstable patients.
- **If inconclusive:** The association is uncertain due to heterogeneity, low event rates, or discordance between methods. This would suggest that the question requires more granular data (continuous monitoring, staffing data) or a different design (e.g., instrumental variable analysis).
