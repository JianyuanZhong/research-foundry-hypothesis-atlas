# Working proposal — Does the early post-TACE lab trajectory persist?

## Unresolved question and clinical importance

Among adults with an HCC diagnosis linked to the encounter at which a first **observed** transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) was recorded, does early change in a clinically validated, same-assay liver-related laboratory measure predict a persistent within-person change 30–90 days later, beyond preprocedure value, preprocedure trend, and testing intensity? Clinically, an early abnormal value can be transient, reflect acute illness or measurement variation, or mark a more persistent loss of reserve that should trigger prospective monitoring before another locoregional procedure. Distinguishing these possibilities could guide a future, clinically adjudicated monitoring study; this dataset cannot itself establish a monitoring rule.

The **falsifiable hypothesis** is that, for one or more expert-validated same-assay measures, the early trajectory (days 0–14) contains incremental information about the observed late (days 30–90) assay value/change over the pre-index level and slope. A time-ordered partially pooled trajectory model should characterize that persistent-versus-resolving pattern better than a pre-index trend baseline, if adequate repeated-measure support exists.

### Evidence, claims and strongest rival

The strongest claim supported by the inspected literature is that hepatic reserve and post-locoregional-therapy decompensation are clinically consequential, and that liver-function trajectories have been studied after TACE [K1, K2]. K1's abstract describes a four-center TACE/TARE cohort and clinically recognizable ascites, variceal bleeding, or encephalopathy outcomes; K2's abstract describes a 162-patient retrospective comparison using time to baseline ALBI-grade progression. Neither validates a lab-only endpoint in this HCC extract, and neither removes treatment selection or co-intervention concerns. Delphi-2M [K3] shows that learned models can use time-ordered disease histories to model temporal dependence, but its diagnosis-code population/task does not establish post-TACE biochemical dynamics.

The **unresolved claim tested here** is descriptive/prognostic: measured early assay trajectory is associated with, and helps estimate, later measured same-assay values in this selected treated cohort. A supportive association or prediction gain is not evidence that TACE caused injury or that the laboratory change is hepatic decompensation. The strongest rival is that pre-existing cirrhosis/tumor progression, baseline severity, co-interventions, selection into TACE, acute illness, regression to the mean, assay drift, and/or more intensive post-procedure testing create an apparent early-to-late pattern. Pre-index pseudo-windows and explicit testing-intensity checks can expose some but not all of these alternatives; unmeasured severity remains unresolved.

## Time boundaries, estimand and falsification

For each qualifying index date, use lab measurements in pre-index days **−90 through −1**, early days **0 through 14**, and late days **30 through 90**; report day-0 values separately as a sensitivity check. Use `start time` as day 0 and `test time` for all lab intervals. Primary target is the patient-assay late-window observed value (median of valid measurements in that window; retain late measurement count and date). The descriptive/prognostic estimand is the difference in held-out late-value estimation and the distribution of within-patient early-to-late assay change when early-window information is added beyond pre-index value/trend. It is conditional on observed measurements and the selected treated population, not a causal contrast. Do not turn a numeric shift into clinical injury without assay validation.

**Negative-time control:** Place a pseudo-index 120 days before actual TACE and reproduce the same widths and eligibility using only pre-TACE labs: pseudo-early at pseudo-days 0–14 (actual TACE-relative days −120 to −106) and pseudo-late at pseudo-days 30–90 (actual −90 to −30). Keep the analysis and patient split identical. An equally large pseudo-index signal, or evidence the trend predates TACE, weakens the proposed post-index-specific trajectory. If those windows lack observations, say the falsification could not be run.

**Measurement/severity rivals:** Compare models with pre-index testing frequency and encounter intensity and report errors/changes by prespecified observation-intensity strata. Compare whether apparent persistence disappears after controlling descriptively for the testing process or after one fixed-window summary per patient. Check whether results are concentrated in admissions or patients with recorded concurrent procedures. Diagnoses lack event timestamps and severity/tumor/imaging and adjudicated complication fields are absent; therefore the local data only partially assess these rivals. Post-index test frequency is an outcome-dependent measurement process, not a proven confounder; modeling it does not identify missing-not-at-random mechanisms.

## Matched model comparison and analysis

Use one locked cohort, same-assay eligibility, date windows and patient-level chronological partition for every method. Sort by first-observed TACE date; allocate earliest 70% of eligible patients to training, next 15% to tuning, latest 15% to final test (freeze date cut points from cohort coverage before inspecting held-out outcomes). Keep all rows and pseudo-indices for a patient in one split. Tune/choose once on training/tuning, then evaluate once on held-out patients. Report patients, assays, number of measurements, missingness and late outcomes by split; no random row split.

1. **Baseline A0 — pre-index trend.** Fit assay-specific regression using pre-index level (last valid result), slope where at least two pre-index dates permit it, age, sex, and pre-index testing/encounter intensity to estimate late-window assay value. This asks whether early change adds anything beyond measured starting reserve/trend and surveillance.
2. **Simple extension A1 — early summary.** Extend A0 with predeclared early-window median and early-minus-pre-index value, using the same assay and adjustment set. This makes the information gain from early results explicit without a complex model.
3. **Learned alternative B — partially pooled segmented trajectory.** Fit a Bayesian continuous-time piecewise trajectory model to the same dated same-assay pre-index and early observations. Include population-level pre/early/late trends and patient- and assay-stratified random intercept/slope and early deviation with partial pooling and a robust observation-error distribution. Produce posterior predictive distributions for late values and patient-specific estimated early deviation/persistence. As a sensitivity component, model assay-observation occurrence/count or interval from prior level and encounter intensity; describe how that changes trajectory estimates, without claiming it resolves unmeasured informative testing.

B is not a mandatory neural network. It is a substantively useful learned alternative because it can estimate heterogeneous resolving versus persistent patterns, partial-pool sparse repeated measures, retain irregular timing, and express uncertainty that the last-value/slope summary suppresses. Compare B with A1 on the same available pre/early information to ask whether trajectory shape/heterogeneity matters, not to award a small accuracy gain. If patient/assay repeated-measure support is too sparse to estimate these components or convergence is poor, defer B and report that dependency; do not silently pool units or invent a new endpoint.

Primary evaluation: held-out assay-stratified MAE for late numeric assay value; also RMSE, calibration intercept/slope, predictive log score/CRPS when supported, and 50/80/95% posterior predictive coverage and width. Estimate 95% uncertainty intervals using paired patient-cluster bootstrap for prediction metric differences, alongside (not instead of) model posterior uncertainty and convergence/effective sample size diagnostics. For change interpretation report signed paired early-to-late within-person distributions with uncertainty and assay mapping. Compare A0→A1 for early incremental information, A1→B for temporal-model information, and actual post-index results against matched pseudo-index. Do not aggregate incomparable assays.

### Prespecified interpretation / falsification criteria

- **Supportive of the bounded hypothesis:** validated same-assay rows have adequate late-window coverage; A1 improves held-out MAE over A0 by a prespecified material amount (initial operational threshold ≥5%, with paired patient-bootstrap interval excluding no improvement); B's uncertainty-calibrated trajectories show clinically reviewed adverse-direction change persisting at days 30–90; and the actual early-to-late pattern is materially larger than the pseudo-window pattern and not confined to high observation intensity or concurrent procedures. The 5% is a decision threshold for this research screen, not a validated clinical effect size; set assay-specific materiality with lab experts before fitting if assay scales make it inappropriate. This result supports prospective study of lab trajectories only.
- **Adverse to the hypothesis:** with sufficient validated assay support and reasonably precise intervals, no adverse-direction late persistence or no incremental early information appears, or matched pseudo-window patterns are as large, or the association is accounted for descriptively by pretrend/testing intensity. This rejects the proposed post-index-specific measured-trajectory signal in these data, not TACE biological injury in general.
- **Inconclusive:** sparse early/late pairs, unknown unit/method equivalence, broad intervals spanning meaningful benefit and no effect, failed model convergence, inadequate temporal split support, or unavailable pseudo-windows. Do not interpret imprecision as evidence against the hypothesis.

These decision rules establish no causal injury or treatment benefit. A causal claim would need dated clinical decompensation and co-intervention, tumor/stage and treatment-severity information, appropriate untreated/alternative-treatment comparators and a defensible causal design. A clinical decision rule would additionally require expert-adjudicated endpoints, assay and outcome validity, external/temporal validation, and evaluation of benefits/harms.

## Alternatives, feasibility and actual deliverable

The actual scientific deliverable is **newly fit estimates and predictions from A0, A1 and (if support/convergence permit) B**, plus a locked independent patient-level test report: cohort/source QC and counts; assay-specific late trajectories; held-out metrics with uncertainty/calibration; fitted posterior trajectory and observation-process outputs; matched pseudo-window and observation-intensity comparisons; and interpretation of supportive/adverse/inconclusive results. This proposal is a design; no patient-level clinical effect or model result was computed during discovery.

Planned CPU envelope: source CSVs include a 2.2-GB lab file and 35-MB procedure file, so stream/column-select parsing, cache derived eligible lab rows, and avoid broad materialization. Future solver planning limit is 16 CPUs, 256 GiB memory and 8 hours (not a discovery budget). Expected baseline fitting after cohort extraction under 30 minutes and hierarchical model 1–4 hours at patient-assay summary/repeated-measure scale with 4–8 CPUs and 16–32 GiB are **unmeasured estimates**; package availability, cohort support, runtime and Bayesian convergence are unverified. No GPU is currently justified: models are moderate tabular longitudinal fits; reconsider only if measured profiling shows matrix compute dominates. No CUDA probe or full study was run.

**Alternatives explicitly not selected.** A neural disease-sequence/transformer adaptation inspired by Delphi [K3] could learn complex timing dependencies, but local diagnoses lack diagnosis timestamps and episode-scale repeated assay data may be too sparse to justify its added representation/compute. Defer unless support is adequate and the hierarchical model misses reproducible trajectory structure. The full Oncoformer multimodal demonstration was not inspected as full text: only its supplement is available per the reference guide, and HCC image payloads are unavailable; a lab-only adaptation would not reproduce its multimodal claim and adds little over this same-assay design. ALADYNOULLI's latent longitudinal signature method is a possible exploratory alternative, but no external genetic inputs or validated common-unit matrix are available and latent states are not needed for this specific persistence-vs-surveillance test; revisit only if assay harmonization and cohort size support stable latent structure. None of these deferrals is based on a categorical neural/GPU prohibition.
