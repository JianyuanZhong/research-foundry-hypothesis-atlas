# Do models that encode laboratory testing practices lose more performance under whole-hospital transport?

## Scientific opening and hypothesis

Clinicians and prediction models often rely on laboratory values, but the frequency with which those values are measured and the pattern of missingness can vary substantially across hospitals. A model that learns hospital-specific testing practices may perform well within its training hospitals but lose performance when transported to a hospital with different testing intensity. The unresolved question is whether including testing-frequency and missingness-pattern features makes a model more or less transportable than a model using only clinical laboratory values.

Hypothesis: Among eICU ICU stays, a prediction model that includes laboratory testing frequency and missingness patterns loses more performance under whole-hospital transport than a comparable model using only clinical laboratory values, after accounting for illness severity and hospital effects.

## Clinical importance

If models encode testing practices rather than patient condition, their predictions may be unreliable when deployed in hospitals with different laboratory workflows. This has direct implications for clinical decision support, risk stratification, and the generalizability of ICU prediction models. A negative result (testing-practice features improve or do not harm transportability) would suggest that such features capture useful patient-level information rather than purely institutional artifacts.

## Evidence context

[K1] Wang et al. 2026 provide eICU-CRD context for creatinine/urine-output AKI criteria and the importance of specifying which criteria are available, supporting the need to define outcomes and data availability explicitly. [K2] Ren et al. 2025 show that intraoperative hypotension trajectories, not only minimum values, can carry prognostic information, supporting the relevance of temporal patterns in ICU data. [K3] Ensarioğlu & Yıldırım 2026 distinguish hypotension burden from waveform features, motivating a careful separation of patient-level signals from measurement artifacts.

## Population and temporal boundaries

Use the eICU CRD 2.0 source files listed in `datasets/eicu/README.md`, with patient-level joins only within eICU. Define the cohort as adult ICU stays with at least one early laboratory record and a creatinine value available before or at the index time. Define the exposure window as the first 24 hours after the first laboratory record, or a prespecified alternative 12-hour window if the 24-hour definition produces sparse outcomes.

The outcome is subsequent creatinine-defined AKI, defined as an increase in creatinine to at least 2 times the baseline value within 72 hours after the exposure window. Baseline creatinine is the first creatinine value available before or at the index time.

## Data bindings

- Source files: `datasets/eicu/README.md` lists 31 source files, including `patient.csv`, `lab.csv`, `vital.csv`, `disposition.csv`, and `hospital.csv`.
- Tables: `patient` (patient demographics, admission time, discharge time, hospital ID), `lab` (laboratory values, timestamps, test names), `vital` (vital signs, timestamps), `disposition` (ICU discharge, hospital discharge, death), `hospital` (hospital identifiers and characteristics).
- Columns: `patient_id`, `admission_time`, `discharge_time`, `hospital_id`, `lab_name`, `lab_value`, `lab_time`, `vital_name`, `vital_value`, `vital_time`, `disposition_type`, `disposition_time`.
- Joins: `patient_id` within eICU; `hospital_id` for whole-hospital validation.
- Time fields: `admission_time`, `discharge_time`, `lab_time`, `vital_time`, `disposition_time`.

## Covariates and features

- Clinical laboratory values: creatinine, BUN, electrolytes, and other available laboratory values.
- Testing frequency: number of laboratory tests per unit time, calculated from `lab_time` and `lab_name`.
- Missingness patterns: proportion of expected laboratory tests that are missing, calculated from the difference between expected and observed test counts.
- Illness severity: APACHE II score, calculated from available vital signs and laboratory values.
- Hospital effects: hospital ID, included as a fixed effect or random effect in the model.

## Baseline versus learned alternative

- Baseline: A logistic regression model using only clinical laboratory values and illness severity, without testing-frequency or missingness-pattern features.
- Learned alternative: A gradient-boosted tree model using clinical laboratory values, illness severity, testing-frequency, and missingness-pattern features.

The learned alternative can reveal whether testing-practice features carry useful information beyond clinical values, and whether that information is transportable across hospitals.

## Analysis and validation

- Whole-hospital validation: Train the model on a subset of hospitals and validate on held-out hospitals, ensuring no patient from a validation hospital appears in the training set.
- Performance metrics: AUROC, calibration (Brier score, calibration-in-the-large, calibration-slope), and net benefit (decision curve analysis).
- Uncertainty: Bootstrap confidence intervals for performance metrics, with 1000 resamples.
- Falsification criteria: If the learned alternative does not lose more performance under whole-hospital transport than the baseline, the hypothesis is not supported. If the learned alternative performs better or similarly, the hypothesis is adverse. If the performance difference is not statistically significant, the result is inconclusive.

## Limitations and next steps

- This is an association study, not a causal study. Hospital effects may not be fully captured by hospital ID.
- The outcome is creatinine-defined AKI, which may not capture all forms of kidney injury.
- The next step is to implement the cohort construction, exposure/outcome windows, and whole-hospital validation, and to run the bounded coverage diagnostic.
