# Residual physiologic instability in the final 12 hours before live ICU discharge

Status: proposed adaptation of assessed repairable expert seed [starting question]; not a reproduction and no results have been computed.

## Scientific question and falsifiable hypothesis

Among adults with a first eligible live ICU discharge during a hospital admission, does residual physiologic instability in the preceding 12 hours identify a higher probability of a subsequent ICU readmission or in-hospital death, and does the ordering and persistence of that instability add information beyond a transparent landmark summary?

Primary hypothesis: after adjustment for prespecified admission and ICU-stay context, greater pre-discharge instability burden is associated with a higher 48-hour cumulative incidence of the first adverse event (ICU readmission or in-hospital death). The longitudinal hypothesis is that a model using the ordered hourly trajectory (including persistence, recovery, and missingness) improves held-out competing-risk prediction and calibration over a matched model using only summary statistics from the same 12-hour window. The alternative can be adverse: a flat summary may perform as well, showing that ordering is not needed for this decision.

This is not a claim that the risk is preventable, that discharge caused the outcome, or that a model should delay discharge. MIMIC-IV does not provide a validated preventability label, discharge-intent/clinical-readiness assessment, ward monitoring intensity, clinician rationale, or a randomized discharge decision.

## Why it matters and what is new

A clinically consequential decision is whether a patient is sufficiently recovered for ICU-to-ward transition. Existing risk prediction can show that sicker patients have worse outcomes, but it does not establish whether the residual trajectory immediately before discharge carries information beyond static context. A positive result would support targeted prospective review of patients with persistent or worsening instability at the transition; a null result would discourage treating late physiologic noise as a reason for more ICU care. The substantive advance is a prespecified comparison of an interpretable landmark burden with a longitudinal representation on identical, leakage-controlled inputs, while modeling readmission and death as distinct first events.

Novelty is not certified here. A public search found a 2026 abstract describing a MIMIC-IV postoperative readmission benchmark with sequential and aggregate models, but its full article was not acquired or inspected; that different valve-replacement cohort does not establish this question. A systematic review and direct comparison with any prior ICU-discharge score are required before clinical claims.

## Population, landmark, and temporal boundaries

Use MIMIC-IV 3.1 core plus the separately released notes, snapshot [source checksum].

1. Start with icu/icustays rows having non-null subject_id, hadm_id, stay_id, intime, outtime, inner-joined to hosp/admissions on subject_id, hadm_id.
2. Keep adults using patients.anchor_age (join on subject_id), with age interpreted according to the catalog convention (anchor_age >89 represented as 91). Exclude neonatal/pediatric cases by age <18.
3. Define an eligible live ICU discharge at t0 = icustays.outtime only when t0 <= admissions.dischtime and deathtime is null or strictly later than t0. Do not use future outcomes or notes to define eligibility. A missing death time is not proof of survival after the hospitalization.
4. To avoid correlated landmarks and ambiguous prior readmissions, retain the earliest eligible ICU discharge per subject_id, hadm_id, ordered by outtime. Record the excluded later eligible stays as a sensitivity analysis, not as the primary cohort.
5. The feature window is the half-open interval [t0 - 12 hours, t0). Events qualify only when their clinical time and their entry time are both before t0: charttime < t0 and storetime < t0 for chart/lab events; starttime < t0 and (null endtime or endtime > t0-12h) for interval events. Restrict events to the index stay_id and times between intime and t0.
6. Use one fixed, subject-level 70/15/15 train/validation/test split, stratified on the primary first-event class where feasible; every subject_id and all its admissions belong to one split. Fit scaling, imputation, thresholds learned from data, model selection, and calibration only in train/validation. The held-out test is used once for final estimates.

## Exposure construction

Bin the 12-hour window into twelve one-hour bins. For each physiologic item, retain the last eligible value in each bin after unit/range checks, plus a missingness indicator and count of raw observations. Resolve arterial versus non-invasive MAP by retaining both source-specific variables and a predeclared priority rule for a combined MAP; do not average incomparable units. Normalize FiO2 to a fraction after verifying the local encoding; retain O2 flow separately because room-air FiO2 and nasal-cannula flow are not interchangeable.

The interpretable residual-instability index (RII), defined without test-set tuning, is:

- in each observed hourly bin, flag MAP <65 mmHg, heart rate <50 or >120 bpm, respiratory rate <8 or >24/min, SpO2 <92%, temperature <36 or >38 C, FiO2 >0.21 or O2 flow >2 L/min;
- use the fraction of observed domain-hours flagged, the number of affected domains, the longest consecutive flagged-bin run, and the mean absolute successive change after train-derived robust scaling;
- separately flag invasive/NIV overlap and recent vasopressor interval, and include laboratory latest value plus change from the earliest eligible value as secondary features;
- require at least four distinct hourly bins with any eligible vital/oxygen observation for the primary RII analysis; report coverage and repeat with six bins. Do not code unobserved as normal. The learned model can use all eligible stays through explicit missingness masks, with the four-bin cohort as a matched sensitivity analysis.

The cutoffs are an operational phenotype, not clinical adjudication. Prespecify a sensitivity using train-set quintiles of each continuous feature and a sensitivity excluding treatment-dependent oxygen/vasopressor variables.

## Outcomes and competing risks

Follow from t0 until the earliest of hospital discharge, the stated horizon, or a first event.

- ICU readmission: a subsequent icustays.intime > t0 with the same subject_id, hadm_id; require a positive non-overlap with the prior stay and report a stricter sensitivity requiring at least 1 hour between outtime and next intime.
- In-hospital death: admissions.deathtime > t0 and before/equal to dischtime, or a consistent hospital_expire_flag death record after t0. Resolve discordance explicitly and report the number excluded for impossible timing. The primary death event is death before a readmission; death after readmission is a secondary post-readmission outcome.
- Primary horizon: 48 hours. Secondary horizons: 7 days and to hospital discharge.
- Primary estimand: cause-specific cumulative incidence of first ICU readmission and first death, and the composite first adverse event, comparing RII (per train-derived SD and prespecified categories) with adjustment covariates.
- Treat death as a competing event when estimating readmission CIF, and readmission as a competing event when estimating death CIF. Use cause-specific pooled discrete-time hazards (or Fine-Gray as a sensitivity, not the sole analysis), with inverse-probability/censoring handling only if censoring before hospital discharge is introduced. Do not label a competing event as ordinary non-event in CIF calculations.

## Models, baselines, and evaluation

The actual deliverable is two newly fitted risk estimators plus an association analysis, not a result claimed in advance.

Baseline L (transparent landmark): penalized cause-specific discrete-time logistic hazards at 48 hours (and the 7-day sensitivity), using the RII summaries and the same prespecified admission/stay covariates: age, gender, admission_type, admission_location, first/last careunit, ICU LOS, hospital day at t0, post-ICU destination from transfers, prior ICU count within the admission before t0, and missingness/observation count. Continuous covariates are train-scaled; categorical levels are train-fitted. Report coefficient/odds-ratio directions and calibration.

Longitudinal alternative T: a small masked GRU (with a prespecified temporal-transformer sensitivity only if sample/support permits) over the same twelve hourly bins, with numeric values, train-scaled values, domain masks, observation counts, and treatment interval indicators; append the same static covariates at the final state. Train it to predict the same discrete-time readmission/death hazards with competing-risk softmax (readmission, death, no event) and censoring mask. Early stopping and hyperparameters are validation-only. This model can reveal whether ordering, persistence, recovery after an excursion, and informative observation gaps add information that min/max/latest summaries lose. It cannot reveal causal mechanism or clinical intent.

Compare L and T on the untouched test split using bootstrap confidence intervals clustered by subject for: cause-specific time-dependent AUROC and AUPRC, 48-hour CIF Brier score/integrated Brier score, calibration slope/intercept and calibration plots, and the paired difference in Brier score. A model is not scientifically preferred for a tiny discrimination gain without calibration and clinical decision analysis. Prespecify a net-benefit analysis only across clinically supplied threshold ranges; do not invent a treatment threshold from MIMIC. Report subgroup performance by careunit, discharge destination, sex, age group, and measurement density, with uncertainty and no causal interpretation.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive evidence requires all of: (1) a monotonic association between higher RII and higher adjusted first-event CIF with uncertainty that excludes no association in the primary analysis; (2) acceptable event support and no material reversal under six-bin coverage, strict timing, and treatment-exposure sensitivities; and (3) for the longitudinal claim, a reproducible improvement in test-set integrated Brier/calibration over L with subject-clustered uncertainty, not merely training performance. This supports prognostic/incremental information, not preventability or a discharge policy.

Adverse evidence is a null or reversed RII association, poor transport across careunits/destinations, or no longitudinal improvement after leakage and missingness controls. That would falsify the proposed residual-instability signal or show that the measured variables do not add usable information; it would not prove discharge is safe.

Inconclusive evidence includes too few eligible measurements/events, unstable estimates, wide intervals, poor calibration, or unresolved timestamp/units. It requires a larger or externally validated cohort rather than a forced conclusion.

Computationally checkable claims: cohort counts and exclusions; field/item availability and timestamp compliance; feature and outcome construction; no train/test subject overlap; model convergence; held-out discrimination, CIF, Brier, calibration, uncertainty, subgroup results; and whether conclusions match those outputs. Clinically unobservable here: whether instability was preventable, whether it reflected true bedside physiology rather than charting/monitoring intensity, whether an intervention would avert readmission/death, whether discharge was appropriate, and whether a deployment would improve outcomes. These require chart review/adjudication, prospective monitoring, an external hospital, and ideally an intervention study.

## Alternatives considered and selection record

1. Static-only logistic baseline using admission/stay covariates was retained as the clinical context comparator but is insufficient as the main scientific baseline because it cannot test late physiologic information.
2. The selected landmark summary model L is the transparent primary comparator: CPU-feasible, interpretable, and directly tests whether residual burden matters.
3. The selected masked GRU T is a substantive but bounded longitudinal alternative on identical inputs; it tests trajectory information rather than model complexity for its own sake. A transformer is deferred to a sensitivity because a 12-bin window may not justify its additional parameters.
4. A mechanistic state-space model was deferred: no verified waveform data or validated organ-state labels are available, and a mechanistic latent state would require assumptions not identifiable from these tables.
5. Text/NLP and multimodal models were excluded from the primary study because notes can leak post-landmark information and MIMIC images/raw waveforms are unavailable. Discharge and radiology text may be evaluated only in a separately time-locked exploratory study with expert review.
6. Fine-Gray alone was rejected because it does not replace reporting cause-specific hazards and CIFs; it remains a sensitivity analysis.
7. A neural model is not excluded on principle. GPU use is optional: the small baseline should fit on CPU; the GRU can fit on CPU or one allocated A100. No GPU discovery claim is needed.
