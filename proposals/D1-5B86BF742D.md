# Provisional eICU hypothesis: laboratory-monitoring reduction during persistent ICU instability

Status: provisional and untested. This is a design draft, not an analysis result.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a continuous ICU stay who remain physiologically unstable at a prespecified index, a reduction in laboratory-testing intensity during the preceding 12 hours is associated with higher risk of a time-local deterioration in the following 12 hours, beyond contemporaneously measured severity and recent support. The association should attenuate when end-of-life documentation, discharge/transfer context, and recent support de-escalation are handled explicitly. A residual association in held-out hospitals would be a monitoring-process signal, not evidence that changing laboratory orders causes deterioration.

## Why it matters

A robust association would identify a potentially actionable monitoring-process signal for prospective review. Attenuation would show that the apparent gap is better explained by severity, timing, discharge, or documentation behavior. A negative or imprecise result would constrain claims that less testing means worse outcomes and redirect work toward measurement/process validation rather than a treatment intervention.

## Evidence-supported versus unresolved claims

The configured eICU catalog and table metadata support that the dataset contains longitudinal, timestamped laboratory, vital, respiratory, infusion, patient, and hospital records, with patientunitstayid as the within-dataset join key and hospitalid for hospital-level stratification. The expert seed [starting question] supplies the opening that testing frequency/missingness may encode hospital practice and may transport poorly. It does not establish the association, causality, endpoint validity, or that the required rows are sufficiently populated.

The unresolved claim is whether a dynamic reduction in testing during persistent instability predicts subsequent deterioration after time-local severity and care-context adjustment. No full cohort analysis has been run in this episode.

## Population and temporal boundaries

Use adults in continuous ICU stays, excluding stays that cannot establish a valid index or have insufficient observation time. At each eligible landmark, require a prespecified window of prior observations and a 12-hour future window. Define the exposure as the change in counts of qualifying laboratory observations over two consecutive 12-hour windows, with a fixed laboratory panel and missingness rules chosen before analysis. Define instability from contemporaneous vital/respiratory/infusion measurements, with a prespecified threshold and sensitivity analyses. The primary outcome is a new time-local deterioration event in the next 12 hours, such as a prespecified respiratory-support escalation or hemodynamic deterioration, with death, discharge, and transfer treated as competing events. Avoid using future values as predictors.

## Baseline and substantive alternative

Baseline: a prespecified severity-adjusted comparison using counts or rates of qualifying labs in the preceding window, with fixed clinical thresholds and a competing-risk model for deterioration, death, discharge, and transfer. This answers whether the association exists under a transparent process definition.

Alternative: a longitudinal model using the sequence of laboratory intensity, vital/respiratory/infusion trajectories, and time-varying care context, with hospital-held-out evaluation and uncertainty intervals. It can reveal whether the signal depends on trajectory shape, persistence, or a specific transition rather than a single interval count, information the baseline loses. A mechanistic interpretation remains unavailable because the data cannot establish why testing changed.

## Analysis, falsification, and interpretation

Fit the baseline and alternative on the same eligible landmarks, with prespecified missingness rules, cluster/hospital handling, and uncertainty intervals. Compare adjusted associations and calibration/discrimination only as secondary descriptive performance. Use negative-control outcomes or pre-index windows where scientifically valid, and sensitivity analyses excluding end-of-life documentation, support de-escalation, and discharge/transfer. A supportive result requires a prespecified direction, clinically meaningful effect, uncertainty excluding the null under the stated analysis, and persistence in held-out hospitals after rival adjustment. An adverse result is attenuation to null, reversal, or failure across hospitals/definitions, supporting severity/documentation explanations. An inconclusive result is wide uncertainty, sparse events, or unstable endpoint definitions; it does not refute the hypothesis.

The experiment cannot establish that ordering fewer tests causes deterioration, identify a biological mechanism, or justify changing care. Those claims require prospective monitoring, clinical adjudication of deterioration, and ideally an intervention study.
