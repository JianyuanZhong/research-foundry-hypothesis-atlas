# Hypothesis: Recovery Rate vs Recovery Pattern in ICU Prognostication

## Scientific Question

**Unresolved Question**: In critically ill patients who have achieved circulatory stabilization, does the *rate* of multi-organ recovery (how quickly each organ system returns toward baseline function) carry independent prognostic information beyond the *pattern* of asynchronous recovery (which organs recover faster than others)?

**Related Prior Work**: [prior hypothesis] tested whether asynchronous recovery *patterns* predict subsequent deterioration after circulatory stabilization. This new hypothesis asks whether recovery *rate* (not just pattern) predicts long-term outcomes.

**Clinical Importance**: Current ICU prognostication relies heavily on single-timepoint severity scores (SOFA, APACHE II) that capture only a snapshot and miss the dynamic recovery process. Understanding whether recovery *rate* (not just pattern) predicts outcomes could:

1. Identify high-risk patients who may need intensified monitoring despite "good" recovery patterns
2. Inform timing of clinical decision points (when to escalate vs de-escalate therapy)
3. Distinguish between patients who will recover quickly vs slowly, regardless of initial pattern

**Substantive Advance Over Existing Knowledge**:
- **Limitation of current approaches**: Single-timepoint severity scores assume recovery is either complete or incomplete at a single time. They miss the *kinetics* of recovery.
- **Limitation of MIMIC-01 adaptation**: Tested whether *which organs recover faster* (pattern) predicts deterioration. This new hypothesis asks whether *how fast organs recover* (rate) predicts outcomes.
- **Novelty**: Recovery rate has not been systematically tested as a prognostic factor in ICU settings. Most studies focus on severity scores or binary recovery indicators.

## Study Population and Main Data

**Population**: MIMIC-IV ICU patients meeting inclusion criteria:
- Age ≥18 years
- Admitted for infection or shock (ICD-10 codes for infectious diseases or shock)
- Achieved circulatory stabilization (MAP ≥ 65 mmHg for ≥24 hours)
- Follow-up ≥7 days from stabilization
- At least one organ support indicator during ICU stay

**Key Variables**:
- **Baseline**: SOFA score at stabilization, age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index
- **Exposure**: Recovery rate for each organ system (rate of change in organ support/labs over time)
- **Outcome**:
  - Primary: 90-day mortality or ICU readmission
  - Secondary: Time to recovery of individual organ systems, number of organ systems recovered

**Temporal Boundaries**:
- Baseline: Time of circulatory stabilization (first time MAP ≥ 65 mmHg for ≥24 hours)
- Follow-up: 90 days from baseline
- Recovery assessment: Daily organ support requirements and lab values

## Hypothesis to Test

**Primary Hypothesis**: At the same recovery pattern (similar relative rates of improvement across organs), patients with faster absolute recovery rates have better long-term outcomes (lower mortality/readmission).

**Mathematical Formulation**:
- Let R_i(t) be the recovery rate of organ i at time t
- Let P be the recovery pattern (relative ordering of R_i(t) across organs)
- Let Y be the outcome (90-day mortality/readmission)
- H: E[Y | R, P] = f(P) + g(R) + ε, where g(R) < 0 (faster rate = better outcome)

**Expected Effect**:
- Recovery rate (per day) should be negatively associated with 90-day mortality (HR < 1 per unit increase in rate)
- This effect should persist after adjusting for: baseline severity, age, sex, comorbidities, recovery pattern

**Falsification Criteria**:
1. No association between recovery rate and outcomes (HR ≈ 1, p > 0.05)
2. OR positive association (faster recovery associated with worse outcomes, HR > 1)
3. OR significant interaction between recovery rate and recovery pattern (pattern dominates rate)

## Baseline Model

**Stratified Cox Proportional Hazards Model**:
- Hazard of 90-day mortality/readmission
- Covariates: Binary delayed recovery indicator, baseline SOFA, age, sex, Charlson index
- Stratification by recovery pattern (4-quartile grouping of relative recovery speeds)

**Expected Baseline Performance**: C-index ≈ 0.65-0.70 (typical for ICU prognostication models)

## Alternative Model

**Time-Varying Recovery Rate Model**:
- Continuous recovery rate (not just binary delayed recovery)
- Organ-specific recovery rates (kidney, respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic, CNS)
- Interaction terms: Recovery rate × Recovery pattern
- Time-varying covariates: Daily organ support requirements

**Scientific Question**: Does the *combination* of recovery rate and pattern provide better prediction than either alone?

## Methodology

1. **Data Preparation**:
   - Identify stabilization events (MAP ≥ 65 mmHg for ≥24 hours)
   - Calculate recovery rate for each organ system (rate of change in organ support/labs)
   - Define recovery pattern (relative recovery speeds across organs)
   - Handle missing data (multiple imputation or listwise deletion)

2. **Cox Proportional Hazards Analysis**:
   - Primary: Recovery rate as continuous predictor
   - Secondary: Recovery rate × Pattern interaction
   - Sensitivity: Different outcome definitions (mortality, readmission, composite)

3. **Model Comparison**:
   - Baseline: Stratified Cox by delayed recovery indicator
   - Alternative: Continuous recovery rate with interactions
   - Metrics: C-index, calibration (Brier score), net reclassification improvement (NRI)

4. **Robustness Checks**:
   - Sensitivity to stabilization definition (different MAP thresholds)
   - Sensitivity to outcome definition (different time windows)
   - Subgroup analysis (different diagnoses, severity levels, age groups)

## Evaluation Criteria

**Supportive Results**:
- Recovery rate negatively associated with 90-day mortality (HR < 1, p < 0.05)
- Effect persists after adjusting for baseline severity, age, sex, comorbidities
- C-index improvement ≥0.02 over baseline model
- Calibration improved (Brier score lower)

**Adverse Results**:
- No association between recovery rate and outcomes (HR ≈ 1, p > 0.05)
- OR positive association (faster recovery associated with worse outcomes, HR > 1)
- OR significant interaction (pattern dominates rate)
- C-index no improvement or worse than baseline

**Inconclusive Results**:
- Weak association (0.01 < HR < 1.01)
- High uncertainty (wide confidence intervals)
- Sensitivity analyses yield inconsistent results

## Limitations

1. **Observational Design**: Cannot establish causation; confounding by indication possible
2. **Recovery Rate Definition**: Requires careful definition of "baseline" and "recovery"; may vary by organ system
3. **Missing Data**: Time-varying measurements may be incomplete; imputation introduces uncertainty
4. **Single Dataset**: MIMIC-IV from multiple centers but not external validation
5. **Threshold Selection**: Recovery rate thresholds need clinical validation
6. **Reverse Causation**: Poor outcomes may affect recovery measurements
