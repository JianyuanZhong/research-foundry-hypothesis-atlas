## Hypothesis to test

At the same BMI, people with higher visceral adiposity (lower weight-adjusted waist index) are more likely to develop cardiovascular and kidney disease, whereas people with lower muscle strength (lower grip strength) are more likely to develop infection or disability. This differential pattern suggests that body fat and muscle strength have distinct pathways to different disease outcomes.

## Study population and main data

- **Population**: UK Biobank participants aged 40-69 at baseline (assessment center visit)
- **Main data tables**:
  - `population`: Age, sex, recruitment date, assessment center ID
  - `main`: BMI, waist circumference, imaging flag
  - `biological_samples`: Grip strength (kg), body composition measures
  - `health_outcomes`: Cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, infection, disability outcomes

## Temporal boundaries

- **Baseline**: Assessment center visit (index date from `population.age_at_assessment`)
- **Follow-up**: From baseline until first occurrence of outcome or censoring (death, loss to follow-up, end of study)
- **Imaging requirement**: Imaging must precede outcome (baseline imaging only)

## Key variables

### Exposure measures
- **BMI**: kg/m² from `main` table
- **Visceral adiposity proxy**: Weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) = waist circumference (cm) / sqrt(height (m) × BMI)
- **Muscle strength proxy**: Grip strength (kg) from `biological_samples`, standardized by age and sex

### Outcome measures
- **Cardiovascular disease**: CVD events from `health_outcomes` (ICD codes for CVD)
- **Kidney disease**: CKD events from `health_outcomes` (ICD codes for kidney disease)
- **Infection**: Infection events from `health_outcomes` (ICD codes for infections)
- **Disability**: Incident disability from `health_outcomes` (ICD codes for disability)

### Covariates
- Age, sex, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity (from `population` and `main`)
- Comorbidities (from `health_outcomes` history)
- Medication use (from `additional_exposures`)

## Baseline model

A stratified Cox proportional hazards model estimating hazard ratios for each outcome, stratified by BMI tertiles, with:
- Main effect of WWI (continuous)
- Main effect of grip strength (continuous, standardized)
- Adjusted for age, sex, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, comorbidities

**Null hypothesis**: No differential effect of WWI vs grip strength on outcomes within the same BMI tertile.

## Alternative model

A body composition-adjusted Cox model including:
- WWI and grip strength as joint predictors
- Interaction terms: WWI × grip strength (testing whether their effects differ)
- Mediation analysis: testing whether grip strength partially mediates the WWI-outcome relationship

## Falsification criteria

The hypothesis is falsified if:
1. **No differential effect**: Within each BMI tertile, the hazard ratio for WWI and grip strength are statistically indistinguishable (95% CIs overlap substantially, ΔHR < 0.10)
2. **Opposite pattern**: WWI is associated with reduced risk of CVD/kidney disease, or grip strength is associated with reduced risk of infection/disability
3. **No effect**: Neither WWI nor grip strength shows significant association with outcomes after adjustment (all p > 0.05)

The hypothesis is supported if:
1. WWI shows stronger association with CVD/kidney disease than grip strength shows with infection/disability within the same BMI tertile
2. The WWI-outcome interaction is significant (p < 0.05)
3. Effect sizes are clinically meaningful (ΔC-index ≥ 0.03 between models)

## Scientific significance

This study addresses an important clinical gap: BMI is widely used for risk stratification but fails to capture body composition differences. By demonstrating that visceral adiposity and muscle strength have differential effects on different disease outcomes, this work could:
1. Improve risk prediction models by incorporating body composition measures
2. Guide targeted interventions (e.g., muscle-strengthening for infection prevention, weight-loss for CVD prevention)
3. Inform clinical guidelines on body composition assessment

## Main limitations

1. **Imaging selection bias**: Only participants with baseline imaging are included, potentially introducing selection bias
2. **Single measurement**: Baseline grip strength and WWI may not represent long-term body composition
3. **Reverse causation**: Pre-existing disease may affect body composition measures
4. **Residual confounding**: Unmeasured factors may influence both body composition and outcomes
5. **Measurement error**: Self-reported or single-timepoint measures may have error
