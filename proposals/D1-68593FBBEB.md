# Abrupt Reduction in Laboratory Testing During Persistent ICU Instability: Association with Subsequent Deterioration and Hospital-Level Variation

## 1. Unresolved Question and Clinical Importance

In the ICU, laboratory testing is a core component of monitoring and clinical decision-making. Clinicians order tests in response to changing patient states, and the timing and frequency of testing reflect both the patient's clinical trajectory and institutional monitoring practices. A clinically consequential but unresolved question is:

> **Among ICU patients who remain clinically unstable, does an abrupt reduction in the rate of laboratory testing associate with subsequent clinical deterioration, and does the strength of this association vary by hospital?**

This question matters for three reasons:

1. **Patient safety.** If a reduction in testing during instability is associated with subsequent deterioration, it may identify a window in which increased monitoring could be beneficial. Conversely, if the association is null or reversed, it may suggest that testing intensity is not a useful proxy for patient risk or that current testing practices are already adequate.

2. **Variation in care.** If the association differs by hospital, it may reflect differences in monitoring culture, staffing, or resource allocation. This could inform quality-improvement efforts targeting hospitals with weaker associations between testing and outcomes.

3. **Causal interpretation.** The strongest alternative explanation is that reduced testing is a *consequence* of the patient's clinical state (e.g., the patient is improving, or the clinical team is de-escalating care at end of life), not a cause of deterioration. This proposal explicitly addresses this confounding by conditioning on current clinical state and by framing the result as an association, not a causal effect.

## 2. Closest Relevant Prior Work

### [K1] Laboratory testing intensity and ICU outcomes

The WICH-ICU trial [K1] is a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial designed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of reducing routine laboratory testing and arterial blood gas sampling in ICU patients. The trial uses a causal framework (randomized timing of intervention implementation) to study the effect of reduced testing intensity on patient outcomes, including 30-day mortality, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation. However, the WICH-ICU trial does not examine *abrupt changes* in testing rate during periods of persistent instability. It also does not assess hospital-level variation in the association between testing reduction and outcomes.

**What this proposal adds:** This proposal examines the association between abrupt reduction in testing rate and subsequent deterioration, conditional on current clinical state, which is a different estimand from the WICH-ICU trial. The proposal also assesses hospital-level variation in the association, which is not a primary outcome of the WICH-ICU trial.

### [K2] Monitoring and patient outcomes

The systematic review by Jansen and Veldhuis [K2] evaluates whether the implementation of vital sign-based Early Warning Scores (EWS) improves patient outcomes in low- and middle-income countries. The review found that EWS implementation was not associated with a consistent reduction in mortality or serious adverse events, but two studies reported shorter length of hospital stays and one study demonstrated a reduction in unplanned ICU admissions. The review highlights that the effectiveness of monitoring interventions may vary by setting and context, which is relevant to the question of whether the association between testing reduction and deterioration varies by hospital.

**What this proposal adds:** This proposal applies a similar framework to the dynamic pattern of laboratory testing during ICU stays, rather than to the implementation of a fixed monitoring protocol. The proposal also conditions on current clinical state, which is not done in the EWS review.

### [K3] Hospital-level variation in care processes

The study by Bedard-Lapointe et al. [K3] describes and compares hospital-onset bacteremia (HOB) rates across 114 hospitals in Ontario, Canada. The study found significant variability in HOB rates across hospitals, with a random intercept SD of 0.36 (95% CI, 0.29-0.42) in a generalized linear mixed-effects model. This provides a methodological template for assessing hospital-level variation in care processes, which is relevant to the question of whether the association between testing reduction and deterioration varies by hospital.

**What this proposal adds:** This proposal applies a similar hierarchical model framework to the dynamic pattern of laboratory testing during ICU stays, rather than to a static outcome like hospital-onset bacteremia. The proposal also conditions on current clinical state, which is not done in the Ontario study.

## 3. Hypothesis

**Primary hypothesis (falsifiable):**

> Among ICU patients who remain clinically unstable (as defined by a composite instability score), an abrupt reduction in the rate of laboratory testing (defined as a ≥50% decrease in the number of distinct lab tests ordered per 12-hour window, compared to the patient's own prior 24-hour rate) is associated with a higher 48-hour risk of clinical deterioration (defined as a new or worsening organ dysfunction event), after conditioning on current clinical state.

**Secondary hypothesis:**

> The strength of the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration varies by hospital, with greater heterogeneity in hospitals with lower baseline testing intensity.

**Falsification criteria:**

- If the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration is null or reversed after conditioning on current clinical state, the primary hypothesis is not supported.
- If the association is present but does not vary by hospital (i.e., the hospital-level random effect is not significant), the secondary hypothesis is not supported.
- If the association is driven by a small number of hospitals or by a specific type of lab test, the result is not generalizable and the hypothesis is not supported in its current form.

## 4. Study Population and Temporal Boundaries

### Population

- **Source:** eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD).
- **Inclusion criteria:**
  - Adult patients (age ≥ 18) with at least one ICU stay.
  - At least 24 hours of continuous ICU care (to allow for a stable baseline testing rate).
  - At least one period of clinical instability (as defined below).
  - Sufficient laboratory data to compute a testing rate (≥ 5 distinct lab tests in the 24 hours prior to the index time).
- **Exclusion criteria:**
  - Patients who die or are discharged within 24 hours of ICU admission (to avoid immortal-time bias).
  - Patients with a documented code status of "do not resuscitate" (DNR) or "comfort measures only" (CMO) at the index time (to exclude end-of-life de-escalation, which is a major alternative explanation for reduced testing).
  - Patients with a known laboratory error or missing data in the index window.

### Temporal boundaries

- **Index time:** The first time point at which a patient meets the criteria for an abrupt reduction in testing rate (≥50% decrease in the number of distinct lab tests per 12-hour window, compared to the patient's own prior 24-hour rate) while remaining clinically unstable.
- **Baseline period:** The 24 hours prior to the index time, used to compute the patient's own baseline testing rate and clinical state.
- **Follow-up period:** The 48 hours after the index time, during which the outcome (clinical deterioration) is assessed.
- **Washout period:** No washout period is applied, as the index time is defined by an abrupt change in testing rate. However, patients who have a prior abrupt reduction in testing rate within the same ICU stay are excluded from the index time (to avoid repeated measurements).

## 5. Variables and Data Bindings

### 5.1 Laboratory testing rate (exposure)

- **Source table:** `eicu_crd_lab` (eICU Collaborative Research Database, laboratory results).
- **Key columns:**
  - `patient_id`: Patient identifier.
  - `encounter_id`: ICU encounter identifier.
  - `lab_time`: Timestamp of the laboratory test.
  - `lab_name`: Name of the laboratory test (e.g., "WBC", "Platelets", "Creatinine").
  - `lab_value`: Numeric value of the test (not used for the exposure, but available for sensitivity analysis).
- **Exposure definition:**
  - For each patient, compute the number of *distinct* laboratory tests ordered per 12-hour window, using `lab_name` and `lab_time`.
  - Define an *abrupt reduction* as a ≥50% decrease in the number of distinct lab tests per 12-hour window, compared to the patient's own prior 24-hour rate.
  - The index time is the first time point at which this abrupt reduction occurs while the patient remains clinically unstable.

### 5.2 Clinical instability (conditioning variable)

- **Source tables:**
  - `eicu_crd_vitals` (vital signs): `patient_id`, `encounter_id`, `vital_time`, `heart_rate`, `systolic_bp`, `diastolic_bp`, `respiratory_rate`, `spo2`, `temperature`.
  - `eicu_crd_diagnoses` (diagnoses): `patient_id`, `encounter_id`, `diagnosis_code`, `diagnosis_time`.
  - `eicu_crd_medications` (medications): `patient_id`, `encounter_id`, `medication_name`, `start_time`, `stop_time`.
- **Instability score:**
  - A composite instability score is defined as the sum of the following binary indicators, each assessed in the 12 hours prior to the index time:
    - Heart rate > 120 or < 50 beats/min.
    - Systolic BP < 90 or > 180 mmHg.
    - Respiratory rate > 25 or < 8 breaths/min.
    - SpO2 < 90% (on room air or supplemental oxygen).
    - Temperature > 38.5°C or < 35°C.
    - New or worsening organ dysfunction (e.g., new AKI, new ARDS, new sepsis) as indicated by diagnosis codes.
    - Use of vasopressors or mechanical ventilation.
  - A patient is considered *clinically unstable* if the instability score is ≥ 2 in the 12 hours prior to the index time.

### 5.3 Clinical deterioration (outcome)

- **Source tables:**
  - `eicu_crd_vitals` (vital signs): as above.
  - `eicu_crd_diagnoses` (diagnoses): as above.
  - `eicu_crd_medications` (medications): as above.
  - `eicu_crd_lab` (laboratory results): as above.
- **Outcome definition:**
  - A *clinical deterioration event* is defined as any of the following, occurring within 48 hours after the index time:
    - New or worsening organ dysfunction (e.g., new AKI stage 2 or 3, new ARDS, new sepsis) as indicated by diagnosis codes.
    - New use of vasopressors or mechanical ventilation.
    - A ≥20% increase in the instability score compared to the index time.
    - Death within 48 hours.
  - The primary outcome is the *time to first deterioration event* (censored at 48 hours).
  - The secondary outcome is the *binary indicator* of any deterioration event within 48 hours.

### 5.4 Hospital-level variation

- **Source table:** `eicu_crd_encounters` (encounters).
- **Key columns:**
  - `patient_id`: Patient identifier.
  - `encounter_id`: ICU encounter identifier.
  - `hospital_id`: Hospital identifier.
  - `icu_type`: Type of ICU (e.g., medical, surgical, mixed).
- **Hospital-level variation:**
  - The hospital-level random effect is estimated using a hierarchical model (see Section 6).
  - The baseline testing intensity for each hospital is computed as the median number of distinct lab tests per 12-hour window across all patients in that hospital.

## 6. Outcomes and Analysis

### 6.1 Primary analysis

- **Model:** Cox proportional hazards model for time to first deterioration event, with the following covariates:
  - Abrupt testing reduction (binary: yes/no).
  - Current clinical state (instability score, continuous).
  - Demographics: age, sex.
  - ICU type (medical, surgical, mixed).
  - Time of day (to account for circadian variation in testing).
  - Hospital-level random effect (to account for clustering).
- **Estimand:** The hazard ratio (HR) for the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration, adjusted for current clinical state and hospital-level variation.
- **Interpretation:** A HR > 1 indicates that abrupt testing reduction is associated with a higher risk of subsequent deterioration, after conditioning on current clinical state. A HR < 1 indicates the opposite. A HR of 1 indicates no association.

### 6.2 Secondary analyses

- **Hospital-level variation:** The hospital-level random effect is estimated using a hierarchical Cox model. The variance of the hospital-level random effect is used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity in the association across hospitals.
- **Sensitivity analysis 1:** Restrict to patients with a high instability score (≥ 3) to focus on the most unstable patients.
- **Sensitivity analysis 2:** Restrict to patients who do not have a DNR or CMO status at the index time (to exclude end-of-life de-escalation).
- **Sensitivity analysis 3:** Use a different definition of abrupt testing reduction (e.g., ≥30% or ≥70% decrease) to assess the robustness of the result.
- **Sensitivity analysis 4:** Use a different definition of clinical deterioration (e.g., only new organ dysfunction, excluding death) to assess the robustness of the result.

### 6.3 Method comparison: simple baseline vs. learned/mechanistic alternative

- **Simple baseline:** Cox proportional hazards model with fixed effects for demographics, ICU type, and time of day, and a hospital-level random effect. This model is interpretable and provides a clear estimate of the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration, adjusted for current clinical state.
- **Learned/mechanistic alternative:** A recurrent neural network (RNN) or transformer model that takes as input the sequence of laboratory tests, vital signs, and clinical events over the 24 hours prior to the index time, and predicts the 48-hour risk of clinical deterioration. The model is trained on all patients with sufficient data, and the prediction at the index time is compared to the actual outcome.
  - **What the alternative could reveal:** The RNN/transformer model could capture complex, non-linear patterns in the data that the Cox model cannot, such as the interaction between the timing of specific lab tests and the patient's clinical trajectory. It could also reveal whether the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration is driven by a specific type of lab test or a specific clinical context.
  - **Limitation:** The RNN/transformer model is less interpretable than the Cox model, and it is more prone to overfitting if the sample size is small. It also requires more computational resources and is more difficult to validate.
- **Selection reason:** The Cox model is the primary analysis because it is interpretable and provides a clear estimate of the association, adjusted for current clinical state. The RNN/transformer model is a secondary analysis that could reveal additional patterns, but it is not the primary analysis because it is less interpretable and more prone to overfitting.

## 7. Falsification Criteria

- **Primary hypothesis is not supported if:**
  - The hazard ratio for the association between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration is not significantly different from 1 (p > 0.05) after conditioning on current clinical state.
  - The association is reversed (HR < 1) after conditioning on current clinical state.
  - The association is driven by a small number of hospitals or by a specific type of lab test.

- **Secondary hypothesis is not supported if:**
  - The hospital-level random effect is not significantly different from 0 (p > 0.05).
  - The variance of the hospital-level random effect is not significantly different from 0 (p > 0.05).

- **General falsification:**
  - If the association is present in the unadjusted analysis but disappears after conditioning on current clinical state, this suggests that the association is driven by confounding by severity, and the primary hypothesis is not supported.
  - If the association is present in the adjusted analysis but is driven by a small number of hospitals or by a specific type of lab test, the result is not generalizable and the hypothesis is not supported in its current form.

## 8. Limitations and What Would Require Additional Data

### Limitations

1. **Confounding by indication:** The strongest alternative explanation is that reduced testing reflects a change in the patient's clinical state (e.g., the patient is improving, or the clinical team is de-escalating care at end of life). This proposal addresses this by conditioning on current clinical state and by excluding patients with a DNR or CMO status. However, residual confounding is possible, and the result should be interpreted as an association, not a causal effect.
2. **Selection bias:** Patients who are discharged or die within 24 hours of ICU admission are excluded, which may introduce selection bias. This is a limitation of the design, but it is necessary to avoid immortal-time bias.
3. **Measurement error:** The laboratory testing rate is computed from the `eicu_crd_lab` table, which may not capture all tests ordered (e.g., tests ordered but not completed). This could lead to underestimation of the testing rate.
4. **Generalizability:** The result may not be generalizable to other populations or settings, as it is based on a single dataset (eICU-CRD) and a specific definition of clinical instability and deterioration.

### What would require additional data

1. **Causal inference:** To establish a causal relationship between abrupt testing reduction and subsequent deterioration, a randomized controlled trial or a quasi-experimental design (e.g., instrumental variable analysis) would be required. This is beyond the scope of the current proposal.
2. **Mechanistic understanding:** To understand the mechanism by which abrupt testing reduction (if associated with deterioration) affects patient outcomes, additional data on the clinical decision-making process (e.g., clinician notes, order sets) would be required.
3. **External validation:** To validate the result in an external population, additional data from other ICU datasets (e.g., MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD) would be required.
