# Hospital Heterogeneity in Vasopressor Dose-Response: Does Timing and Titration Explain Why Treatment Findings Don't Transport Across ICUs?

## A Falsifiable, Data-Bound Hypothesis for eICU-CRD (evolved from expert seed eicu-01)

### 1. Hypothesis

**Leading claim (to be tested):** In ICU patients receiving vasopressors, the dose-response relationship between cumulative vasopressor exposure (time-weighted norepinephrine-equivalent dose over the first 24 hours) and ICU mortality is heterogeneous across hospitals. Hospitals with earlier vasopressor initiation (shorter time from ICU admission to first vasopressor) and steeper initial dose escalation (higher slope of NEE dose over the first 24 hours) have a flatter (less harmful) dose-mortality relationship.

**Strongest rival:** The dose-mortality relationship is homogeneous across hospitals; apparent heterogeneity is driven by case-mix differences (severity, comorbidities) not fully captured by APACHE scores. Under this rival, adjusting for APACHE score and demographic variables eliminates the hospital-level variation in the dose-response slope.

### 2. Scientific Opening

**Established:** Vasopressor dose is associated with mortality in septic shock. The norepinephrine-equivalent dose (NEE) is a standard metric for comparing vasopressor intensity across studies. [K1]

**Unresolved:** Whether the dose-mortality relationship is the same across all hospitals, or whether it varies systematically with hospital-level practices such as the timing of vasopressor initiation and the rate of dose escalation. The expert seed [eicu-01] posits that "differences between hospitals in the effects of the same vasopressor-escalation strategy are partly related to timing and titration practices."

**Why this matters:** If the dose-mortality relationship is heterogeneous across hospitals, then treatment findings from single-center studies may not transport. Understanding the source of heterogeneity (timing, titration, or case-mix) is essential for generalizing clinical trial results and for developing hospital-specific dosing guidelines.

### 4. Analysis Plan

**Cohort definition:**
- ICU patients with ≥1 vasopressor infusion record (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, vasopressin, or phenylephrine)
- APACHE score available
- Hospital with ≥20 vasopressor patients
- Expected analytic cohort: ~20,000 patients across ~96 hospitals

**Exposure metrics (per patient, first 24h from ICU admission):**
1. **Cumulative NEE exposure**: time-weighted average NEE dose (mcg/min × hours) over first 24h
2. **Timing of initiation**: infusionoffset of first vasopressor record (hours from ICU admission)
3. **Escalation rate**: slope of NEE dose over first 24h (mcg/min per hour), computed by linear regression of drugrate on infusionoffset
4. **Peak NEE dose**: maximum drugrate in first 24h

**Outcome:** ICU mortality (unitdischargestatus = "Expired" from patient table)

**Primary analysis — Hierarchical logistic regression:**
- Level 1 (patient): NEE dose (continuous), timing (hours), escalation rate (mcg/min/h), APACHE score, age, sex
- Level 2 (hospital): random intercept
- **Model 1 (homogeneous):** Fixed slope for NEE dose
- **Model 2 (heterogeneous):** Random slope for NEE dose
- **Model comparison:** Likelihood ratio test (LRT) or AIC to test whether the random slope is significant

**Moderation analysis:**
- Interaction: NEE dose × timing (does earlier initiation flatten the dose-response?)
- Interaction: NEE dose × escalation rate (does steeper escalation modify the dose-response?)
- Hospital-level: correlate hospital mean timing/escalation with hospital-specific dose-response slope (random effects from Model 2)

**Sensitivity analyses:**
1. Restrict to septic shock (ICD-9/10 codes for sepsis/shock from diagnosis table)
2. Restrict to first 6h exposure window
3. Use peak NEE dose instead of cumulative
4. Exclude hospitals with <20 vasopressor patients (already done in primary)
5. Adjust for lactate (if available in lab table)
6. Use predicted ICU mortality (from APACHE) as alternative outcome

**Alternative analysis:**
- XGBoost with whole-hospital holdout validation to check if the dose-response pattern generalizes across hospitals
- Compare AUROC and calibration of hospital-specific vs. global models

### 5. Falsification Criteria

- **Supportive:** Random slope model significantly outperforms fixed slope (LRT p < 0.05); hospital-level timing or escalation rate correlates with hospital-specific dose-response slope (|r| > 0.3, p < 0.05)
- **Adverse:** No significant heterogeneity (LRT p > 0.10); no correlation between timing/escalation and slope (|r| < 0.1, p > 0.10)
- **Inconclusive:** Marginal heterogeneity (p 0.05–0.10) or weak correlation (|r| 0.1–0.3, p 0.05–0.10)

### 6. Limitations

1. **InfusionDrug records are orders, not actual administration.** The drugrate field represents the ordered rate, which may differ from the actual delivered dose due to interruptions, boluses, or protocol changes.
2. **Drug rate completeness.** Some drugrate values are zero or missing; the median norepinephrine rate is 6.0 mcg/min, but the range extends to 2667 mcg/min (likely data entry errors or bolus doses recorded as continuous rates).
3. **Infusionoffset can be negative** (before ICU admission), reflecting vasopressors started in the ED or pre-ICU. This is a feature (captures pre-ICU timing) but also a limitation (pre-ICU course is incomplete).
4. **APACHE score is a single severity measure.** It does not capture all relevant confounders (e.g., comorbidities, pre-ICU interventions, source of admission).
5. **Observational design.** The association between timing/escalation and dose-response slope is not causal. Hospitals that initiate vasopressors earlier may also have other practice differences (e.g., earlier intubation, more aggressive fluid resuscitation).
6. **Single time period.** eICU-CRD covers 2014–2015; practice patterns may have changed since then.
7. **US-only.** Findings may not generalize to other healthcare systems.

### 8. What This Study Would Establish

- **If supportive:** The dose-mortality relationship is heterogeneous across hospitals, and this heterogeneity is partly explained by timing and titration practices. This would suggest that single-center findings on vasopressor dosing may not transport, and that hospital-specific dosing guidelines may be needed.
- **If adverse:** The dose-mortality relationship is homogeneous across hospitals after adjusting for severity. This would suggest that single-center findings are generalizable, and that apparent heterogeneity is driven by case-mix differences.
- **If inconclusive:** The evidence is insufficient to determine whether the dose-response relationship is heterogeneous. Further studies with richer data (e.g., actual administration rates, continuous hemodynamic monitoring) would be needed.

### 9. What This Study Would NOT Establish

- Causal effects of timing or titration on outcomes (observational design)
- Optimal dosing strategies (no randomization)
- Generalizability to other healthcare systems (US-only)
- Mechanisms underlying any observed heterogeneity
