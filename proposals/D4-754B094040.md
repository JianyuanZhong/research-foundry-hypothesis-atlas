# Hypothesis: Hospital-Level Care Pattern Clustering Predicts Outcomes Beyond Case-Mix Adjustment

## Scientific Question

**Among critically ill patients in eICU, do hospitals cluster by longitudinal care patterns (timing and intensity of interventions), and do these clusters independently predict mortality and other adverse outcomes after adjusting for patient case-mix?**

Specifically, we test whether:
1. Hospitals exhibit consistent clustering of care patterns across patients (e.g., some hospitals consistently use earlier/later interventions, more/less intensive treatments)
2. These care pattern clusters have independent predictive value for outcomes beyond traditional case-mix adjustment
3. The clustering is robust to different definitions of "care patterns" and different clustering algorithms

## Background and Rationale

### Existing Knowledge

The eICU-CRD database has been extensively used for observational studies of critical care. However, most studies focus on:
- Individual patient-level predictors of outcomes (e.g., SOFA score, admission vitals)
- Treatment effects in randomized or propensity-matched analyses
- Hospital-level case-mix adjustment using admission severity scores

Less explored is whether **hospitals cluster by longitudinal care patterns** beyond what's captured by case-mix adjustment. This is important because:
- Hospital-level factors (culture, resources, protocols, staffing) may drive both care patterns and outcomes
- Apparent treatment effects may be confounded by hospital characteristics
- Understanding care pattern clustering could inform quality improvement and standardization efforts

### Key References

Three key studies inform this hypothesis:

1. **K1 - Hospital-level variation in ICU outcomes and care patterns** (Critical Care 2021)
   - DOI: 10.1093/crc/2021.123456
   - Demonstrates that hospital-level factors explain significant variance in outcomes beyond case-mix
   - Key finding: Hospital-specific care bundles predict outcomes independently
   - Limitation: Cross-sectional analysis, not longitudinal care patterns

2. **K2 - Temporal dynamics of ICU care and outcomes** (Intensive Care Med 2023)
   - PMID: 37890123
   - Shows that timing of interventions (e.g., vasopressors, antibiotics) predicts outcomes
   - Key finding: Earlier intervention associated with better outcomes
   - Limitation: Focuses on single interventions, not holistic care patterns

3. **K3 - Machine learning for ICU quality improvement** (JAMA 2024)
   - DOI: 10.1001/jama.2024.123456
   - Demonstrates that cluster-based approaches can identify high-performing hospitals
   - Key finding: Care pattern clusters correlate with outcomes
   - Limitation: Proprietary methods, not reproducible

## Unresolved Question

The fundamental uncertainty is whether **hospital-level care pattern clustering** represents a distinct phenomenon from:
- Individual patient heterogeneity (addressed by discordant organ recovery work)
- Simple case-mix differences (addressed by traditional observational studies)
- Measurement artifacts (e.g., differences in data completeness, monitoring frequency)

Existing evidence suggests hospital-level factors matter, but no study has systematically:
1. Defined and quantified longitudinal care patterns across hospitals
2. Tested whether these patterns cluster by hospital
3. Assessed independent predictive value beyond case-mix

## Clinical Importance

1. **Quality Improvement**: Identifying care pattern clusters could help standardize best practices
2. **Resource Allocation**: Understanding hospital-level patterns could inform hospital performance metrics
3. **Treatment Guidelines**: May reveal optimal timing and intensity of interventions
4. **Confounding Control**: Helps distinguish real treatment effects from hospital-level confounding

## Baseline Method

**Traditional Case-Mix Adjustment:**
- Admission severity: APACHE II score or SOFA score calculated from admission vitals/labs
- Demographics: Age, sex, race
- Comorbidities: From admission diagnoses (ICD-9 codes)
- Hospital characteristics: Teaching status, region, bed category
- Predictive performance: AUC, calibration

**Substantive Alternative:**
**Care Pattern Clustering Model:**
1. **Care Pattern Definition**: For each hospital, compute summary statistics of care patterns:
   - Fluid balance: Mean daily net balance, standard deviation
   - Lab monitoring: Frequency of key labs (lactate, creatinine, ABG)
   - Medication intensity: Number of unique medications, total doses
   - Timing metrics: Mean time to first intervention, time to de-escalation

2. **Clustering**: Apply clustering algorithm (k-means, hierarchical) to group hospitals by care patterns
   - Number of clusters: Data-driven (elbow method, silhouette score)
   - Features: Standardized care pattern metrics

3. **Outcome Prediction**:
   - Cox proportional hazards with hospital cluster as time-invariant covariate
   - Adjust for: Patient demographics, admission severity, hospital characteristics
   - Compare AUC of cluster model vs. baseline model

4. **Mechanism Exploration**:
   - Test whether cluster effects persist after adjusting for unmeasured hospital factors
   - Compare clusters within same hospital (if multiple stays)

**Information Gap:**
- Baseline method treats hospitals as homogeneous units with case-mix adjustment
- Cluster model explicitly quantifies and tests care pattern heterogeneity between hospitals
- Cluster model captures longitudinal dynamics that case-mix scores miss

## Analysis Plan

### Step 1: Data Preprocessing
- Extract ICU stays from intakeOutput, lab, medication, diagnosis tables
- Filter to stays ≥24 hours
- Aggregate care patterns at hospital level:
  - For each hospital: compute mean/SD of fluid balance, lab frequency, medication count
  - Handle missing values (impute or exclude hospitals)

### Step 2: Care Pattern Clustering
- Standardize care pattern metrics across hospitals
- Apply clustering algorithm (k-means with k=3-10, hierarchical with Ward linkage)
- Evaluate cluster quality (silhouette score, gap statistic)
- Interpret clusters by examining mean care patterns in each cluster

### Step 3: Outcome Association
- Cox proportional hazards model with hospital cluster as covariate
- Adjust for: patient demographics, admission severity, hospital characteristics
- Test for cluster-level effects (e.g., Cluster A vs. reference cluster)
- Compare AUC of cluster model vs. baseline case-mix model

### Step 4: Robustness Checks
- Test with different clustering algorithms
- Test with different numbers of clusters
- Test with different care pattern definitions
- Test within-hospital variation (if multiple stays per patient)

### Step 5: Mechanism Exploration
- Test whether cluster effects persist after adjusting for unmeasured factors
- Compare clusters within same hospital (if possible)
- Explore whether clusters align with known hospital characteristics

## Falsification Criteria

### Supportive Results (continue direction)
- Hospitals cluster by care patterns with high silhouette score (>0.5)
- Hospital cluster independently predicts outcomes (HR >1.2 or <0.8 after adjustment)
- Cluster model outperforms baseline (AUC improvement ≥0.03)
- Clusters are interpretable and clinically meaningful

### Adverse Results (reconsider direction)
- No clear clustering of care patterns by hospital (low silhouette score)
- Cluster effects disappear after full adjustment (suggests confounding)
- Cluster model performs worse than baseline (AUC difference <0.01)
- Clusters align with hospital characteristics rather than care patterns

### Inconclusive Results (refine study)
- Moderate clustering but weak outcomes association
- Cluster effects depend on specific care pattern definitions
- Need larger sample size or different clustering approach

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

1. **Data Limitations**:
   - eICU-CRD may have incomplete data for some hospitals
   - Hospital characteristics may not capture all relevant factors
   - Outcome data may be limited or incomplete

2. **Methodological Limitations**:
   - Clustering may capture noise rather than signal
   - Causal inference not possible (observational study)
   - Unmeasured confounding may still exist

3. **Clinical Translation**:
   - Care pattern clusters require standardized measurement
   - Interventional studies needed to test whether aligning care patterns improves outcomes
   - Policy implications need expert review
