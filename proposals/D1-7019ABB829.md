# Scientific Proposal: Hyperoxia Exposure and Outcomes Differ by Pulmonary Status in Critically Ill Patients

## 1. Refined Hypothesis Statement

### Primary Hypothesis
In critically ill patients with adequate oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≥ 300 mmHg), sustained hyperoxia exposure (PaO2 > 100 mmHg for > 4 hours) is associated with increased risk of in-hospital mortality, but this association is **modified by pulmonary disease status**:
- Patients with **primary pulmonary disease** (COPD, pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary edema) show a **stronger positive association** between hyperoxia duration and mortality
- Patients with **non-pulmonary primary disease** (cardiac, sepsis, neurological) show a **weaker or null association** between hyperoxia duration and mortality

### Secondary Hypothesis
The relationship between hyperoxia and outcomes is mediated by the duration of respiratory support (mechanical ventilation vs. oxygen therapy only), with greater harm observed in patients requiring mechanical ventilation.

## 2. Study Population

**Population**: Adult patients in the eICU database with arterial blood gas (ABG) monitoring and respiratory support records.

**Inclusion criteria**:
- Age ≥ 18 years
- ICU stay duration ≥ 24 hours
- At least 2 arterial blood gas measurements during ICU stay
- Documented respiratory support (mechanical ventilation or supplemental oxygen)
- PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≥ 300 mmHg at any time point (to ensure adequate oxygenation)

**Exclusion criteria**:
- Pre-existing chronic lung disease (COPD, interstitial lung disease) documented in admission diagnosis
- Severe hypoxemia at admission (PaO2/FiO2 < 300 mmHg)
- Missing respiratory care data
- Pediatric patients (< 18 years)

## 4. Primary Outcome

**In-Hospital Mortality**: Defined by `apachePatientResult.actualicumortality = 1` within 30 days of ICU admission.

## 5. Primary Exposure

**Hyperoxia Exposure**: Defined as time spent with:
- PaO2 > 100 mmHg (sustained hyperoxemia threshold)
- Duration ≥ 4 hours (to distinguish transient from sustained exposure)
- During periods of adequate oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2 ≥ 300 mmHg)

**Measurement**: Calculate total hyperoxia duration (in hours) during the first 72 hours of ICU admission using respiratoryCharting PaO2 measurements, interpolated with vitalAperiodic SpO2 data where ABG is unavailable.

## 6. Key Effect Modifier

**Pulmonary Disease Status**: Categorized as:
- **Primary Pulmonary Disease**: COPD, pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary edema, pneumothorax (based on diagnosis table)
- **Non-Pulmonary Primary Disease**: Cardiac, sepsis, neurological, gastrointestinal, other

## 7. Baseline and Alternative Methods

### Baseline Method
**Logistic regression** with:
- Primary exposure: Hyperoxia duration (continuous)
- Covariates: Age, gender, Apache II score, primary diagnosis category, hospital fixed effects
- What this tests: Overall association between hyperoxia duration and mortality

### Substantive Alternative Method
**Interaction-based Cox proportional hazards model** with:
- Primary exposure: Hyperoxia duration (time-dependent)
- Effect modifier: Pulmonary disease status (interaction term)
- Covariates: Age, gender, Apache II score, hospital fixed effects
- What this reveals that baseline loses:
  1. **Differential Risk by Disease Type**: Whether hyperoxia is harmful primarily in patients with pulmonary disease
  2. **Threshold Effects**: Whether there's a hyperoxia duration threshold above which risk increases
  3. **Clinical Heterogeneity**: Whether treatment thresholds for oxygenation should differ by pulmonary status

### Information Gain
The alternative method can distinguish between:
- A uniform effect of hyperoxia across all patient populations
- A differential effect where pulmonary disease status modifies the risk
- Whether hyperoxia is a marker of disease severity rather than a direct cause

## 8. Analysis Plan

### 1. Data Preprocessing
- Handle missing ABG values using linear interpolation from vitalAperiodic SpO2
- Flag and exclude measurements with PaO2 > 500 mmHg (likely artifacts)
- Calculate hyperoxia duration using time-series analysis of respiratoryCharting PaO2
- Categorize pulmonary disease based on diagnosis table

### 2. Primary Analysis
- Cox proportional hazards model with time-dependent hyperoxia exposure
- Test interaction between hyperoxia duration and pulmonary disease status
- Primary effect measure: Hazard ratio (HR) per additional hour of hyperoxia
- Stratified analysis by pulmonary disease category

### 3. Secondary Analysis
- **Ventilator Stratification**: Compare hyperoxia effects in mechanically ventilated vs. non-ventilated patients
- **Dose-Response**: Test for non-linear relationship (quadratic terms)
- **Hospital Variation**: Mixed-effects model to test for hospital-level variation in hyperoxia management
- **Subgroup Analysis**: By primary diagnosis (sepsis, cardiac, neurological, pulmonary)

### 4. Falsification Criteria
- **Null**: No association between hyperoxia duration and mortality (HR = 1.0, 95% CI includes 1.0)
- **Adverse**: Hyperoxia duration associated with decreased survival (HR > 1.0, 95% CI excludes 1.0)
- **Supportive**: Stronger adverse association in patients with primary pulmonary disease compared to non-pulmonary disease (significant interaction p < 0.05)

## 9. Clinical Significance

**If supported**:
1. **Stratified Oxygen Therapy**: Clinicians could use pulmonary disease status to guide oxygenation targets
2. **Treatment Guidelines**: Evidence could support lower oxygen targets for non-pulmonary disease patients
3. **Quality Improvement**: Hospitals could audit hyperoxia exposure rates by patient population
4. **Trial Design**: Inform stratification in future oxygenation trials (e.g., EPOC trial)

**If rejected**:
1. **Uniform Risk**: Suggests hyperoxia may be harmful across all patient populations
2. **Need for Alternative Measures**: May require investigation of other hyperoxia biomarkers
3. **Measurement Issues**: May indicate problems with ABG measurement or hyperoxia definition

## 10. Data Availability and Limitations

**Available Data**: All required tables are available in the eICU dataset:
- `respiratoryCharting`: Arterial blood gas measurements (PaO2, FiO2)
- `respiratoryCare`: Respiratory support type and settings
- `diagnosis`: Primary and secondary diagnoses
- `apachePatientResult`: Mortality outcomes
- `vitalAperiodic`: SpO2 monitoring data
- `apacheApsVar`: Severity scores
- `patient`, `hospital`: Demographics and hospital characteristics

**Limitations**:
1. **Measurement Error**: ABG measurements are intermittent; hyperoxia duration may be underestimated
2. **Artifact Handling**: SpO2 readings may have artifacts that need careful handling
3. **Resuscitation Timing**: Early hyperoxia may reflect resuscitation efforts rather than therapeutic decisions
4. **Confounding by Indication**: Patients with different underlying pathologies may have different oxygenation patterns
5. **Causality**: Observational design cannot establish causation

**Missing Evidence**:
- **Ground Truth for Hyperoxia**: Need to verify that ABG measurements are accurate
- **Treatment Thresholds**: No clear evidence for optimal PaO2 targets in different patient populations
- **Mechanistic Insights**: Observational design cannot establish biological mechanisms

## 13. Novelty and Advance Over Existing Knowledge

**What is already known**:
- The EPOC trial showed that higher oxygen targets are associated with higher mortality in patients with respiratory disease
- Hyperoxia is known to cause oxidative stress and organ damage through multiple mechanisms
- Most critical care research focuses on avoiding hypoxemia rather than preventing hyperoxia

**What this study adds**:
1. **Temporal Dynamics**: Quantifies the duration of hyperoxia exposure rather than just peak PaO2
2. **Pulmonary Disease Stratification**: Tests whether the hyperoxia-mortality relationship differs by primary disease category
3. **Clinical Actionability**: Provides evidence for stratified oxygen therapy guidelines
4. **Observational Validation**: Validates/explores the EPOC findings in a large observational cohort

**Unresolved Question**:
The exact mechanism by which hyperoxia causes harm in patients with pulmonary disease versus non-pulmonary disease remains unclear. This study tests whether pulmonary disease status modifies the hyperoxia-mortality relationship, which could inform targeted oxygen therapy strategies.

## 14. Conclusion

This proposal tests whether sustained hyperoxia exposure in critically ill patients is associated with increased mortality, and whether this association is modified by pulmonary disease status. The findings could inform clinical guidelines for oxygen therapy, suggesting that oxygen targets should be stratified by primary disease category. The study uses well-established eICU tables and follows a rigorous analytical approach with clear falsification criteria.
