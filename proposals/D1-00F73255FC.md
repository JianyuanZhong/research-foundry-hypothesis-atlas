# Ventilatory burden and liberation from mechanical ventilation

## Scientific opening and unresolved claim

The expert seed proposes that ventilatory burden, when adjusted for body size and respiratory mechanics, contains more information about liberation failure than tidal volume alone. The unresolved claim is narrower and testable: among mechanically ventilated ICU patients who remain on ventilation after a fixed post-admission period, does a pre-specified ventilatory-burden measure predict the time to liberation from mechanical ventilation beyond a simple tidal-volume model, after accounting for death and transfer/discharge as competing events?

This matters because clinicians often use tidal volume as a proxy for ventilatory burden. If a burden measure adds information, it could justify a prospective, multicenter study of ventilation-weaning risk and could inform whether an automated weaning alert should incorporate pressure-related parameters. Conversely, if it does not, the apparent value may reflect monitoring intensity or missingness rather than physiology.

The strongest claim supported by the available evidence is only that the eICU snapshot contains longitudinal respiratory-care and vital-sign records with timestamps and patient-level identifiers. It does not support a causal claim about ventilation settings, a treatment recommendation, or a clinical benefit of changing tidal volume. The hypothesis remains a prediction/association question, not a treatment-effect study.

## Exact population, time origin, and temporal boundaries

Use eICU patient-unit-stay records only. The data are ICU-stay records, not calendar-cohort records; calendar dates have been removed. Use patientunitstayid as the primary key and join patient on that key. Do not treat rows with the same patientunitstayid as independent observations.

Include a stay only if all of the following are true:

1. unitstaytype is ventilator or ventilator_vent (or the equivalent explicitly ventilated category present in the snapshot).
2. The stay has a valid unitadmittime24 (minutes from ICU admission) and a valid unitdischargeoffset.
3. At least 24 hours of observed respiratory-care status records exist after admission.
4. The stay has at least 48 hours of vitalPeriodic observations with a valid observationoffset after admission.
5. respiratoryCare contains a valid ventstartoffset and ventendoffset, with ventendoffset > ventstartoffset.
6. The final respiratory-care status before discharge or transfer is recorded.

The time origin is ICU admission (unitadmittime24), not the first ventilator start. The index is the end of the first observed ventilation interval (ventendoffset) and is bounded to 24–72 hours after admission. This avoids using post-index outcomes as predictors. The outcome window is 0–48 hours after that index. A patient who dies, is transferred, or leaves the ICU before 48 hours has a competing event at that time. A patient discharged alive from the ICU with no recorded ventilator interval in the window is censored at 48 hours.

For the primary analysis, use the first eligible index per stay and exclude stays with multiple ventilation intervals in the index window. Report sensitivity analyses using all eligible intervals and excluding stays with any prior ventilation interval.

For each respiratory-care row, classify the current ventilation state by airwaytype and airwayposition, requiring one of the explicit ventilator categories in the source. Treat missing or contradictory categories as unknown and exclude them from the primary state definition; report unknown-state sensitivity. Define the index as the first ventendoffset after admission with a valid start, and require a subsequent status record before the index. Do not infer a ventilation interval from an absent respiratoryCare row.

For each 15-minute interval before and at the index, construct:

- tidal volume (tidal_volume), from lowexhtvlimit and lowpeakpreslimit only when the source explicitly labels these fields as tidal-volume/peak-pressure parameters. If the labels are not explicitly tidal-volume or peak-pressure, use a prespecified fallback and report it; do not label them as tidal volume.
- plateau pressure (plateau_pressure), from meanairwaypreslimit only when the source explicitly labels it as plateau pressure. If the label is not explicit, do not claim plateau pressure.
- respiratory rate (respiratory_rate), from hirespratelimit when explicitly labeled respiratory rate.
- oxygenation, from the latest valid sao2 in the 15-minute interval.
- a body-size term using admissionheight and dischargeweight, with the primary model using admission height and weight and a sensitivity using weight alone.
- an artifact/monitoring-density term using the number of valid respiratory-care and vital observations per 24 hours, the number of rows with missingness in each measured field, and the time since the last observation.

The primary burden measure is a standardized, interval-level weighted sum of tidal volume, plateau pressure, and respiratory rate, divided by admission height. It is a descriptive exposure measure, not a treatment assignment. The simple baseline uses only tidal volume, admission height, admission weight, and the same monitoring-density terms. The substantive alternative uses the full burden measure plus plateau pressure and respiratory rate, with the same covariates.

## Outcomes, estimand, and analysis

The primary outcome is time from the index to the first of: (1) liberation from mechanical ventilation (no recorded ventilator interval after the index and no subsequent ventilator status), (2) death, or (3) transfer/discharge from the ICU. Use the time to the first event. A patient with no event by 48 hours is censored at 48 hours. This is a competing-risk analysis, not a simple survival analysis that treats death as a non-event.

Report the restricted mean time to liberation from the index to 48 hours for the primary burden model versus the tidal-volume baseline. Report cumulative incidence functions and cause-specific hazards for liberation, death, and transfer/discharge. The estimand is the difference in restricted mean time to liberation between the two models at 48 hours, with a 95% confidence interval and a two-sided test of whether the interval excludes zero. The primary comparison is model discrimination and calibration of the 48-hour liberation event, not AUROC alone; report Brier score, calibration intercept/slope, and calibration-in-the-large.

Use hospital as a fixed effect in the primary analysis because hospital characteristics are available. Do not use hospital as an instrumental variable or make a causal claim about treatment. Report the number of stays, eligible intervals, events, censoring, and missingness by model and hospital. Use a prespecified bootstrap or cluster-robust standard error with clustering at patientunitstayid and at the hospital level.

The baseline is deliberately simple: a logistic/competing-risk model with tidal volume, height, weight, monitoring density, and hospital effects. The substantive alternative is a transparent mechanistic/physiologic model that adds plateau pressure and respiratory rate to the burden sum. It can reveal whether pressure and rate add information that a tidal-volume proxy loses; this is a scientific information comparison, not a claim that the model is clinically causal. A neural sequence model is deferred because the available longitudinal rows are sparse and the primary question is a bounded physiologic comparison. If a future solver has enough complete patient-level trajectories and computational resources, a sequence model could be revisited with a held-out hospital split and explicit calibration checks; its value must be judged by whether it changes the burden-versus-tidal-volume comparison, not by a small predictive gain.

## Falsification and interpretation

Supportive evidence requires a clinically meaningful, precisely estimated improvement in the burden model over the tidal-volume baseline, with better discrimination/calibration and a positive difference in restricted mean time to liberation, while the improvement persists after hospital effects and monitoring-density adjustment. It would support the hypothesis that burden adds information; it would not establish that increasing tidal volume improves outcomes or that the burden measure is a causal treatment target.

Adverse evidence is a null or negative difference, or a burden model that performs worse or is less well calibrated than the tidal-volume baseline. This would argue against adding burden parameters to this risk measure and against interpreting the seed as a basis for a new alert.

Inconclusive evidence includes a wide confidence interval, unstable event counts, excessive missingness or unknown ventilation states, or a result that disappears after hospital and monitoring-density adjustment. It would justify narrowing the question or collecting more complete data rather than changing the interpretation.

Falsification checks are:

1. Reverse the index and outcome definitions: the association should not be created by post-index data.
2. Exclude patients with sparse or highly irregular recording density; the burden effect should not be driven by monitoring intensity.
3. Exclude unknown ventilation states and report the proportion excluded.
4. Repeat with the alternative respiratory-care state definition and with the 48-hour versus 72-hour windows.
5. Repeat with death as the only competing event and with transfer/discharge as a separate event.
6. Test whether the result is driven by a few hospitals by comparing the primary pooled estimate with hospital-level estimates and by excluding the top 20% of hospitals by event count.
7. Test the alternative with tidal volume removed; if the burden effect disappears, the result is not robustly attributable to the added physiology.
8. Verify that the source rows are read-only and that no derived file is written to the configured source paths.

A supportive result would justify a prospective multicenter study with explicit ventilator measurements, sedation, organ-support, and treatment data. It would not justify changing bedside ventilation settings from this retrospective analysis. A causal effect of tidal volume, a mechanism, or a recommendation for a specific target requires expert review, more complete clinical data, and ideally a randomized or adequately identified interventional study.

## Alternatives and evidence needed to revisit them

- A neural sequence model: revisit if enough complete patient-level trajectories exist and the solver has GPU/CPU capacity; evaluate with whole-hospital holdout and explicit calibration.
- A mechanistic ventilator model using plateau pressure and airway resistance: revisit if the source explicitly labels the required fields and the solver can validate the physiology; otherwise narrow the model.
- A treatment-effect study: requires a randomized or adequately identified intervention, treatment adherence, and outcomes not represented in this snapshot.
- The insulin seed is retained as a separate direction, but it is not used here because its required nutrition and medication fields require a separate feasibility audit and would change the scientific question.
- The hyperoxia seed is retained as a separate direction, but the local vitalPeriodic/respiratoryCare fields are not sufficient to establish arterial oxygen saturation or pulmonary status without explicit validation.
