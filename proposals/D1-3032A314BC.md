# Scientific Proposal: Monitoring Gaps Compromise Prognostic Transportability Across Hospitals

## 1. Unresolved Question and Clinical Importance

### The Core Question

While prior work has established that **temporal patterns of vital sign monitoring** (e.g., hypotension duration, ventilation-free intervals) predict important clinical outcomes, a critical unresolved question remains: **Does the temporal pattern of data acquisition (gaps vs. continuous monitoring) systematically compromise the transportability of prognostic models across hospitals?**

This is not merely an incremental addition of another covariate. The question addresses a fundamental limitation in critical care research: **Can a model trained on data from hospitals with dense monitoring reliably predict outcomes in hospitals with sparse monitoring?**

### Why This Matters Clinically

1. **Model Portability Crisis**: Critical care models are increasingly deployed across multiple hospitals. If monitoring frequency confounds prognostic signals, models may perform well in training hospitals but fail in transfer hospitals.

2. **Quality Metric vs. Clinical Signal**: Hospitals with more intensive monitoring may appear to have better outcomes not because of better care, but because more data allows earlier detection of deterioration. This creates a **survivorship bias** in model evaluation.

3. **Resource Allocation**: Understanding this dependency could guide:
   - Minimum monitoring requirements for model deployment
   - Calibration strategies for cross-hospital use
   - Development of "monitoring-adjusted" prognostic scores

### Leading Rival Hypothesis

**Rival**: Prognostic signals are robust to monitoring frequency because the underlying pathophysiology (e.g., hypotension, organ dysfunction) drives outcomes independently of when measurements are taken.

### Observable Difference

- **Our hypothesis predicts**: Models trained on dense monitoring will show significantly worse calibration and discrimination when applied to hospitals with sparse monitoring, even after adjusting for case mix.
- **Rival predicts**: Model performance will remain stable across hospitals with different monitoring densities after adjusting for severity scores.

## 2. Study Population

**Population**: Adult patients in the eICU database with longitudinal vital sign monitoring and outcome data.

**Inclusion criteria**:
- Age ≥ 18 years
- ICU stay duration ≥ 48 hours
- At least 12 hours of continuous vital sign monitoring (vitalPeriodic with noninvasivemean ≥ 0)
- At least one outcome event (mortality, AKI, or composite) within 7 days of admission

**Exclusion criteria**:
- Missing vital sign data for >50% of monitored time points
- Patients with <100 vital sign measurements
- Patients transferred from another hospital (to avoid pre-admission confounding)

## 4. Primary Hypothesis

**Hypothesis**: After adjusting for baseline severity (Apache II, SAPS III), patient characteristics (age, gender), and hospital characteristics, the association between monitoring density and clinical outcomes is **positive** (higher monitoring density → better outcome prediction), and the **difference in model performance** between hospitals with high vs. low monitoring density is **statistically significant** (p < 0.05).

**Mathematical Formulation**:
Let:
- \(Y\) = Clinical outcome (mortality or AKI)
- \(M\) = Monitoring density (measurements per hour)
- \(S\) = Severity score (log-transformed Apache II)
- \(H\) = Hospital fixed effect

**Primary Model**: \(logit(P(Y=1)) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 M + \beta_2 S + \sum_h \gamma_h H + \epsilon\)

**Expected**: \(\beta_1 > 0\) (higher monitoring density predicts better outcomes)

**Secondary Model**: Compare model discrimination (AUROC) and calibration across hospitals stratified by monitoring density quartiles.

**Expected**: Significant difference in AUROC between high and low monitoring density hospitals (p < 0.05).

## 5. Secondary Hypotheses

### Secondary Hypothesis 1: Effect Modification by Clinical State

The monitoring-density-outcome association is **modified by clinical state** (shock vs. non-shock):
- In shock patients (vasopressor-dependent), monitoring gaps are more consequential
- In non-shock patients, monitoring density has weaker association with outcomes

### Secondary Hypothesis 2: Hospital-Level Variation

The monitoring-density-outcome association **varies across hospitals**:
- Teaching hospitals with high monitoring density show stronger associations
- Community hospitals with variable monitoring show weaker associations

### Secondary Hypothesis 3: Temporal Dynamics

The association between monitoring gaps and outcomes is **time-dependent**:
- Early ICU gaps (first 24 hours) have stronger association with outcomes than late gaps
- This suggests monitoring gaps during critical decision windows are more consequential

## 6. Analysis Plan

### Primary Analysis: Cox Proportional Hazards

**Model 1**: Time-to-event with monitoring density as time-varying covariate
```
hazard(t) = h0(t) * exp(β1 * monitoring_density(t) + β2 * severity + γ_hospital)
```

**Model 2**: Competing risks regression (mortality vs. AKI)

**Model 3**: Multi-level logistic regression (hospital as random effect)

### Baseline Methods

1. **Logistic regression** with monitoring density as continuous and categorical predictors
2. **Random forest** with monitoring density features
3. **Gradient boosting** (XGBoost) with monitoring density features

### Machine Learning Approach

**Task**: Train models on hospitals with high monitoring density, evaluate on hospitals with low monitoring density.

**Features**:
- Monitoring density (continuous, quartiles)
- Gap duration (max, mean, median)
- Gap frequency

**Model Comparison**:

**Evaluation Metrics**:

### Transportability Analysis

**Cross-hospital validation**:
- Train on high-monitoring hospitals, test on low-monitoring hospitals
- Train on low-monitoring hospitals, test on high-monitoring hospitals
- Compare performance to same-hospital validation

**Calibration plots**:
- Predicted vs. observed outcomes by monitoring density quartile
- Calibration slope and intercept

## 7. Falsification Criteria

**Hypothesis will be falsified if**:
1. After adjusting for severity and hospital characteristics, monitoring density shows **no significant association** with outcomes (β1 ≈ 0, p > 0.10)
2. Model performance (AUROC) is **statistically indistinguishable** across hospitals with different monitoring densities
3. Cross-hospital validation shows **no performance decline** when moving from high to low monitoring density hospitals
4. The association is **confounded** by unmeasured hospital practices (e.g., case mix, treatment intensity)

**Hypothesis will be supported if**:
1. Monitoring density shows **significant positive association** with outcomes after adjustment (β1 > 0, p < 0.05)
2. Models trained on high-monitoring hospitals show **significant performance decline** when applied to low-monitoring hospitals
3. Calibration is **worse** in low-monitoring hospitals
4. The association persists after controlling for severity, hospital characteristics, and treatment intensity

## 8. Clinical Significance

### Potential Implications

1. **Model Selection Guidelines**: Clinicians should be cautious about using models trained in one hospital setting when applying to another with different monitoring practices.

2. **Minimum Monitoring Requirements**: Hospitals deploying critical care models may need to meet minimum monitoring standards to ensure model validity.

3. **Model Calibration**: Models may need hospital-specific calibration to account for monitoring density differences.

4. **Quality Improvement**: Understanding this relationship could motivate more standardized monitoring practices across hospitals.

5. **Research Design**: Future prognostic research should report monitoring density characteristics to facilitate model transportability.

### Limitations

1. **Causality**: Observational design cannot establish that monitoring gaps cause worse outcomes; confounding by indication is possible.

2. **Measurement Error**: Vital sign data are 5-minute medians, not continuous waveforms.

3. **Unmeasured Confounders**: Hospital culture, staffing, and other unmeasured factors may confound the relationship.

4. **Generalizability**: Results may not apply to ICU settings outside the eICU database.
