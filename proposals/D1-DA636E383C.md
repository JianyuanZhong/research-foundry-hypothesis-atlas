# Candidate Proposal: Ventilatory Burden and Cardiovascular Instability in Critical Illness

## Scientific Question

**Primary Hypothesis**: In critically ill patients requiring mechanical ventilation, the duration of mechanical ventilation (ventilatory burden) and the timing of weaning from ventilation are independent predictors of subsequent cardiovascular instability (defined as hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation or systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg), even after adjusting for baseline organ dysfunction and hospital-level factors [K1].

**Secondary Hypothesis**: Patients who are weaned from mechanical ventilation earlier (shorter time to ventilation weaning) have better cardiovascular stability in the first 48 hours post-weaning compared to those with prolonged ventilation, even when adjusted for baseline severity [K2][K3].

## Unresolved Question

While existing literature describes [K1]:
- Ventilatory burden as a measure of respiratory support duration
- The relationship between APACHE II scores and mortality
- Single-organ recovery trajectories (e.g., time to renal recovery)
- Hemodynamic instability as an adverse outcome

There is limited evidence on [K2][K3]:
1. **The independent contribution of ventilatory burden** to cardiovascular instability beyond baseline severity
2. **How the timing of ventilation weaning** affects subsequent hemodynamic stability
3. **Whether respiratory recovery timing** predicts cardiovascular complications through shared pathophysiological mechanisms (e.g., inflammatory cascades, hemodynamic reserve depletion)
4. **Hospital-level variations** in weaning practices and their impact on cardiovascular outcomes

## Clinical Importance

1. **Ventilator Weaning Guidelines**: Understanding how ventilatory burden affects cardiovascular stability could inform safer weaning protocols, particularly for patients at risk of cardiovascular collapse during weaning [K1].

2. **Risk Stratification**: Identifying patients at high risk of cardiovascular instability after ventilation weaning could enable proactive hemodynamic monitoring and early intervention.

3. **Multidisciplinary Care**: Recognizing the interplay between respiratory and cardiovascular recovery could improve coordination between pulmonology and critical care medicine teams.

4. **Quality Improvement**: Hospital-level analysis could identify practices that optimize the respiratory-cardiovascular recovery trajectory.

## Main Limitations

- **Causality**: Observational design cannot establish that ventilatory burden *causes* cardiovascular instability; confounding by baseline severity and disease process must be addressed through statistical methods.
- **Measurement**: Ventilatory burden is measured as duration of mechanical ventilation, not as a comprehensive measure of respiratory effort or burden (e.g., does not capture spontaneous breathing efforts, PEEP levels, etc.).
- **Outcome Definition**: Cardiovascular instability is defined by hemodynamic parameters and vasopressor use, which may not capture all forms of cardiovascular compromise.
- **Generalizability**: eICU database represents US ICUs; findings may not generalize to ICUs with different practices or patient populations.

### Derived Variables

1. **Ventilatory Burden**:
   - Continuous: `ventendoffset - ventstartoffset` (minutes)
   - Categorical: tertiles of ventilation duration
   - Binary: Ventilated ≥72 hours vs <72 hours

2. **Time to Ventilation Weaning**:
   - Continuous: `ventendoffset` (minutes from admission)
   - Categorical: Weaned within 48 hours vs >48 hours
   - Binary: Ventilated ≥72 hours vs <72 hours

3. **Cardiovascular Instability (Outcome)**:
   - Defined as: (a) Systolic BP <90 mmHg requiring vasopressor, OR (b) Vasopressor escalation
   - Time to first episode (in minutes from admission)
   - Duration of instability episodes

4. **Baseline Severity**:
   - APACHE II score (from apachePredVar or apachePatientResult)
   - Age, sex

## Population Definition

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Patients with at least one record in `respiratoryCare` table (mechanical ventilation)
- Patients with at least one record in `vitalAperiodic` table (vital signs)
- Patients with at least one record in `infusionDrug` table (to assess vasopressor use)
- Patients with at least one record in `apachePredVar` or `apachePatientResult` table (baseline severity)
- Patients admitted to ICUs with complete hospital information

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Patients with missing hospital information
- Patients with incomplete vital signs data (less than 50% of expected observations)
- Patients with ventilation duration <24 hours

**Temporal Boundaries:**
- Study window: 0 to 72 hours post-admission
- Primary outcome window: 0 to 72 hours post-admission
- Secondary outcome window: 0 to 168 hours post-admission

## Analysis Plan

### Primary Analysis: Cox Proportional Hazards Model

**Outcome**: Time to first episode of cardiovascular instability (or 72 hours if no event)

**Covariates:**
- Ventilatory burden (continuous: hours of ventilation)
- Ventilatory burden (categorical: tertiles)
- Time to ventilation weaning (continuous: hours)
- Time to ventilation weaning (categorical: weaned within 48h vs >48h)
- APACHE II score (continuous)
- Age
- Sex
- Teaching status of hospital (binary)
- Region (categorical)

**Model**:
```
hazard(t) = h0(t) * exp(β1 * ventilatory_burden + β2 * weaning_time + β3 * apacheii + β4 * age + β5 * sex + β6 * teaching + β7 * region)
```

### Secondary Analysis: Hospital-Level Fixed Effects

**Model**: Multilevel Cox regression with hospital as random effect

**Purpose**: Assess whether hospital-level factors explain variation in the ventilatory-cardiovascular relationship

### Sensitivity Analyses

1. Adjustment for comorbidities (pastHistory table)
2. Alternative outcome definitions:
   - Time to first systolic BP <90 mmHg (regardless of vasopressor)
   - Time to vasopressor initiation (regardless of BP threshold)
3. Subgroup analyses:
   - By baseline APACHE II score (low vs high severity)
   - By type of ventilation (invasive vs non-invasive)
   - By hospital teaching status

## Method Comparison

### Baseline Approach (Traditional Risk Scores)

**Method**: Logistic regression predicting cardiovascular instability using APACHE II, age, sex.

**Limitations**: Does not capture temporal dynamics of ventilatory burden or weaning timing [K1].

### Alternative Approach (Machine Learning)

**Method**: Random Forest or Gradient Boosting with time-varying features.

**Advantages**: Can capture non-linear relationships and interactions [K3].

**Limitations**: Less interpretable, may overfit.

## Falsification Criteria

**Hypothesis will be falsified if:**
1. Ventilatory burden shows no association with cardiovascular instability after adjustment (p > 0.05) [K1]
2. Time to ventilation weaning shows no association with cardiovascular stability (p > 0.05)
3. No significant hospital-level variation
4. No significant interaction with baseline severity

**Hypothesis will be supported if:**
1. β1 > 0 (p < 0.05) for ventilatory burden
2. β2 < 0 (p < 0.05) for weaning time
3. Significant hospital-level effect (p < 0.05)
4. Consistent findings across sensitivity analyses

## Methodological Alternatives Considered

Before selecting this approach, the following methodological alternatives were considered:

1. **Simple correlation analysis**: Would test basic relationships but would not adjust for confounding or capture temporal dynamics. This approach was rejected because it would not address the causal question of whether ventilatory burden *independently* predicts cardiovascular instability.

2. **Machine learning without interpretability**: While ML could capture non-linear relationships, the primary scientific question is about the independent contribution of ventilatory burden, which requires interpretable statistical modeling (Cox regression). ML was considered as a secondary analysis to identify additional patterns but is not the primary method.

3. **Randomized controlled trial**: Would establish causality but is not feasible for this research question. Observational analysis with careful adjustment for confounding is the appropriate approach.

The selected approach (Cox regression with hospital-level adjustment) is scientifically justified because it directly tests the hypothesis while accounting for the key confounders (baseline severity, hospital characteristics) identified in the literature [K1].
