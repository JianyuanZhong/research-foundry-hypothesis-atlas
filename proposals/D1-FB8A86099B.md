# Provisional hypothesis: laboratory-monitoring reduction during persistent ICU instability

Status: provisional and untested. This is a design draft, not a result.

## Proposed relationship

Among adults with a continuous ICU stay who remain physiologically unstable at a prespecified index, a reduction in laboratory-testing intensity during the preceding 12 hours is associated with higher risk of a time-local deterioration in the following 12 hours, beyond contemporaneously measured severity and recent support. The association is expected to attenuate when end-of-life documentation, discharge/transfer context, and recent support de-escalation are handled explicitly. A residual association in held-out hospitals would be a monitoring-process signal, not evidence that changing laboratory orders causes deterioration.

## Evidence-supported versus unresolved claims

The configured eICU catalog and table metadata support that the dataset contains longitudinal, timestamped laboratory, vital, respiratory, infusion, patient, and hospital records, with patientunitstayid as the within-dataset join key and hospitalid for hospital-level stratification. The expert seed [starting question] supplies the opening that abrupt reductions in testing while unstable may predict deterioration and vary across hospitals. Neither establishes that association. The unresolved claim is whether a dynamic testing reduction, conditional on current instability, predicts near-term deterioration beyond current state.

The strongest rival is a care-process explanation: clinicians may reduce testing because they judge recovery, impending discharge/transfer, or end-of-life limitation; interface or documentation interruptions may create apparent gaps; and testing frequency may proxy unmeasured severity. The experiment must distinguish these explanations rather than treating adjustment as proof of mechanism.

## Population and temporal design

Use adult eICU stays with a valid unitdischargeoffset greater than 18 hours and a single continuous unit interval. Index at hourly times from 6 through unitdischargeoffset minus 12 hours. Require at least one valid vital observation and one laboratory observation in the preceding 24 hours, and label missingness separately from zero testing. Restrict the primary analysis to continuous stays; treat death, discharge, and transfer before the outcome window as competing events rather than silently coding them as non-deterioration.

At index t, define testing intensity from lab.csv.gz using patientunitstayid, labresultoffset, and labname: count distinct analytes and distinct laboratory episodes in (t-12,t], without treating a revised result as a new test unless its result time is distinct. Compare (t-24,t-12] with (t-12,t]. The primary reduction is a positive prior count followed by zero current distinct analytes, or at least a 50% fall when the prior interval has at least two episodes; continuous change and episode count are secondary.

The primary outcome is the first qualifying deterioration in (t,t+12]: new or escalated respiratory support from respiratoryCare.csv.gz (respcarestatusoffset, airwaytype, ventstartoffset, ventendoffset, highironoxlimit) or new/escalated vasopressor/infusion from infusionDrug.csv.gz (infusionoffset, drugname, drugrate, infusionrate, patientweight). Do not use post-index laboratory values as the outcome. Death before the event and discharge/transfer before the event are competing events; mortality fields in apachePatientResult.csv.gz are secondary.

Adjust only for information available at or before t: vitalPeriodic.csv.gz (observationoffset, temperature, SaO2, heart rate, respiration, systemic pressures), respiratory state from respiratoryCharting.csv.gz (respchartoffset, respchartentryoffset, type/value labels), recent infusion/support, age, sex, unit type, and hospital. Use carePlanEOL.csv.gz (cpleolsaveoffset, cpleoldiscussionoffset, activeupondischarge) to define prespecified EOL-context strata and a sensitivity analysis excluding documented EOL plans before t. Missing EOL documentation is not equivalent to no EOL care.

## Baseline, substantive alternative, and falsification

The simple baseline is a prespecified discrete-time logistic model with the same index times, competing-event censoring, hospital fixed/clustered effects, and clustered uncertainty. The substantive alternative is a longitudinal recurrent-event/discrete-time hazard model with time-varying testing intensity and physiologic trajectories. It can reveal whether the signal is transient, persistent, or preceded by a trajectory; it is not justified merely for a small predictive gain.

Use hospital-held-out validation, calibration, discrimination, absolute risk difference, and uncertainty intervals. Negative controls are: (1) testing intervals permuted within hospital while preserving stay length and outcome; (2) a post-index testing exposure, which should not predict the prior deterioration; and (3) a documentation-timestamp control from non-laboratory charting where available. A result that disappears with EOL/discharge adjustment or negative controls supports a documentation/selection explanation.

Supportive results require a reproducible held-out-hospital association after state and EOL/discharge handling, with null negative controls. Adverse results are null, unstable, or attenuated associations after explicit rival adjustment. Inconclusive results include wide intervals, sparse events, or inability to distinguish interface interruption from clinical intent. None establishes causality or a recommendation to increase testing.

## Method comparison and actual deliverable

The baseline loses temporal ordering and persistence because it treats each landmark as a static association. The longitudinal alternative uses the same available inputs and can estimate whether a testing reduction is a transient landmark artifact or part of a trajectory, with time-varying physiologic state and competing events. The actual scientific deliverable is a fitted comparison of these two prespecified models, held-out-hospital performance, uncertainty intervals, and negative-control results—not a claim that a more complex model is clinically superior.

The three demonstration papers are not being reproduced here. The natural-history/longitudinal demonstration motivates a time-local, competing-event design; the cancer and Bayesian demonstrations are deferred because this eICU question does not require their cohort or model-specific machinery. The expert seed is the substantive opening, not a validated result.
