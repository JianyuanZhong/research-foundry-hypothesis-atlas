# Scientific Proposal: Discordant Recovery Phenotype in Decompensated Cirrhosis

## Unresolved Question

**Clinical Problem:** In patients with decompensated cirrhosis undergoing liver transplantation evaluation, current risk stratification relies heavily on single-time-point MELD scores and static laboratory values. However, patients with the same baseline MELD score exhibit vastly different trajectories of clinical deterioration and transplant waiting list progression.

**Key Uncertainty:** Does the *rate of change* in organ function (particularly coagulation and synthetic function) over the first 30 days post-diagnosis predict which patients will require urgent transplant versus those who can wait longer? Existing models treat "abnormal labs" as a binary state rather than capturing the dynamic trajectory of recovery or deterioration.

## Clinical Importance

1. **Transplant Allocation:** More accurate risk stratification could optimize liver transplant allocation, reducing waitlist mortality while ensuring appropriate urgency.

2. **Prognostic Guidance:** Clinicians could better counsel patients about their likely course, improving shared decision-making about transplant timing.

3. **Resource Utilization:** Identifying patients at imminent risk of decompensation could trigger earlier interventions before catastrophic events occur.

4. **Beyond MELD:** MELD score has known limitations, particularly in patients with coagulopathy, hepatorenal syndrome, or severe encephalopathy where synthetic function is already severely impaired.

## Substantive Advance Over Existing Knowledge

Current research on cirrhosis prognosis typically:
- Uses single-time-point or averaged laboratory values
- Relies on established scores (MELD, Child-Pugh) without modification
- Treats laboratory abnormalities as static rather than dynamic

**This proposal advances by:**
1. Modeling the *trajectory* of synthetic function (INR, albumin, bilirubin) rather than just absolute values
2. Capturing discordance between different organ function parameters (e.g., improving bilirubin but deteriorating INR)
3. Integrating medication patterns (diuretics, lactulose, vasoactive drugs) as proxies for clinical management intensity
4. Accounting for hospital-level variation in care patterns

## Hypothesis

**Primary Hypothesis:** The rate of change in synthetic function parameters (INR, albumin, bilirubin) over the first 30 days post-diagnosis of cirrhosis, when modeled as a dynamic trajectory rather than static values, independently predicts time to decompensation and mortality above and beyond baseline MELD score.

**Secondary Hypothesis:** Patients exhibiting discordant trajectories (e.g., improving one parameter while deteriorating another) have different risk profiles than those with concordant trajectories, suggesting that multi-parameter trajectory analysis captures heterogeneity that single-parameter models miss.

## Population and Temporal Boundaries

- **Population:** Adults with diagnosis of cirrhosis in the HCC cohort
- **Temporal Window:** Index date (cirrhosis diagnosis) to 90 days follow-up
- **Inclusion:** Patients with ≥3 laboratory measurements within 30 days of index date
- **Exclusion:** Patients with missing key labs (INR, albumin, bilirubin), active malignancy (except HCC), or transplant already performed

### Key Variables

- **Primary outcome:** Time to first decompensation event (ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome) or death
- **Predictors:**
  - Baseline MELD score (calculated from INR, bilirubin, creatinine)
  - Rate of change in INR, albumin, bilirubin over 30 days
  - Discordance metric: variance in trajectory slopes across parameters
  - Medication intensity score (number of days on vasoactive agents)
- **Covariates:** Age, sex, comorbidities (from diagnoses), hospital characteristics

## Methodology

### Baseline Approach
**Logistic Regression / Cox Proportional Hazards Model:**
- Outcome: Decompensation within 90 days (binary) or time to decompensation
- Predictors: Baseline MELD score, age, sex, comorbidity count
- Rationale: Standard clinical approach that current practice uses

### Substantive Alternative
**Dynamic Trajectory Modeling:**
- **Method:** Latent growth curve modeling or piecewise linear regression to model parameter trajectories
- **Alternative 1 (Mechanistic):** Piecewise regression with breakpoints at day 14 and 30 to capture different recovery/deterioration phases
- **Alternative 2 (Learned):** Simple sequence model (e.g., LSTM or Transformer) trained on 30-day lab sequences to predict decompensation
- **Alternative 3 (Hybrid):** Random forest with engineered features from trajectory slopes and discordance metrics

### Why the Alternative Adds Scientific Information

The baseline approach treats labs as static snapshots, missing:
1. **Recovery trajectories:** Patients with improving labs despite high baseline scores may have lower risk
2. **Deterioration acceleration:** Patients whose labs worsen rapidly may need urgent intervention
3. **Discordance:** Different parameters may signal different pathophysiological processes (e.g., synthetic vs. cholestatic dysfunction)
4. **Non-linear dynamics:** Recovery may plateau or accelerate at different rates

The trajectory approach can distinguish these patterns that baseline models conflate.

## Analysis Plan

1. **Descriptive:**
   - Distribution of decompensation events by baseline characteristics
   - Typical lab trajectories in decompensated vs. stable patients
   - Medication patterns by outcome

2. **Univariate Analysis:**
   - Time-to-event analysis for each trajectory slope category
   - Hazard ratios for discordant vs. concordant trajectories

3. **Multivariate Modeling:**
   - Cox proportional hazards model with trajectory features
   - Compare AUC and C-index between baseline and trajectory models
   - Calibration plots

4. **Discordance Analysis:**
   - Define discordance as >50% divergence in trajectory slopes between parameters
   - Test if discordance independently predicts outcome after adjusting for concordant parameters

5. **External Validation:**
   - Split cohort into training (60%) and validation (40%)
   - Evaluate model performance on held-out set

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis would be **falsified** if:
1. Rate of change in labs does not add predictive value beyond baseline MELD (ΔC-index < 0.02)
2. Discordant trajectories show no independent association with outcome after adjustment
3. The trajectory model performs worse than or equal to the baseline model on validation set
4. Hospital-level variation accounts for all apparent trajectory-outcome relationships (suggesting confounding by care intensity)

## Supportive Results

Supportive evidence would show:
1. Trajectory slopes significantly improve prediction beyond baseline MELD (ΔC-index ≥ 0.05)
2. Discordant trajectories have distinct risk profiles
3. Rapid deterioration in any parameter predicts worse outcomes
4. The model generalizes to validation cohort with similar performance

## Adverse/Inconclusive Results

**Adverse:**
- Trajectory features introduce bias (e.g., patients with frequent labs have different outcomes)
- Discordance metric captures care-seeking behavior rather than true pathophysiology
- No clear clinical actionability identified

**Inconclusive:**
- Sample size insufficient for precise trajectory estimation
- Hospital-level confounding cannot be fully adjusted
- Trajectory patterns differ by hospital but no unified model emerges

## Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------------|
| Missing lab values | Use multiple imputation; restrict to patients with sufficient data |
| Hospital-level confounding | Include hospital as random effect or fixed effect |
| Selection bias (frequent lab testing) | Model testing frequency as covariate or use inverse probability weighting |
| Temporal leakage | Ensure all features are computed only from pre-outcome data |
| Small sample size | Consider pooling with other cirrhosis cohorts if HCC insufficient |
