# UKB 40 adaptation: glycemic worsening with weight loss as an early pancreatic-cancer signal

## Scientific opening

The UKB 40 seed asks whether similar HbA1c worsening accompanied by weight loss predicts later pancreatic cancer more strongly than weight gain. This is clinically important because pancreatic cancer is often diagnosed late, and a reproducible pre-diagnostic metabolic pattern could justify earlier imaging or specialist review. However, the seed's claim is not yet identifiable: weight loss may reflect intentional dieting, diabetes treatment, another malignancy, or early pancreatic cancer itself. The unresolved question is whether the excess risk is concentrated in a prespecified short window and persists after accounting for medication and pre-existing cancer, rather than merely describing people who were already ill.

## Hypothesis and strongest rival

**Hypothesis:** Among UKB participants with valid HbA1c and weight at two assessment visits and no prior pancreatic cancer, a prespecified increase in HbA1c accompanied by weight loss (rather than weight gain) is associated with a higher hazard of pancreatic cancer diagnosed within 2 years after the second visit, compared with the same HbA1c change accompanied by weight gain. The association should be stronger in the short window than in a later window and should not be explained by baseline pancreatic cancer or by common diabetes treatments.

**Strongest rival:** The apparent signal is reverse causation or treatment confounding. A person with undiagnosed pancreatic cancer may lose weight and have worsening glucose before diagnosis; diabetes medications, dieting, smoking, alcohol use, or other cancers may also produce weight loss and alter HbA1c. If the association disappears after excluding early diagnoses, accounting for treatment, or appears similarly for all cancers, the hypothesis is not supported as a pancreatic-specific early-warning pattern.

## Population and temporal design

- Source: UKB completed subset, `ukb672073` split tables and Parquet convenience tables listed in `datasets/population_multiomics/README.md`.
- Population: participants with HbA1c (`30750`) and weight (`21002`) measured at two assessment visits, with assessment dates (`53`), and no pancreatic cancer diagnosis before the second visit.
- Baseline: second valid joint visit. Exclude participants with a pancreatic cancer diagnosis before this visit. Define the follow-up origin as the second assessment date.
- Exposure: change in HbA1c from visit 1 to visit 2 and change in weight. Use clinically interpretable categories and continuous sensitivity analyses; prespecify the weight-loss versus weight-gain comparison within similar HbA1c change strata.
- Outcome: pancreatic cancer diagnosis using cancer diagnosis date (`40005`) and cancer type (`40006`), with ICD-10 diagnosis fields (`41270`, `41280`) as sensitivity checks. Primary window: 0–2 years after the second visit. Secondary: 2–5 years.
- Treatment context: medication codes (`20003`) for diabetes treatment and other relevant medications, with timing relative to the second visit where available.

## Baseline and learned alternative

**Baseline:** a prespecified Cox model with age, sex, assessment interval, HbA1c level and change, weight change, diabetes treatment, smoking, and prior cancer history, plus a weight-change-by-HbA1c-change interaction. This tests the core association and temporal pattern.

**Learned alternative:** a time-to-event model with a small neural network or gradient-boosted survival model trained on the same covariates, using the second visit as origin and 2-year pancreatic cancer as the primary outcome. It should be evaluated against the Cox baseline using time-dependent discrimination and calibration, with internal cross-validation and no leakage from post-baseline data. The learned model can reveal nonlinear interactions and treatment-modified patterns that a linear interaction term misses, but it cannot establish causality. If it adds little beyond the prespecified interaction, the simpler model is preferred.

## Analysis and falsification

- Primary estimand: hazard ratio for weight loss versus weight gain within similar HbA1c-change strata, with 95% confidence intervals.
- Temporal test: compare the 0–2-year hazard ratio with the 2–5-year hazard ratio. A pancreatic-specific early-warning pattern requires a stronger short-window association.
- Competing explanations: exclude diagnoses within a prespecified early grace period in sensitivity analysis; adjust for and stratify by diabetes treatment; compare with all-cancer and non-pancreatic-cancer outcomes; repeat in participants without prior cancer and with stable assessment intervals.
- Supportive result: a positive, statistically robust short-window association that attenuates or disappears in later follow-up and is not replicated for all cancers.
- Adverse result: no association, a stronger later association, or a similar association for all cancers, which would argue against a pancreatic-specific early-warning interpretation.
- Inconclusive result: too few pancreatic cancer events, excessive missingness, or unstable estimates after treatment adjustment; in that case, the design should be parked rather than expanded to a composite cancer endpoint.

## Key evidence and advance

[K1] Yuan et al. (2020) studied diabetes duration and recent weight change in US cohorts and found that recent-onset diabetes is associated with higher subsequent pancreatic cancer risk, but the study did not test the specific interaction between HbA1c change and weight-change direction in a UKB repeated-measurement design [K1]. Our proposal adds a time-ordered UKB test that separates short-window from later risk and explicitly accounts for treatment and reverse causation.

[K2] Li et al. (2026) systematically reviewed pancreatic cancer risk prediction models in new-onset diabetes and reported moderate pooled discrimination with substantial heterogeneity, while identifying unintentional weight loss and rapid glycemic deterioration as recurrent warning signals [K2]. This supports the clinical relevance of the variables, but it does not establish that the combined pattern is pancreatic-specific in UKB or that it survives treatment adjustment.

[K3] Amadou et al. (2026) used UK Biobank anthropometric and metabolic biomarker data to show that glucose and HbA1c mediate a substantial proportion of the obesity-pancreatic cancer association [K3]. This bounds the interpretation: glycemic measures are relevant to pancreatic cancer risk, but their study was not designed to test a pre-diagnostic weight-loss warning signal or to separate early from later diagnoses.

## Data limitations and next steps

The available data can support an association and temporal pattern, but cannot establish that weight loss causes pancreatic cancer or that the pattern is a validated screening rule. Clinical adjudication, stage at diagnosis, treatment details, and external validation would be needed for stronger claims. The first bounded check is to verify the number of participants with two valid HbA1c/weight measurements, the distribution of assessment intervals, and the number of pancreatic cancer events in the 0–2-year window.

## Demonstration-paper dispositions

- Natural-history transformer paper: not pursued for this question; its longitudinal modeling is conceptually relevant but its full cohort and methods are not needed for a bounded UKB survival analysis.
- Cancer longitudinal routine-data paper: relevant ambition, but the main article and STAR Methods are unavailable; only the supplement was inspected as a limitation. It motivates temporal leakage control, not a reproduction.
- Bayesian longitudinal EHR/genetic paper: not pursued here; its genetic/EHR framework is not required for this biomarker-focused question and would add complexity without resolving the main reverse-cause concern.
