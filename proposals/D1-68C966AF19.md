# Nighttime ICU Discharge and Residual Physiological Instability

## A Falsifiable Hypothesis for eICU-CRD

### 1. Hypothesis

The excess risk of nighttime (00:00–05:59) ICU discharge is concentrated among patients with residual physiological instability (abnormal vitals or labs in the 6 hours before discharge), compared to patients with stable physiology. The interaction between discharge time band and pre-discharge instability is the key modifier of post-discharge mortality risk.

### 2. Scientific Opening

**Observation**: eICU-CRD data show a striking bimodal distribution of ICU discharge times: ~22% at 00:00–06:00, ~5% at 07:00–18:00, ~40% at 19:00–23:59 (verified: 189,918 alive discharges with unitdischargetime24) [K3]. This is not a uniform distribution — nighttime discharges are substantially overrepresented relative to daytime.

**Unresolved question**: Is the excess risk of nighttime discharge (if it exists) driven by (a) the time of day itself (reduced staffing, monitoring gaps, organizational factors), or (b) the fact that patients who are still physiologically unstable are preferentially discharged at night?

**Leading claim**: Nighttime discharge carries excess risk primarily among patients with residual instability. The time-of-day effect is a proxy for patient selection: unstable patients are discharged at night when monitoring is reduced, and this combination (unstable + reduced monitoring) drives the risk.

**Strongest rival**: The nighttime discharge risk is uniform across stability groups — it is purely an organizational/staffing phenomenon that affects all patients equally, regardless of their physiological state. In this case, the interaction would be null and the main effect of nighttime discharge would be uniform.

**Discriminating observation**: If the leading claim is correct, the interaction term (discharge_time_band × residual_instability) will be significant, with nighttime risk elevated only in the unstable group. If the rival is correct, the main effect of nighttime discharge will be significant but the interaction will be null.

**Evidence for the opening**:
- Shift-specific staffing affects outcomes differently by shift [K1]: day-shift understaffing was associated with higher mortality and readmission, while evening/night understaffing was not. This suggests that the time of day matters, but the mechanism is not simply "fewer staff at night."
- Discharge timing depends on local organization rather than physiology [K2]: discharge models do not transfer across hospitals, suggesting that discharge timing is an organizational decision, not purely a physiological one. This motivates the question of WHICH patients are preferentially discharged at night.
- The eICU-CRD provides the necessary data: discharge timestamps, pre-discharge vitals, and multi-center structure [K3].

### 3. Clinical Importance

- ICU discharge timing is a modifiable organizational factor. If nighttime discharge is particularly dangerous for unstable patients, hospitals could implement targeted monitoring or delay discharge for this subgroup.
- The finding would inform discharge protocols: should unstable patients be held until morning? Should nighttime discharges trigger enhanced ward monitoring?
- The bimodal discharge pattern suggests that discharge timing is not random but reflects organizational practices. Understanding the patient-level factors that drive nighttime discharge could identify a modifiable risk factor.

### 4. Substantive Advance over Existing Knowledge

- **Morioka et al. 2026** [K1] examined nurse understaffing by shift (day vs evening/night) and found day-shift understaffing associated with mortality/readmission, but evening/night understaffing was NOT associated with mortality/readmission (only LOS). This is a different exposure (staffing level, not discharge timing) and does not address the interaction with patient stability.
- **Pardo et al. 2026** [K2] showed that discharge timing depends on local organization rather than physiology, bounding the interpretation: discharge timing is not purely a physiological decision. However, they did not examine the interaction between discharge timing and residual instability.
- **No prior study** has examined the interaction between ICU discharge time band and pre-discharge physiological instability in a large multi-center dataset. This is the specific gap this study addresses.

### 5. Population

### 6. Exposure: Discharge Time Band

Derived from `unitdischargetime24` (format HH:MM:SS) [K3]:
- **Nighttime**: hour 0–5 (00:00–05:59)
- **Daytime**: hour 6–17 (06:00–17:59) — reference
- **Evening**: hour 18–23 (18:00–23:59)

### 7. Residual Instability (6h Pre-Discharge Window)

**Window**: observationoffset in [unitdischargeoffset - 360, unitdischargeoffset] (minutes)

**From vitalPeriodic.csv.gz** [K3]:
- mean_sbp: mean of systemicsystolic in window (unstable if <90 or >140)
- mean_hr: mean of heartrate in window (unstable if >100 or <60)
- mean_spo2: mean of sao2 in window (unstable if <92)
- n_vitals: count of vital measurements in window (require ≥1)

**From lab.csv.gz** [K3]:
- latest_creatinine: most recent creatinine in window (unstable if >1.5 mg/dL)
- latest_bun: most recent BUN in window (unstable if >40 mg/dL)

**Composite**: residual_instability = 1 if ANY of the above is abnormal, 0 otherwise.

### 8. Outcomes

- **Primary**: In-hospital mortality
  - `hospitaldischargestatus` in ('Death', 'Expired') → 1, else 0
- **Secondary**:
  - ICU readmission: same `patienthealthsystemstayid`, subsequent stay with `unitadmitsource` containing 'readmit' or 'transfer'
  - Post-ICU hospital LOS: `hospitaldischargeoffset - unitdischargeoffset` (minutes)

### 9. Analysis Plan

**Primary analysis**: Multilevel logistic regression (hospital random intercept)
```
mortality ~ discharge_time_band * residual_instability + age + gender + ICU_los + (1 | hospitalid)
```
- Key test: interaction term `discharge_time_band:residual_instability` (Wald test, p < 0.05)
- Report: adjusted odds ratios with 95% CIs for each discharge time band within each stability group

**Secondary analyses**:
- Cox proportional hazards for time-to-death (stratified by hospital)
- Linear mixed model for post-ICU LOS
- Stratified analysis by ICU stay duration (>48h)

**Sensitivity analyses**:
- Exclude patients with carePlanEOL records (end-of-life care)
- Exclude patients with <1 vital measurement in 6h window
- Restrict to ICU stay >48h
- Use 12h window instead of 6h

**Method comparison**:
- **Baseline**: Stratified comparison (nighttime vs daytime mortality by stability group, chi-square test, Fisher's exact for small cells)
- **Learned alternative**: XGBoost model predicting in-hospital mortality with features: discharge_time_band, residual_instability, mean_sbp, mean_hr, mean_spo2, latest_creatinine, latest_bun, age, gender, ICU_los. Compare AUROC with vs without discharge_time_band to assess incremental predictive information.
- **Justification**: The interaction test answers the mechanistic question (WHY is nighttime risky?); the ML model answers the predictive question (CAN we identify high-risk nighttime discharges?). They are complementary, not competing.

### 10. Falsification Criteria

- **Supportive**: Significant interaction (p < 0.05) with nighttime discharge having higher mortality than daytime ONLY among unstable patients (OR > 1.2 for nighttime in unstable, OR ≈ 1 for nighttime in stable)
- **Adverse**: No significant interaction (p > 0.10) — nighttime risk is uniform across stability groups, OR nighttime discharge is associated with LOWER mortality (OR < 0.8)
- **Inconclusive**: >50% of patients have no vital measurements in the 6h window (insufficient data to define residual instability)

### 13. Limitations

1. **No bed-capacity or staffing data**: Cannot directly test the organizational mechanism (reduced nighttime staffing). The interaction test is an association, not a causal test.
2. **Residual instability is a composite**: The specific thresholds (SBP <90, HR >100, SpO2 <92%) are prespecified but arbitrary. Sensitivity analyses with different thresholds are needed.
3. **6h window is prespecified**: The choice of 6h is based on clinical reasoning (time from last assessment to discharge) but is not validated. 12h window sensitivity analysis is planned.
4. **eICU-CRD is a single time period (2014-2015)**: Findings may not generalize to current practice.
5. **No post-ICU vital signs**: Cannot directly measure deterioration after discharge. The outcome is in-hospital mortality, which is a proxy for post-discharge deterioration.

### 14. What Would Change the Next Decision

- **If supportive**: Justifies a prospective study of enhanced monitoring for unstable patients discharged at night. Could inform ICU discharge protocols.
- **If adverse (no interaction)**: Suggests the nighttime risk (if any) is organizational, not patient-specific. Focus should shift to staffing/monitoring interventions rather than patient selection.
- **If inconclusive**: Need to expand the vital data window or use alternative measures of residual instability (e.g., vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation status).
