# Scientific Proposal: Post-Infection Proteomic Recovery Trajectories Predict Long-Term Organ Outcomes

## Unresolved Question

**Primary Question**: Do distinct temporal proteomic recovery trajectories following hospitalization for infection predict long-term cardiovascular and renal outcomes, and do these trajectories differ based on the organ system most severely affected during the acute infection?

**Secondary Question**: Can machine learning models identify proteomic recovery "phenotypes" that stratify patients by risk for long-term organ dysfunction, outperforming traditional clinical risk scores?

## Clinical Importance

### 1. Early Identification of High-Risk Patients
- Current post-infection care relies heavily on clinical scores (SOFA, APACHE) that may not capture individual recovery trajectories
- Identifying patients with aberrant proteomic recovery could enable:
  - Early intervention strategies to prevent long-term organ damage
  - Targeted monitoring and follow-up
  - Personalized rehabilitation programs

### 2. Understanding Recovery Mechanisms
- Distinct proteomic recovery trajectories may reflect different biological pathways:
  - **Pro-inflammatory phenotype**: Persistent elevation of acute-phase reactants, cytokines
  - **Endothelial dysfunction phenotype**: Markers of vascular injury and repair
  - **Immunomodulatory failure phenotype**: Dysregulation of immune cell populations

### 3. Bridging Acute and Chronic Care
- Current systems treat acute infection management and long-term chronic disease prevention as separate silos
- This study bridges that gap by identifying recovery patterns that predict future organ outcomes

## Hypothesis

**Primary Hypothesis**: Among patients hospitalized for infection, distinct temporal proteomic recovery trajectories (patterns of change in protein levels over time following discharge) predict long-term cardiovascular and renal outcomes, independent of baseline characteristics and acute severity scores.

**Specific Predictions**:
1. Patients with "aberrant recovery trajectories" (e.g., persistent inflammation, delayed endothelial repair markers) will have higher rates of long-term cardiovascular and renal events compared to those with "normal recovery trajectories"
2. The relationship between recovery trajectory and long-term outcomes differs by the organ system most severely affected during the acute infection (e.g., cardiac vs. pulmonary vs. other)
3. Machine learning models using proteomic recovery trajectories will outperform traditional clinical risk scores (e.g., SOFA, APACHE II, eGFR-based models) in predicting long-term organ outcomes

## Study Population

- **Population**: UK Biobank participants who were hospitalized for infection (ICD-10 codes for infectious diseases)
- **Inclusion criteria**:
  - No prior hospitalization for infection before the index infection hospitalization
  - Age 40-70 years at index hospitalization
  - Complete proteomic data collected before and after the index hospitalization
- **Exclusion criteria**:
  - Pre-existing cardiovascular or renal disease before index hospitalization
  - Multiple infection hospitalizations within 6 months of each other
  - Missing proteomic or outcome data

## Suggested Approach

### Phase 1: Define Recovery Trajectories
1. Identify proteomic measurements that vary over time following infection
2. Define "recovery trajectory" as the pattern of change in key proteins from pre-illness to post-illness timepoints
3. Cluster patients into distinct recovery trajectory groups using unsupervised methods (e.g., k-means, hierarchical clustering, or trajectory modeling)

### Phase 2: Association with Long-term Outcomes
1. For each recovery trajectory group, compare rates of long-term cardiovascular and renal outcomes
2. Use Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios for outcomes by trajectory group
3. Adjust for baseline characteristics, acute severity scores (SOFA, APACHE), and comorbidities

### Phase 3: Machine Learning Approaches
1. Develop supervised machine learning models (e.g., random forests, gradient boosting, or neural networks) using proteomic recovery trajectories as features
2. Predict long-term organ outcomes and evaluate performance using AUC, calibration, and decision-curve analysis
3. Compare against traditional clinical risk scores as baselines

### Phase 4: Organ-Specific Analysis
1. Stratify analysis by the organ system most severely affected during the acute infection
2. Test whether recovery trajectories have different associations with outcomes in different organ systems
3. Explore potential mechanisms (e.g., does cardiac injury during infection lead to different proteomic recovery patterns than pulmonary injury?)

## Scientific Significance

### 1. Novel Biological Insight
- This study moves beyond static baseline proteomics to examine dynamic recovery patterns
- Recovery trajectories may capture biological processes (e.g., endothelial repair, immune resolution) that are not reflected in baseline measurements
- May reveal novel pathways involved in post-infection organ vulnerability

### 2. Clinical Utility
- Recovery trajectory-based risk stratification could identify high-risk patients for targeted interventions
- May inform development of biomarker panels for post-infection monitoring
- Could lead to personalized rehabilitation strategies

### 3. Methodological Advancement
- Integrates proteomic dynamics with longitudinal clinical outcomes
- Demonstrates utility of machine learning for trajectory-based risk prediction
- Provides framework for studying other dynamic biomarker processes in clinical settings

## Main Limitations

### Data Limitations
- UK Biobank proteomic measurements may not capture all relevant proteins or timepoints
- Limited post-illness proteomic follow-up (may not have multiple timepoints for trajectory analysis)
- Potential for misclassification of infection types or severity

### Analytical Challenges
- Trajectory clustering may be sensitive to preprocessing and parameter choices
- Confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., genetic predisposition, environmental exposures)
- Reverse causation (long-term disease affecting recovery pattern)

### Generalizability
- UK Biobank participants may not represent all populations (e.g., younger, sicker individuals)
- Long-term follow-up in UK Biobank may be limited for some outcomes

## Falsification Criteria

The hypothesis will be falsified if:
1. No distinct proteomic recovery trajectory groups are identified (all patients follow similar recovery patterns)
2. Recovery trajectory groups show no association with long-term outcomes after adjustment for baseline characteristics and acute severity
3. Machine learning models using recovery trajectories perform no better than traditional clinical risk scores
4. Recovery trajectories show consistent associations regardless of the organ system affected during acute infection (suggesting a general effect rather than organ-specific mechanism)

## Substantive Alternative Approach

**Baseline Approach**: Traditional clinical risk scores (SOFA, APACHE II) based on clinical parameters (blood pressure, organ function, consciousness)

**Learned Alternative**: Machine learning models using proteomic recovery trajectories as features

**What the Alternative Reveals**:
- Clinical risk scores capture acute severity but may miss subtle biological processes
- Proteomic recovery trajectories may capture biological mechanisms of organ vulnerability that are not reflected in clinical parameters
- The alternative could reveal that biological recovery processes (captured by proteomics) are more predictive of long-term outcomes than acute clinical severity
